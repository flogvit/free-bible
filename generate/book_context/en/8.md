# Historical and Cultural Context for the Book of Ruth

## Historical Setting

The book of Ruth is set in the period of the judges, a turbulent era in Israel's history between Joshua's conquest of Canaan and the establishment of the monarchy (c. 1200–1020 B.C.). The author is unknown, although Jewish tradition attributes the book to the prophet Samuel. Most scholars believe it was written down significantly later than the events it describes, likely in the early monarchic period or after the exile.

The genealogy in chapter 4, which culminates with King David, suggests that the book was completed no earlier than during David's reign (c. 1000 B.C.). Some scholars argue for a post-exilic date (after 539 B.C.) based on linguistic features and the book's apparent polemic against strict ethnic exclusivism. Regardless of its dating, the book presents itself as a historical narrative from the time of the judges.

The period was characterized by decentralized political power, with tribes operating relatively independently. Famine, which drives the action in the opening chapter, was not uncommon during this unstable time. Moab, where Elimelech and Naomi fled, was a neighboring people east of the Dead Sea with a complex relationship to Israel—sometimes hostile, sometimes peaceful.

## Literary Context

The book of Ruth is a short story (novella) characterized by elegant Hebrew prose, vivid dialogue, and subtle character development. Genrically, it combines elements of idyll, family chronicle, and legal narrative. Its structure follows a classic narrative arc from crisis (loss and famine) to resolution (marriage and heir), with Naomi as the framing character and Ruth as the protagonist.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), Ruth is placed among the "Writings" (Ketuvim), specifically within the Five Megillot (Festival Scrolls), where it is read during Shavuot (the Feast of Weeks/Pentecost). In Christian Bibles, Ruth follows Judges, a chronological-thematic placement that creates a bridge between the period of the judges and the monarchy through David's lineage.

## Cultural Background

The social structure in the time of the judges was patriarchal and clan-based. Women's security and status were tied to men—first their father, then their husband, and finally their sons. A widow without sons, like Naomi and Ruth, found herself in an extremely vulnerable position. Gleaning grain after the harvesters was a legally established right for the poor and foreigners (Lev 19:9–10), which enabled Ruth to provide for her mother-in-law.

The kinsman-redeemer institution (go'el) was central to Israelite society. A close relative had the duty to "redeem" family property and, if necessary, marry a childless widow to continue the name and inheritance of the deceased (levirate marriage, cf. Deut 25:5–10). The custom of removing a sandal (chap. 4) publicly confirmed legal transactions at the city gate, which served as the courtroom.

Ruth's Moabite background is theologically significant. Moabites were traditionally viewed negatively in Israelite tradition (cf. Deut 23:3–6), making her inclusion in David's lineage remarkable. The book illustrates the tension between ethnic particularism and openness to foreigners who show loyalty to Israel's God.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 B.C.)**
Found in Thebes, Egypt. This is the earliest known Egyptian reference to "Israel" as a people in Canaan, confirming Israelite presence in the region during the time of the judges.

**Tel Dan Inscription (c. 840 B.C.)**
Found at Tel Dan in northern Israel. Contains the first extra-biblical reference to the "house of David," confirming the historical existence of the Davidic dynasty and the relevance of Ruth's genealogy.

**Moabite Stone/Mesha Stele (c. 840 B.C.)**
Found in Dibon, Jordan. Provides insight into Moabite culture, religion, and their relationship with Israel, confirming the geopolitical context between the two peoples.

**Gezer Agricultural Calendar (c. 950–900 B.C.)**
Found at Gezer, Israel. Describes the agricultural cycle including grain harvest, illuminating the farming context that frames Ruth's meeting with Boaz during the barley harvest.
