# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings

## Historical Framework

The book of 2 Kings continues the narrative from 1 Kings, covering the period from approximately 850 B.C. to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 B.C.—about 265 years of history. The book describes the final phase of the divided kingdom, with Israel (the northern kingdom) falling to Assyria in 722 B.C., and Judah (the southern kingdom) being conquered by Babylon in 586 B.C. Traditionally, the prophet Jeremiah has been credited as the author, but modern scholarship views the book as part of the Deuteronomistic history, edited during or shortly after the exile in Babylon.

The historical period described was characterized by great power politics. Assyria dominated the Middle East from the 900s until 612 B.C., followed by the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II. Both Israel and Judah found themselves squeezed between these powers and Egypt to the south. The book is written from the perspective of exiled Judeans seeking to understand why disaster struck them.

Religiously, the period was marked by tension between Yahweh worship and Canaanite religion, especially the Baal cult. Several kings promoted syncretistic worship, while prophets like Elisha and reforming kings such as Hezekiah and Josiah fought for faithfulness to the covenant with Yahweh. The Deuteronomistic perspective evaluates each king based on whether they "did what was right" or "what was evil in the Lord's eyes."

## Literary Context

The book of 2 Kings belongs to the historical literature of the Old Testament and is part of "the Former Prophets" in the Jewish canon (Nevi'im). Genrewise, the book combines royal chronicles, prophetic narratives, and theological historiography. The style alternates between short, formalized royal notices and longer dramatic narratives, particularly those connected to the prophet Elisha in the early chapters.

The book has a clear structure: Chapters 1–8 focus on Elisha's ministry; chapters 9–17 describe the parallel history of both kingdoms up to Israel's fall; and chapters 18–25 follow Judah's last century until the exile. Each king's reign follows a fixed pattern including accession, evaluation, actions, and death. This structure underscores the book's theological message: disobedience to the covenant leads to judgment.

## Cultural Background

Society in this period was hierarchically organized with the king at the top, followed by court officials, priests, prophets, landowners, craftsmen, and farmers. Cities were administrative and religious centers, while the majority lived in villages engaged in agriculture and livestock raising. The temple in Jerusalem was not only a religious center but also an economic institution with a treasury and commercial activities.

Religious practice included sacrifices at the temple, pilgrimages to shrines, and daily devotion in the home. Baal worship with fertility rituals competed with Yahweh worship. The high places (bamot) were local sacrificial sites that reforming kings sought to abolish. Prophets served as God's spokespersons and could both support and confront royal authority.

Relations with neighboring peoples were complex. Aram-Damascus was sometimes an enemy, sometimes an ally. The Phoenicians brought trade goods and cultural influence, but also the Baal cult. Moab, Edom, and Ammon were vassal states that rebelled when opportunities arose. Assyria and Babylon demanded tribute and deported rebellious populations—a practice that affected both Israel and Judah.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Sennacherib's Prism (ca. 689 B.C.)** – Found in Nineveh, now in the British Museum. This Assyrian king's annals describe the siege of Jerusalem under Hezekiah and confirm the biblical account in 2 Kings 18–19, although the Assyrian version naturally omits their defeat.

**The Tel Dan Stele (ca. 841 B.C.)** – Discovered at Tel Dan in 1993–1994. This Aramaic inscription mentions "the house of David" (byt dwd) and confirms the account in 2 Kings 8–9 regarding conflicts between Aram, Israel, and Judah.

**The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III (ca. 825 B.C.)** – Found in Nimrud, now in the British Museum. It shows King Jehu of Israel (or his envoy) bowing before the Assyrian king, confirming the mention of Jehu in 2 Kings 9–10.

**The Lachish Reliefs (ca. 701 B.C.)** – From Sennacherib's palace in Nineveh. Detailed depictions of the siege of Lachish mentioned in 2 Kings 18:14, showing contemporary warfare and city architecture.

**Babylonian Chronicles (ca. 605–594 B.C.)** – Found in Babylon. They confirm Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns against Judah and the first fall of Jerusalem in 597 B.C. (2 Kings 24), including the deportation of King Jehoiachin.
