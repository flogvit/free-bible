# Historical and Cultural Context for 3 John

## Historical Setting

The Third Epistle of John was likely written toward the end of the first century, probably between 90–100 AD. Traditionally attributed to the apostle John, who according to church tradition spent his final years in Ephesus, the author refers to himself as "the elder" (Greek: *presbyteros*). This title may refer to his age, his apostolic authority, or a leadership role within the congregation. Modern scholars debate whether the author is the apostle himself or another "John the Elder" connected to the Johannine community in Asia Minor.

The letter was written during a period when the first apostles had either passed away or were nearing the end of their lives. The early church faced challenges in preserving apostolic teaching and establishing local leadership. At the same time, traveling preachers and missionaries depended on hospitality from local believers, since public inns were often associated with immorality and idol worship.

Politically, this period fell under the reign of Emperor Domitian (81–96 AD), a time marked by increased pressure on Christians who refused to participate in the imperial cult. The congregations in Asia Minor experienced both internal division and external pressure, making questions of leadership and loyalty especially urgent.

## Literary Context

The Third Epistle of John is the shortest book in the New Testament and follows the format of a personal letter in Hellenistic style. It contains typical elements: sender, recipient (Gaius), greeting, body of the letter, and closing greetings. The language is warm and personal, with clear expressions of love for the recipient and concern for the situation within the congregation.

The letter belongs together with 1 John, 2 John, and the Gospel of John in what is called Johannine literature. While 1 John is more theological and 2 John warns against false teaching, 3 John focuses on practical matters concerning hospitality and church leadership. The letter is structured around three individuals: Gaius (who is praised), Diotrephes (who is criticized), and Demetrius (who is commended).

## Cultural Background

In the Greco-Roman world, hospitality (*philoxenia*) was a fundamental social virtue. Travelers depended on private homes for lodging, and receiving guests was associated with honor. For Christians, this custom took on a theological dimension: welcoming preachers meant participating in the spread of the gospel. The letter reflects this practice and the tension that could arise when different leaders held differing views about who deserved support.

Congregations during this period had not yet developed fixed hierarchical structures. Authority could rest upon apostolic connection, prophetic gift, or local position. Diotrephes' behavior in the letter illustrates conflicts that arose when local leaders asserted independence from apostolic authority. His desire to "be first" reveals the struggle for power within early congregations.

The letter's mention of "the Gentiles" (or "those who do not believe") shows that missionaries did not accept support from non-Christians. This reflects a consciousness about preserving the integrity of the gospel and avoiding compromise with pagan religion. Congregations functioned as an alternative community with its own values and support systems.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Excavations at Ephesus (ongoing since 1863)**
Located in western Turkey, these excavations have uncovered the ancient city where John is traditionally believed to have ministered. The findings include private homes that show how hospitality could be practiced, as well as inscriptions concerning religious and social life in the region.

**Didache (ca. 50–120 AD)**
This early Christian writing, rediscovered in Istanbul in 1873, provides rules for how congregations should treat traveling prophets and teachers. It confirms that issues surrounding hospitality and false preachers were widespread in the early church.

**Papyrus Bodmer VII-VIII (ca. 300 AD)**
Found in Egypt, this manuscript contains both 2 John and 3 John. It is one of the oldest surviving copies and confirms the early circulation and acceptance of these letters within the church.

**First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians (ca. 96 AD)**
Written from Rome to Corinth, this letter shows similar conflicts regarding church leadership during the same period. It confirms that questions of authority were a recurring theme in the early church across different geographical regions.
