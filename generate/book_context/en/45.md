# Romans - Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

The letter to the Romans was written by the apostle Paul around 56–58 AD, likely from Corinth during his third missionary journey. This makes it one of the earliest Christian documents we have, written only about 25 years after Jesus' crucifixion. Paul dictated the letter to a scribe named Tertius (16:22), and Phoebe from the church in Cenchreae was probably its carrier (16:1–2). Unlike many of Paul's other letters, he wrote to a congregation he had neither founded nor visited.

The letter was written during a period when the Christian movement was still young and largely perceived as a Jewish sect. The church in Rome was likely established by Jewish Christians who had been in Jerusalem on Pentecost (Acts 2:10). Emperor Claudius had expelled the Jews from Rome around 49 AD, which also affected Jewish Christians such as Priscilla and Aquila (Acts 18:2). When they returned after Claudius' death in 54 AD, they encountered a congregation now dominated by Gentile Christians, creating tensions between the groups.

Rome was the capital of the empire with a population of over one million people—the largest city of antiquity. The Christian community formed a small minority, probably numbering only a few hundred, who met in private homes scattered throughout the city. Politically, this was a relatively stable period under Emperor Nero (54–68 AD), before the persecutions that followed the great fire in 64 AD. Religiously, Rome was a melting pot of cults from across the empire, with emperor worship serving as a unifying element.

## Literary Context

Romans belongs to the genre "letter" (Greek: *epistolē*), which was an established literary form in antiquity. The letter follows the structure of Greco-Roman letters with an opening, main body, and conclusion, but it is unusually long and theologically systematic. Paul uses the diatribe style, a rhetorical technique where he poses questions from an imaginary opponent and then answers them. This gives the letter a dialogical character. The style is characterized by logical argumentation, biblical quotations from the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament), and vivid images from daily life.

Romans stands first among Paul's letters in the New Testament, not because it was written first, but due to its length and theological weight. The letter has had enormous significance in church history and has been central to major theological upheavals, from Augustine's conversion to Martin Luther's Reformation. Structurally, the letter can be divided into a doctrinal section (chapters 1–11) dealing with salvation, and an exhortatory section (chapters 12–15) providing practical guidelines for Christian living, followed by personal greetings (chapter 16).

## Cultural Background

Roman society was highly hierarchical with clear divisions between social classes. At the top were senators and knights, followed by free citizens, freed slaves, and at the bottom, slaves. The patronage system characterized all social relations—powerful patrons provided support to clients who in return gave loyalty and honor. The Christian house churches partially challenged these structures by bringing together people across social divides for shared meals and worship, although tensions regarding honor and status still persisted.

Religious practice in Rome was polytheistic and pragmatic. Religion primarily concerned the correct performance of rituals to secure the favor of the gods, not personal faith or moral transformation. Alongside the traditional Roman gods flourished mystery cults from the East that promised personal salvation and life after death. Judaism held an established position as *religio licita* (permitted religion) and attracted many sympathizers (*God-fearers*) among Gentiles who admired monotheism and its high ethical standards without fully converting.

The relationship between Jews and Gentiles was complex during this period. Jews constituted a significant minority in Rome, perhaps 40,000–50,000 people, with their own synagogues and rights. At the same time, there was widespread suspicion toward Jewish exclusivity—their rejection of other gods and distinctive customs such as circumcision, Sabbath observance, and dietary practices. The early Christian movement inherited this tension while simultaneously raising new questions: How could Jews and Gentiles worship together? Did Gentiles have to become Jews to become Christians? These questions underlie much of Paul's argumentation in the letter.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Gallio Inscription (52 AD)**
Discovered in Delphi, Greece, in 1905. This inscription mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and can be linked to Acts 18:12–17. It provides a secure chronological anchor for Paul's stay in Corinth and helps us date his missionary journeys and thus also the letter to the Romans.

**The Edict of Claudius (49 AD)**
Mentioned by the Roman historian Suetonius (c. 121 AD), who writes that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because they "constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus." This likely refers to conflicts in Jewish synagogues over Jesus as the Messiah and sheds light on the background of tensions between Jewish and Gentile Christians that Paul addresses.

**The Catacombs of Rome (2nd century AD and later)**
Although from a later period, the Jewish and Christian catacombs in Rome contain inscriptions and symbols that illuminate the coexistence and gradual separation of the two communities. They show how both groups adapted to Roman culture while preserving their distinctive identity.

**Papyrus P46 (c. 175–225 AD)**
Found in Egypt, now divided between Dublin and Michigan. This is the oldest known manuscript of Romans and shows how the letter was preserved and copied in early Christianity. It confirms the integrity of the text and its early spread within the church.

**The Ostia Synagogue (1st–4th century AD)**
Excavated in Ostia, Rome's port city, from 1961. This synagogue is the oldest preserved in Europe and demonstrates Jewish presence and religious practice in the Roman heartland. It provides insight into the Jewish environment from which the first Christians emerged.
