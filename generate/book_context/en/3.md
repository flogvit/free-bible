# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus

## Historical Framework

Leviticus presents itself as God's speech to Moses from the tent of meeting (tabernacle) in the wilderness of Sinai, shortly after the exodus from Egypt, traditionally dated to approximately 1446 B.C. (early dating) or around 1260 B.C. (late dating). Traditionally, Jewish and Christian faith have attributed authorship to Moses. Modern biblical scholarship, particularly the documentary hypothesis, associates most of the material with the "Priestly source" (P), which many scholars date to the exile period (586–539 B.C.) or the early Persian period. At the same time, many acknowledge that the laws are built upon much older traditions from temple service.

The book describes ritual legislation given during the wilderness wanderings but likely also reflects practices from both the First Temple (ca. 960–586 B.C.) and the need to preserve and systematize cultic tradition after the fall of Jerusalem. The priestly tradition had a strong interest in establishing the authority of Aaron's descendants and ensuring the correct performance of rituals.

The religious situation in the described period was characterized by the establishment of Yahwism as Israel's national religion, in contrast to Egyptian polytheism and Canaanite fertility religion. Politically, the Israelites were transitioning from slavery to becoming an independent people with their own law and cultus.

## Literary Context

Leviticus belongs primarily to the genre of law, more specifically ritual and cultic legislation (casuistic and apodictic law). The book contains sacrificial regulations, purity laws, holiness codes, and liturgical instructions. Chapter 16 (the Day of Atonement) has a narrative framework, while most of the text consists of direct divine speech. The "Holiness Code" (chapters 17–26) has a distinct parenetic style with moral exhortations.

The book is the third of the five Books of Moses (Torah/Pentateuch) and constitutes its literary and theological center. In Hebrew, it is called "Vayikra" ("And He Called") after the opening word, or "Torat Kohanim" ("Law of the Priests"). The Greek name "Leviticus" refers to the tribe of Levi (the priests), although Levites are hardly mentioned directly. Structurally, the book can be divided into: sacrificial laws (1–7), consecration of priests (8–10), purity laws (11–15), the Day of Atonement (16), and the Holiness Code (17–26), with additions concerning vows and tithes (27).

## Cultural Background

The society described is a tribal community in transition, organized around the tabernacle as its religious center. The priesthood (Aaron and his sons) constitutes the religious leadership, while Moses functions as the lawgiver. The economy was based on livestock herding and emerging agriculture, which is reflected in the sacrificial regulations (cattle, sheep, grain, oil, wine). The family and clan were fundamental social units, and land ownership was tied to lineage through the Jubilee law.

The religious worldview in Leviticus is built upon a fundamental distinction between holy and profane, clean and unclean. God is absolutely holy and can only be approached through specific rituals performed by authorized priests at the holy place. Sin and impurity threaten the community's relationship with God and must be removed through sacrifices and cleansing rituals. Blood holds central significance as the bearer of life and a means of atonement.

The laws in Leviticus show both similarities to and contrasts with surrounding cultures. Sacrificial practices have parallels throughout the ancient Near East, but Israelite religion stands out due to the prohibition against images, the absence of divine meals, and the ethical connection between cultus and morality (especially in the Holiness Code). Prohibitions against certain practices (child sacrifice to Molech, necromancy, specific sexual practices) define Israel's religious identity in contrast to neighboring peoples.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Ugaritic texts (ca. 1400–1200 B.C.)**
Discovered at Ras Shamra, Syria, from 1929. These texts describe Canaanite religion and sacrificial terminology with striking parallels to Leviticus (sacrifice types such as "šelamim" and "kalil"), demonstrating the shared Semitic background for Israelite cultus.

**The Temple at Ain Dara (ca. 1300–740 B.C., destroyed ca. 740 B.C.)**
Discovered in northern Syria. This Syro-Hittite temple has architectural similarities with the tabernacle and Solomon's Temple, featuring a three-part structure and similar room divisions, illuminating the regional temple tradition.

**Instruction for Temple Officials from Hattusa (Hittite, ca. 1400–1200 B.C.)**
Discovered in central Turkey. These texts contain detailed rules regarding ritual purity among temple personnel, with remarkable similarities to Leviticus' priestly regulations concerning purity and access to the sanctuary.

**The Marseilles Tariff (Punic, ca. 300 B.C.)**
Discovered in Marseille, France (originally from Carthage). This inscription contains a detailed sacrificial tariff with prices and shares for priests, demonstrating that systematized sacrificial legislation was widespread in Semitic cultures.

**The Babylonian šurpu series (compiled ca. 1000 B.C., with older material)**
From Mesopotamia. These ritual texts for cleansing from sin and impurity show parallels to the cleansing rituals in Leviticus, including the symbolic transfer of impurity and the use of water and fire.
