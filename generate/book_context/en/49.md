# Historical and Cultural Context for Ephesians

## Historical Framework

The letter to the Ephesians presents itself as written by the apostle Paul while he was in prison (3:1; 4:1; 6:20), likely in Rome around AD 60–62. The letter belongs to the group of so-called Prison Epistles, together with Colossians, Philippians, and Philemon. Some modern scholars have questioned Pauline authorship due to the letter's distinctive style and theological expressions, but traditionally the church has accepted Paul as the author. The letter lacks the personal greetings that characterize other Pauline letters, which has led to theories that it may have been a circular letter intended for several churches.

Ephesus was the capital of the Roman province of Asia (modern-day western Turkey) and one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire, with an estimated population of 200,000–250,000 people, making it the third or fourth largest city in the empire. Paul himself had founded the church there and ministered in the city for about three years (Acts 19:8–10; 20:31). At the time the letter was written, the church was well established and consisted of both Jewish Christians and Gentile Christian members.

The political situation was marked by the stability of the Roman Empire under Nero (AD 54–68). The imperial cult was strong in Ephesus, and the city had several temples dedicated to Rome and the emperor. At the same time, Jews enjoyed certain religious privileges within the empire, which initially also protected Christians who were viewed as a Jewish sect.

## Literary Context

The letter to the Ephesians belongs to the genre of apostolic letters but stands out due to its solemn, liturgical style that resembles a hymn of praise or a theological meditation. The letter lacks the polemical elements found in Galatians or the detailed responses to specific problems seen in the Corinthian letters. The language is characterized by long, complex sentences (especially chapters 1–3) and a rich use of poetic and hymnic elements.

Structurally, the letter is clearly divided into two main parts: chapters 1–3 are theological and deal with God's plan of salvation and the church's identity in Christ, while chapters 4–6 are practical and address how believers should live out their new identity. The letter has thematic affinity with Colossians, and many passages have parallels between the two letters. In the biblical canon, Ephesians is placed among Paul's letters to churches, after Romans and the Corinthian letters.

## Cultural Background

Ephesus was a cosmopolitan commercial center where people from all over the Mediterranean world met. Society was hierarchically organized with sharp distinctions between free citizens and slaves, men and women, Romans and non-Romans. The household (*oikos*) was the fundamental unit of society, led by the *pater familias*, and included family members, slaves, and clients. The Christian churches met in private homes and had to relate to these social structures, which is reflected in the letter's "household codes" (5:21–6:9).

Religiously, Ephesus was best known for the Temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. The cult of Artemis dominated the city's religious, economic, and social life. In addition, the city was a center for magic and occultism; Acts tells of the burning of magical books (Acts 19:19). Gnostic ideas and mystery religions were also widespread in the region. The letter's language concerning spiritual warfare, cosmic powers, and "the ruler of the kingdom of the air" (2:2; 6:12) spoke directly into this context.

The Jewish diaspora was significant in Ephesus, with synagogues and established communities. The relationship between Jews and Gentiles was a persistent area of tension, which the letter addresses directly in its theology that Christ has "broken down the dividing wall" between the groups (2:14). Hellenistic culture, with its philosophy, rhetoric, and values, characterized the entire society and formed the background for the letter's ethical exhortations.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Temple of Artemis (Artemision)**
- Dating: Begun under King Croesus around 550 BC, completed around 430 BC, burned down in 356 BC and rebuilt; finally destroyed in AD 262.
- Rediscovered by John Turtle Wood in 1869 after excavations began in 1863.
- Illuminates the dominant religious context in which the church lived and the conflict described in Acts 19.

**Library of Celsus and Agora in Ephesus**
- Dating: The library from AD 117, the agora is older.
- Located in the center of ancient Ephesus.
- Demonstrates the city's role as a cultural and intellectual center and provides insight into daily life in a major Roman city.

**Terrace Houses in Ephesus**
- Dating: From the 1st century BC to the 7th century AD.
- Found on the slope along Curetes Street in Ephesus.
- Provides insight into how wealthy households were organized, relevant for understanding house churches and household codes.

**Inscriptions Concerning the Imperial Cult from Ephesus**
- Dating: 1st century AD.
- Found in various temples and public buildings in Ephesus.
- Documents the pressure Christians experienced from the official imperial cult and makes the letter's proclamation of Christ's lordship over all powers politically relevant.

**Chester Beatty Papyrus (P46)**
- Dating: Around AD 200.
- Found in Egypt.
- One of the oldest surviving copies of Ephesians, important for textual history and confirming the early circulation of the letter.
