# Psalms: Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

The Book of Psalms represents a collection of Hebrew poetry composed over a period of nearly a thousand years, from approximately 1000 BC to 400 BC. Traditionally, 73 psalms are attributed to King David, while others are linked to Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses (Psalm 90), and Ethan. Modern scholarship recognizes that the collection underwent a long editorial history, in which older psalms were preserved, edited, and gathered together with newer compositions over several centuries.

The Psalms reflect various historical eras in Israel's history: from the wilderness wanderings, through the monarchy under David and Solomon (ca. 1000–930 BC), the division of the kingdom, the Babylonian exile (586–539 BC), and the Persian period with the rebuilding of the temple. This lengthy process of composition explains why the Psalms contain such diverse experiences—from triumphant royal psalms to laments from the exile.

The final collection was likely completed during the Persian or early Hellenistic period (ca. 400–200 BC), when temple service in Jerusalem had been restored under Persian rule. The Psalms then served as the official songbook for the Second Temple and were used in both public worship and private devotion.

## Literary Context

The Psalms belong to the wisdom literature and poetry of the Old Testament, along with Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs. Hebrew poetry is characterized by parallelism (thoughts are repeated or contrasted in verse lines), imagery, acrostic structures, and varying rhythmic patterns. The Psalms include several subgenres: hymns (songs of praise), laments (individual and communal), thanksgiving psalms, wisdom psalms, royal psalms, pilgrimage songs, and imprecations.

The book is divided into five "books" that may reflect the fivefold division of the Pentateuch: Book I (Psalm 1–41), Book II (42–72), Book III (73–89), Book IV (90–106), and Book V (107–150). Each book concludes with a doxology (a formula of praise). Psalm 1 serves as an entrance gate to the entire collection with its focus on the Torah, while Psalm 150 ends with a climax of praise. In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), the Psalms are placed in Ketuvim (Writings).

## Cultural Background

Israelite society underwent significant changes during the period when the Psalms were composed. During the monarchy, society was organized around kingship, the priesthood, and tribal structures. Jerusalem with its temple became the religious center under David and Solomon. Daily life for most people was tied to agriculture, livestock herding, and craftsmanship. The family was the basic unit, and religious practice included both temple visits during major festivals and daily rituals in the home.

The religious worldview presented in the Psalms assumes one God (YHWH) who created the world, chose Israel as His people, and acts actively in history. The temple was the meeting point between heaven and earth, where offerings, songs, and prayers were offered. The Levites, especially the singing families such as Asaph, Korah, and Heman, were responsible for temple music, and many psalm superscriptions contain musical directions that testify to a sophisticated liturgical tradition with instruments such as the harp (nevel), lyre (kinnor), cymbals, and wind instruments like the shofar.

Israel's religious practice stood in dialogue and contrast with surrounding cultures. Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Canaanite religions all knew psalm traditions and temple cults. Several motifs in the Psalms—such as God's struggle against chaotic forces, the heavenly throne room, and the king as a divine representative—have parallels in these cultures but are reshaped in light of Yahwistic monotheism.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Ugaritic texts (ca. 1400–1200 BC)** – Discovered at Ras Shamra, Syria, from 1929. These Canaanite texts contain hymns and myths with poetic language and motifs similar to the Psalms, especially those connected to the gods Baal and El. They illuminate the cultural and literary context in which the Psalms originated.

**Dead Sea Scrolls (ca. 250 BC–68 AD)** – Discovered at Qumran from 1947. These include the oldest known manuscripts of the Psalms, as well as additional psalms outside the biblical canon. They demonstrate the transmission of the text and variations in the scope of the psalm collection during late Second Temple Judaism.

**Tel Dan Inscription (ca. 840 BC)** – Discovered at Tel Dan, Israel, in 1993. This Aramaic inscription mentions "the house of David," which is the earliest extra-biblical testimony to King David and supports the historicity of the Davidic tradition underlying many psalms.

**Egyptian Hymns to Aton (ca. 1350 BC)** – From the Amarna period in Egypt. Especially the "Hymn to Aton" shows striking similarities with Psalm 104, demonstrating cultural exchange in the ancient Near East and shared poetic traditions for praising the Creator God.

**Assyrian Annals (900–600 BC)** – Discovered at Nineveh and other sites in Mesopotamia. These describe historical events such as the siege of Jerusalem under Sennacherib (701 BC), which form the background for several psalms connected to national crisis and deliverance.
