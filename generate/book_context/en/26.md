# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel

## Historical Framework

Ezekiel was a priest and prophet who ministered among the Jewish exiles in Babylon from approximately 593–571 B.C. He was deported to Babylon in 597 B.C. along with King Jehoiachin and the elite of Judah, and received his call by the Kebar River five years later. The book presents itself as Ezekiel’s own words and visions, and most scholars accept that the material largely originates from the prophet himself, although it may have undergone later editing by his disciples.

The historical framework of the book spans one of the most dramatic periods in Israel’s history. Ezekiel prophesied before, during, and after the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. The first 24 chapters, written before the catastrophe, warn against the coming judgment. After news of the city’s fall reached the exiles, the message shifted to hope for restoration.

The political situation was marked by the fall of the Neo-Assyrian Empire and the rise of the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II. Judah found itself caught between the great powers of Babylon and Egypt, and rebellions against Babylon led to the two deportations in 597 and 586 B.C. Religiously, the period was characterized by syncretism, idolatry, and breach of the covenant with Yahweh, which Ezekiel sharply criticizes.

## Literary Context

Ezekiel is a prophetic book characterized by visions, symbolic actions, and allegories. The book stands out for its dramatic and sometimes bizarre visions, such as the throne vision in chapter 1 and the valley of dry bones in chapter 37. Ezekiel also employs extended allegories, such as the story of the two sisters Oholah and Oholibah (chap. 23), and performs symbolic actions that illustrate his message.

The book is placed among the Major Prophets in the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), in the section called Neviim (Prophets). It naturally follows Jeremiah, who prophesied during the same period but in Judah. The structure is clearly threefold: oracles of judgment against Judah and Jerusalem (chaps. 1–24), oracles of judgment against foreign nations (chaps. 25–32), and promises of restoration (chaps. 33–48). The concluding chapters (40–48) contain a detailed vision of the future temple.

## Cultural Background

The exiles in Babylon lived in their own settlements where they could maintain a certain degree of self-governance. Ezekiel resided in Tel-abib by the Kebar Canal, one of the irrigation canals in the Babylonian canal system. The elders served as leaders among the exiles, and people gathered at Ezekiel’s house to hear the prophet’s words. Economically, the Jews were able to engage in trade and agriculture, and some achieved considerable wealth.

Religiously, the exile represented a deep crisis: the temple was destroyed, and many wondered whether Yahweh had been defeated by Babylon’s gods. Ezekiel emphasizes that Yahweh’s glory left the temple because of the people’s sin (chaps. 10–11), not because of powerlessness. His vision of the new temple (chaps. 40–48) points toward a future restoration in which Yahweh’s glory will return. Ezekiel’s priestly background shapes the book, with strong emphasis on holiness, purity, and cultic regulations.

Babylon was the center of a highly developed civilization with impressive architecture, advanced astrology, and a rich religious tradition centered around gods such as Marduk and Nabu. The Jews were exposed to these cultural and religious currents, which both threatened their identity and forced them to define their faith in new ways. Ezekiel combats both adaptation to Babylonian religion and nostalgic worship of Canaanite gods.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Babylonian Ration Lists (ca. 592 B.C.)** – Found in Babylon, these cuneiform tablets document the distribution of oil and barley to King Jehoiachin of Judah and his sons. This confirms the biblical account of the deportation and shows that Jehoiachin was kept alive in Babylon.

**The Al-Yahudu Archive (6th century B.C.)** – Discovered on the antiquities market but likely originating from the area southeast of Babylon, these more than 100 tablets document a Jewish settlement called "Judah City" and reveal the daily life of Jewish exiles, including trade, property, and family relations.

**Nebuchadnezzar’s Chronicles (605–594 B.C.)** – Found in Babylon, these describe the conquest of Jerusalem in 597 B.C. and confirm the biblical date. The chronicles provide valuable context for the political situation in which Ezekiel prophesied.

**The Lachish Letters (ca. 588–586 B.C.)** – Found at Lachish in Israel, these ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) document the final days before Jerusalem’s fall. They offer insight into the desperate situation in Judah about which Ezekiel prophesied from his exile.

**The Ishtar Gate and Processional Way (ca. 575 B.C.)** – Reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin from excavations at Babylon, these monuments illustrate the splendor of Babylonian civilization that the exiles encountered. They provide an understanding of the cultural environment in which Ezekiel ministered.
