# Historical and Cultural Context for the Gospel of Matthew

## Historical Framework

The Gospel of Matthew was likely written between 80 and 90 CE, although some scholars argue for an earlier date in the 60s. Traditionally attributed to the apostle Matthew (also called Levi), a former tax collector who became one of Jesus' twelve disciples, most modern scholars believe the author was an unknown Jewish-Christian scribe who used the Gospel of Mark as a primary source, along with a hypothetical source called Q (from German "Quelle") and unique material. The gospel was probably written in Antioch in Syria or in Galilee.

The book describes Jesus' life and ministry from approximately 6/4 BCE to 30/33 CE, during the Roman occupation of Judea. By the time the gospel was written, the Romans had destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in 70 CE—a catastrophic event that shaped the entire Jewish community. This destruction forms an important backdrop for the gospel's sharp polemic against religious leaders.

The political situation was characterized by Roman supremacy. Herod the Great ruled as a client king when Jesus was born, while his sons divided the kingdom after his death. From 6 CE onward, Judea was governed directly by Roman procurators. Religiously, Jewish society was fragmented among Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and Zealot groups. After the temple's fall, the Pharisaic movement became dominant and developed into Rabbinic Judaism.

## Literary Context

The Gospel of Matthew belongs to the genre "gospel"—a unique Christian literary form combining biographical elements with theological proclamation. The gospel has a distinct didactic (teaching) style and is structured around five major discourses, which likely intentionally mirror the five books of Moses. Matthew quotes the Old Testament more often than any other gospel, frequently using the formula "so that what was spoken by the prophet might be fulfilled".

As the first gospel in the New Testament, Matthew forms a natural bridge between the OT and NT. The book is structured with a prologue about Jesus' birth and childhood (chapters 1-2), Jesus' public ministry in Galilee (chapters 3-18), the journey to Jerusalem and the passion narrative (chapters 19-27), and the resurrection account (chapter 28). The five discourses are: the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5-7), the mission discourse (chapter 10), the parable discourse (chapter 13), instructions to the church (chapter 18), and the judgment discourse (chapters 23-25).

## Cultural Background

The Jewish society of Jesus' time was hierarchically organized, with chief priests, scribes, and elders serving as religious and political leaders. The synagogue functioned as a local center for teaching, prayer, and community life, while the temple in Jerusalem was the absolute religious focal point. Daily life was regulated by the Torah and the interpretations of the scribes. The family was the fundamental social unit, with a patriarchal structure. Tax collectors like Matthew were despised as collaborators with the occupying power.

Religious practice included Sabbath observance, purity laws, circumcision, tithing, and pilgrimages to Jerusalem during major festivals (Passover, Pentecost, Feast of Tabernacles). Jews expected a Messiah who would restore the kingdom of Israel, but ideas about how this should happen varied. Matthew presents Jesus as the fulfillment of these expectations, yet with an unexpected twist—a suffering Messiah establishing a spiritual kingdom.

Jews lived in constant tension with surrounding Hellenistic culture and Roman religion. Emperor worship and Greco-Roman polytheism were rejected as idolatry. At the same time, Diaspora Judaism was influenced by Hellenistic philosophy and culture. The Gospel of Matthew also reflects the growing tension between the early Christian movement and the synagogue, suggesting a rupture that was occurring when the gospel was written.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Pilate Inscription (ca. 26-36 CE)**
Discovered in Caesarea Maritima in 1961, this is the only contemporary inscription mentioning Pontius Pilate by name. It confirms his historical existence and title as prefect of Judea, strengthening the historical reliability of the gospels.

**The Caiaphas Ossuary (1st century CE)**
Discovered in a tomb cave in Jerusalem in 1990, this bone box likely contains the remains of the high priest Caiaphas who led the trial against Jesus. The inscription reads "Joseph son of Caiaphas", and the find confirms the existence of this central figure in the passion narrative.

**The Dead Sea Scrolls (ca. 250 BCE - 70 CE)**
Found at Qumran from 1947 onward, these texts provide invaluable insight into Jewish belief, practice, and messianic expectations during Jesus' time. They reveal the diversity of Jewish thought and help us understand the religious context of Jesus' proclamation.

**The Writings of Josephus (ca. 75-94 CE)**
The Jewish historian Josephus wrote "The Jewish War" and "Jewish Antiquities" in Rome. He mentions John the Baptist, Jesus, and James (Jesus' brother), providing detailed information about the Herodian dynasty, Jewish sects, and the political situation in the first century.

**The Magdala Synagogue (1st century CE)**
Discovered in 2009 by the Sea of Galilee, this is one of the oldest synagogues found in Galilee. It contains a unique stone with depictions of the temple and provides insight into religious life in the region where Jesus carried out much of his ministry.
