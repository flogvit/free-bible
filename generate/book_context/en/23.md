# Isaiah – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

The book of Isaiah spans a period of more than two hundred years in the history of Judah. The prophet Isaiah, son of Amoz, served in Jerusalem from approximately 740 to 700 B.C., during the reigns of kings Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. His ministry coincided with the expansion of the Neo-Assyrian Empire under rulers such as Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, and Sennacherib. The northern kingdom of Israel fell to the Assyrians in 722 B.C., reducing Judah to a vassal state.

Modern biblical scholarship typically distinguishes three main sections with different historical backgrounds: First Isaiah (chapters 1–39) reflects the Assyrian threat of the eighth century; Second Isaiah (chapters 40–55) presupposes the Babylonian exile in the sixth century and speaks of Cyrus as the Lord’s anointed; while Third Isaiah (chapters 56–66) appears to address the post-exilic rebuilding period after the return from captivity. Traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation has attributed the entire book to the prophet Isaiah, whereas critical scholarship views it as the result of a lengthy editorial process in which Isaiah’s original message was expanded by later disciples and tradition-bearers.

The political situation changed dramatically throughout this period—from Assyrian dominance through Babylonian rule to Persian liberation under Cyrus in 539 B.C. Religiously, the prophets fought against syncretism and idolatry while holding fast to faith in Yahweh as the only God and Lord of history.

## Literary Context

The book of Isaiah represents a rich blend of literary genres: oracles of judgment and salvation, visionary literature, hymnic passages, historical narrative, and apocalyptic texts. The language is among the most poetically refined in the Old Testament, featuring powerful imagery, wordplay, and parallelism. Second Isaiah contains the famous "Servant of the Lord" songs, which have had enormous significance in both Jewish and Christian interpretive traditions.

In the Hebrew canon (Tanakh), Isaiah is regarded as the first of the "Latter Prophets" (Nevi'im acharonim) and stands first among the major writing prophets. The book’s structure follows a basic pattern: judgment upon Judah and the nations (1–39), comfort and restoration (40–55), and exhortations to the restored community (56–66). This movement from judgment to hope gives the book a theological unity that binds its various parts together.

## Cultural Background

Society in Isaiah’s time was marked by social contradictions. The prophet criticized the wealthy who gathered "house to house and field to field" (5:8) at the expense of the poor. Jerusalem functioned as the religious and political center, with the temple as the most important institution. The king played a central role as Yahweh’s representative, and the Davidic dynastic tradition was fundamental to the people’s self-understanding.

Religious practice included sacrifices in the temple, pilgrimages to Jerusalem during the major festivals, and Sabbath observance. At the same time, Isaiah criticized outward piety lacking ethical substance (1:11–17). The Babylonian exile brought about a radical change: without a temple, faith had to find new forms of expression through scriptural study, prayer, and Sabbath observance.

Judah was surrounded by the great powers Assyria, Egypt, and later Babylon, and had to navigate between them. The prophet warned against political alliances that replaced trust in God. Canaanite religion with its fertility cults and worship of gods such as Baal posed a constant temptation. Second Isaiah’s polemic against idols (44:9–20) shows that the struggle for religious loyalty continued even during the exile.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Sennacherib Prism (Taylor Prism)** – ca. 691–689 B.C., discovered in Nineveh (now in the British Museum). This Assyrian cuneiform tablet describes Sennacherib’s campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. and the siege of Jerusalem. The king boasts of having shut up Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" but does not mention capturing the city—something that corresponds with Isaiah’s prophecy concerning the city’s deliverance (37:33–35).

**Cyrus Cylinder** – ca. 539 B.C., discovered in Babylon (now in the British Museum). This Persian document describes Cyrus’s conquest of Babylon and his policy of allowing deported peoples to return home. This confirms the historical background for Second Isaiah’s prophecies concerning Cyrus as the Lord’s anointed (44:28–45:1).

**Siloam Inscription** – ca. 701 B.C., discovered in Hezekiah’s Tunnel in Jerusalem (now in Istanbul). The inscription describes the completion of the water channel that brought water into the city—a defensive measure mentioned in 2 Kings 20:20 and belonging to the same period during which Isaiah served.

**Dead Sea Scrolls, Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ)** – ca. 150–125 B.C., discovered at Qumran in 1947. This is the oldest complete copy of a biblical book ever found and shows that the book of Isaiah already existed as a unified whole at this time. The text is remarkably similar to the Masoretic Text.

**Lachish Letters** – ca. 588–586 B.C., discovered at Tell ed-Duweir (biblical Lachish) in present-day Israel. These ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) provide insight into the situation in Judah just before Jerusalem’s fall and illustrate the desperation and prophetic activity that characterized the period between Isaiah and the exile.
