# Historical and Cultural Context for Hosea

## Historical Framework

Hosea served as a prophet in the Northern Kingdom (Israel) over a long period from about 750 to 720 B.C., during the final decades before Assyria's destruction of the kingdom. The heading (1:1) places him under the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah in Judah, as well as Jeroboam II in Israel. This spans a dramatic period from Israel's last era of greatness under Jeroboam II to the total collapse of the kingdom. Hosea's message was likely recorded and edited by his disciples, with a final redaction in Judah after the fall of the Northern Kingdom in 722 B.C.

Jeroboam II's reign (793–753 B.C.) was marked by territorial expansion and economic prosperity, but this outward success concealed deep moral and religious decay. After his death followed a chaotic period with rapid changes on the throne, coups, and regicide. Six kings ruled within twenty years, four of whom were murdered. At the same time, Assyria rose under Tiglath-Pileser III as a dominant superpower threatening Israel's existence.

The religious situation was characterized by syncretism, where Yahweh worship was mixed with Canaanite Baal cults. The people participated in fertility rituals at the high places, which Hosea condemned as spiritual "adultery." This religious unfaithfulness to the covenant with Yahweh was for Hosea the very root of Israel's political and social problems.

## Literary Context

Hosea belongs to the Twelve Minor Prophets and stands first in this collection in the Hebrew Bible. The book combines several literary genres: symbolic narrative about the prophet's marriage (chapters 1–3), oracles of judgment, accusations formulated as a legal case (Heb. *riv*), laments, and words of hope. The language is poetic and often obscure, with abrupt transitions and intense emotional outbursts reflecting God's wounded love.

The book can be divided into two main parts: chapters 1–3 deal with Hosea's marriage to Gomer as a living picture of God's relationship with Israel, while chapters 4–14 contain prophetic speeches with accusations, judgments, and promises of restoration. Hosea develops unique theological images that have influenced later biblical writers, especially the concept of the covenant relationship between God and Israel as a marriage.

## Cultural Background

Society in Hosea's time was marked by increasing social inequality. The economic growth under Jeroboam II primarily benefited the elite, while farmers lost their land and fell into debt slavery. Corruption characterized the judicial system, and the priesthood neglected its duty to teach the people God's law (4:4–6). Trade flourished along caravan routes, but with it came cultural influence from surrounding peoples.

Religious practice in the Northern Kingdom was diverse and syncretistic. Yahweh was worshiped at the sanctuaries in Bethel and Dan with calf images which Hosea condemned (8:5–6; 10:5). At the same time, Baal was worshiped at high places and under green trees, with rituals that included cultic prostitution and fertility rites. The people believed Baal gave rain, grain, wine, and oil—gifts Hosea insisted came from Yahweh (2:8).

Israel's political situation was marked by a dangerous game between the superpowers Egypt and Assyria. Various factions at court sought support now from one, then the other (7:11; 12:1), a policy Hosea saw as an expression of lack of trust in Yahweh. This wavering between superpowers would eventually lead to Israel's downfall when Assyria under Shalmaneser V and Sargon II besieged and conquered Samaria in 722 B.C.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Samaria Ostraca (ca. 784–772 B.C.)**
Found in Samaria during excavations in the early 1900s. These pottery shards with ink inscriptions document deliveries of wine and oil to the royal palace. They confirm the economic prosperity under Jeroboam II and mention personal names composed with both Yahweh and Baal, illustrating the religious syncretism Hosea attacked.

**Assyrian Royal Annals from Tiglath-Pileser III (745–727 B.C.)**
Found in Nimrud (ancient Calah) in present-day Iraq. These cuneiform texts describe Assyrian campaigns against Israel and mention King Menahem of Israel who paid tribute (cf. 2 Kings 15:19–20). The annals document the political instability in Israel that forms the backdrop for Hosea's prophecies.

**Sargon II's Annals (722–705 B.C.)**
Found in Khorsabad (Dur-Sharrukin) in Iraq. Sargon boasts of having conquered Samaria and deported 27,290 inhabitants. This confirms the final fulfillment of Hosea's judgment prophecies and explains why the book was preserved and edited in Judah.

**Kuntillet Ajrud Inscriptions (ca. 800 B.C.)**
Found at a roadside station in northern Sinai. The inscriptions mention "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah," confirming the syncretistic religious practice where Yahweh was associated with a female deity. This sheds light on the cultic "adultery" Hosea condemned.

**The Dan Stele (ca. 841 B.C.)**
Found at Tel Dan in northern Israel. This Aramaic inscription from a generation before Hosea mentions "the house of Israel" and confirms the regional conflict with Aram-Damascus that also characterizes the background for Hosea's prophecies.
