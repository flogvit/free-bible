# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis

## Historical Framework

Traditionally, Genesis has been attributed to Moses, which would place its composition around 1400–1200 BCE. Modern biblical scholarship often operates with the Documentary Hypothesis, suggesting that the book is composed of several sources: the Yahwist (J), the Elohist (E), and the Priestly Code (P), edited together in their final form during or after the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE). Regardless of one's view on authorship, the text reflects ancient oral traditions preserved through generations.

The book covers an enormous span of time—from creation through the era of the patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph), traditionally dated to approximately 2000–1700 BCE. The patriarchs' journeys between Mesopotamia, Canaan, and Egypt place the narratives in the Bronze Age, a period characterized by population migrations, city-states, and emerging empires. The time of writing likely occurred several centuries after the events described.

Politically, the Middle East during the patriarchal era was dominated by shifting centers of power: Sumerian Ur, the Babylonian empire under Hammurabi, the Egyptian Middle Kingdom, and the Hittites in Anatolia. Canaan was a patchwork of city-states under varying degrees of Egyptian influence. Religiously, polytheism was the norm, with local gods associated with cities, natural forces, and fertility. Israel's monotheistic tradition represented a striking break from this context.

## Literary Context

Genesis belongs to several genres: primeval history (chapters 1–11), patriarchal narratives (chapters 12–50), genealogies, and poetic insertions. The primeval history has a mytho-theological character with a universal perspective, while the patriarchal narratives are family sagas with an episodic structure. The book combines narrative prose with genealogical lists ("toledot" formulas: "This is the account of...") that function as structuring elements throughout the work.

As the first book of the Torah (the Books of Moses) and of the entire Bible, Genesis lays the foundation for everything that follows. It establishes God's character as Creator and covenant partner, humanity's position and fall, and Israel's origin as a chosen people. Structurally, the book is often divided into two main parts: primeval history (1–11), which deals with all of humanity, and patriarchal history (12–50), which narrows the focus to Abraham's descendants. Ten "toledot" formulas give the book its internal structure.

## Cultural Background

Society in the patriarchal era was semi-nomadic and clan-based. The family was the fundamental unit, with the patriarch as head possessing legal and religious authority. Women had limited legal status, but matriarchs such as Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel played crucial roles. Marriage alliances were strategic, and inheritance rights, primogeniture, and blessing held enormous significance. Hospitality was a sacred duty, and pastoral life centered on small livestock dominated the economy.

Religious practice in the patriarchal narratives includes altar building, sacrifices, theophanies (divine revelations), and covenant rituals. God reveals Himself at specific places that become holy (Bethel, Mamre, Beersheba). In contrast to surrounding religions, Israel's God is not tied to a single location but follows the patriarchs on their journeys. Dreams function as a medium of revelation throughout the book. The worldview includes a three-part cosmology: heaven, earth, and the underworld.

Parallels with surrounding cultures are significant. The creation account and the flood narrative share structural similarities with Mesopotamian myths, yet convey a distinct theological message. Patriarchal customs—such as surrogate mother arrangements, adoption practices, and inheritance laws—reflect procedures known from Mesopotamian law codes. At the same time, the text marks a distance from Canaanite religion, particularly fertility cults and human sacrifice.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Ebla Tablets (ca. 2400–2300 BCE)**
Discovered at Tell Mardikh, Syria, with excavations beginning in 1964 and the main archive findings in 1974–1975. These cuneiform tablets document trade connections, administrative practices, and city-states in Syria-Palestine that correspond to Genesis' geographical horizon. Early claims of biblical personal names in the archive have been strongly criticized and are no longer widely accepted.

**Nuzi Texts (ca. 1500–1350 BCE)**
Discovered at Nuzi (modern Yorghan Tepe, Iraq) from 1925 onward. These Hurrian documents describe family law practices—such as adoption arrangements, surrogate mothers, and the binding force of oral blessings—that resemble customs found in the patriarchal narratives (Sarah/Hagar, Jacob's blessing).

**Laws of Hammurabi (ca. 1754 BCE)**
Discovered at Susa, Iran (originally from Babylon) in 1901–1902. This law code from Babylon illuminates legal conventions in Mesopotamia, including slave legislation, marriage contracts, and inheritance rules that provide background for understanding the actions of the patriarchs.

**Mari Archives (ca. 1800–1750 BCE)**
Discovered at Tell Hariri, Syria, from 1933 onward. Over 20,000 tablets document semi-nomadic lifestyles, tribal organization, and prophetic phenomena in upper Mesopotamia. They offer valuable insight into the way of life and political structure in Abraham's original homeland.

**Ipuwer Papyrus (copy from ca. 1250 BCE, possibly based on an older text)**
Held at the Rijksmuseum van Oudheden in Leiden, Netherlands. This Egyptian lament describes social and natural chaos in Egypt. Although some have seen parallels to the plagues in Exodus, this is disputed, and the text is used primarily to understand Egyptian literary genre and social conditions relevant to the Egyptian context of the Joseph narrative.
