# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings

## Historical Framework

1 Kings covers a period of approximately 120 years in Israel's history, from the end of King David's reign (c. 970 B.C.) to the reign of Ahaziah king of Israel (c. 853 B.C.). The book describes Solomon's golden age with the building of the temple, the division of the kingdom after his death (traditionally dated to c. 931/930 B.C.), and the subsequent parallel history of the kingdoms of Israel (north) and Judah (south). In Jewish tradition (Talmud, Bava Batra 15a), authorship is attributed to the prophet Jeremiah, but modern scholarship regards the book as part of the Deuteronomistic History, likely edited into its final form during or after the Babylonian exile (c. 550–500 B.C.).

The book draws on several earlier sources that are explicitly mentioned: "the Book of the Acts of Solomon," "the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel," and "the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah." These were likely official court annals documenting the deeds of the kings. The Deuteronomistic editor collected and edited this material with a clear theological perspective: the kings are judged by their faithfulness to the Lord and the centralization of worship in Jerusalem.

The political situation during this period was marked by shifts in power in the Middle East. Egypt and Assyria were alternately weakened, creating room for a united Israelite kingdom under David and Solomon. After the division of the kingdom, both small states found themselves caught between great powers, with Egypt as a threat from the south and the increasingly powerful Assyria from the north. Religiously, the period was characterized by tension between Yahwism and Canaanite Baal worship, especially evident in the Elijah narratives.

## Literary Context

1 Kings belongs to the genre of historical writing with theological interpretation. It combines annalistic royal history with prophetic stories, particularly the Elijah cycle. The style alternates between concise royal summaries using standardized formulas and vivid narratives featuring direct speech and dramatic tension. The book is shaped by the Deuteronomistic perspective, in which obedience to the law leads to blessing and apostasy to judgment.

In the Hebrew canon (Tanakh), 1 Kings belongs to the "Former Prophets" (Nevi'im rishonim), together with Joshua, Judges, and the books of Samuel. In the Septuagint and Christian Bibles, it is classified as historical literature. Originally, 1 and 2 Kings formed a single continuous book. The structure follows a geographical and thematic pattern: the united kingdom under Solomon, the division of the kingdom, and the parallel history of the two kingdoms with the ministry of the prophet Elijah woven throughout.

## Cultural Background

Society in this period was a hierarchical agrarian community with the king at the top, supported by a court administration, priests, and military leaders. Under Solomon, Israel developed into a more centralized state characterized by forced labor, taxation, and an extensive trade network. The population lived mainly in villages and small towns, with Jerusalem as the religious and political center. After the division of the kingdom, the northern kingdom established alternative cult centers at Dan and Bethel. Daily life was shaped by agriculture, livestock herding, and craftsmanship, with the family as the basic social unit.

Religious practice centered on sacrifices and festivals. Solomon's temple in Jerusalem became the central place of worship for Judah, while the northern kingdom developed its own sanctuaries. Popular religion was often syncretistic, incorporating elements from Canaanite religion: Baal (a fertility god), Asherah (a goddess), and high places throughout the land. The Deuteronomistic ideal of one God, one temple, and pure worship stood in contrast to this popular practice.

Relations with surrounding cultures were close and complex. Solomon formed alliances through marriages with foreign princesses, including Pharaoh's daughter, and trade agreements with Phoenicia (King Hiram of Tyre). Phoenician craftsmanship was crucial for the building of the temple. Cultural influence also came from Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Aramean kingdoms. Conflicts with neighboring peoples—the Arameans, Philistines, Moabites, and Edomites—characterize much of the second half of the book.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Tel Dan Stele (c. 840 B.C.)**
Discovered at Tel Dan in northern Israel in 1993 (with additional fragments found in 1994). This Aramaic inscription, erected by an Aramean king (likely Hazael of Damascus), mentions the "House of David" (byt dwd) and thus confirms the existence of a Davidic dynasty. It refers to military victories over Israel and Judah and correlates with events described in the books of Kings.

**Mesha Stele/Moabite Stone (c. 840 B.C.)**
Discovered at Dibon in Jordan in 1868. King Mesha of Moab describes his liberation from Israelite domination under the Omride dynasty. The stele mentions "the king of Israel," "Omri," and confirms Israel's dominance over Moab as suggested in the books of Kings. It also provides insight into Moabite religion and society.

**Kuntillet Ajrud Inscriptions (c. 800 B.C.)**
Discovered in northeastern Sinai in 1975–1976. Inscriptions mentioning "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" testify to the syncretistic popular religion that the prophets and Deuteronomistic writers condemned. This archaeological find illuminates the religious context for the Elijah narratives.

**Assyrian Royal Inscriptions (9th–8th centuries B.C.)**
From Nineveh and other Assyrian cities. The Kurkh Monolith of Shalmaneser III mentions "Ahab the Israelite" as a participant in the coalition against Assyria at the Battle of Qarqar (853 B.C.), contributing 2,000 chariots and 10,000 soldiers. This confirms Ahab's military strength and places Israelite history within a broader international context, even though this event is not directly mentioned in the Bible.

**Samaria Ostraca (c. 770–750 B.C.)**
Found at Samaria, the capital of Israel. These pottery shards with ink inscriptions document administrative and economic conditions in the northern kingdom. The personal names reveal a mixture of Yahwism (names ending in -yahu) and Baal worship (names beginning with Baal-), illustrating the religious syncretism that the book criticizes.
