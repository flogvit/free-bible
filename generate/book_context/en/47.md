# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians

## Historical Framework

The Second Letter to the Corinthians was written by the apostle Paul, likely around AD 55–56, from Macedonia. This is one of the undisputed Pauline letters, with Paul's authorship widely accepted among scholars. The letter emerged during a period of strained relations between Paul and the congregation he himself had founded in Corinth around AD 50–51. Several events occurred between 1 and 2 Corinthians: Paul had made a "painful visit" to Corinth (2 Cor 2:1), written a "letter with tears" that is now lost (2 Cor 2:4), and sent Titus to resolve the conflict.

Some scholars suggest that 2 Corinthians may be composed of multiple letters, particularly because chapters 10–13 have a markedly different tone than chapters 1–9. This theory remains debated, and many maintain the unity of the letter, explaining the shift in tone by Paul's receipt of new, troubling news while he was writing.

At that time, Corinth was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia and an important commercial city under Emperor Nero. The city was known for its cosmopolitan character, religious diversity, and moral challenges. The congregation consisted mainly of Gentile Christians from various social backgrounds and faced pressure both from Jewish-Christian opponents ("super-apostles") and the surrounding Greco-Roman culture.

## Literary Context

The Second Letter to the Corinthians belongs to the genre of Hellenistic letters but is among Paul's most personal and emotionally charged epistles. It contains elements of apologetics (defense speech), exhortation, and autobiographical narrative. The style alternates between intense emotional appeals, ironic polemic (especially in the "fool's speech" in chapters 11–12) and theological reflection. Unlike 1 Corinthians, which deals more with concrete congregational problems, this letter focuses more on the nature of the apostolic office and Paul's own legitimacy.

In the biblical canon, 2 Corinthians is placed as the eighth book of the New Testament, following 1 Corinthians. The letter can be structured into three main sections: Paul's defense of his ministry and reconciliation with the congregation (chapters 1–7), the collection for the poor in Jerusalem (chapters 8–9), and the powerful defense against false apostles (chapters 10–13).

## Cultural Background

Corinth was a rebuilt Roman colony, destroyed by the Romans in 146 BC and restored by Julius Caesar in 44 BC. Its population was a mixture of Roman veterans, freed slaves, Greeks, and Jews. The city's location on the isthmus between two harbors (Cenchreae and Lechaeum) made it a hub for trade between east and west. Social mobility and the pursuit of status characterized society, creating tensions within the congregation as well, where some sought worldly honor and eloquence.

Religiously, Corinth was marked by diversity: temples dedicated to Apollo, Aphrodite, Asclepius, Isis, and the imperial cult existed side by side. Greco-Roman rhetoric was highly valued, and traveling philosophers and orators competed for followers. This environment explains why Paul's opponents criticized his "weak" appearance and simple speech (2 Cor 10:10), and why he had to defend a cross-centered theology that appeared as foolishness to the Greeks.

The relationship between Judaism and the emerging Christian movement was complex. The "super-apostles" whom Paul criticizes were likely Jewish-Christian missionaries who emphasized their Jewish roots and spectacular spiritual experiences. The collection for Jerusalem (chapters 8–9) had both practical and symbolic significance: it aimed to relieve need while demonstrating unity between Gentile and Jewish-Christian congregations.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Gallio Inscription (AD 52)**
Found in Delphi, Greece. This inscription mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and confirms the dating of Paul's first stay in Corinth to AD 50–51 (cf. Acts 18:12–17), providing a firm anchor for the chronology of Paul's ministry.

**The Erastus Inscription (1st century AD)**
Found in Corinth near the theater. The inscription mentions an Erastus who funded the paving of a square as thanks for his office. He may be identical with the Erastus mentioned in Romans 16:23, showing that some Christians held high social status.

**The Temple of Asclepius in Corinth**
Excavated in Corinth. The healing cult here illuminates the religious context in which Paul discusses weakness and strength, and God's power being made perfect in weakness (2 Cor 12:9).

**The Isthmian Sanctuary**
Just outside Corinth. The Isthmian Games were held every two years and attracted large crowds. This athletic environment may have influenced Paul's use of athletic metaphors and his understanding of honor and shame within the Corinthian context.

**Papyrus Letters from Egypt (1st–2nd century AD)**
Found in several Egyptian locations. These private letters show standard formulas and conventions in Hellenistic letter writing, helping us understand both what was common and what was distinctive about Paul's letter style.
