# Historical and Cultural Context for Joshua

## Historical Framework

The Book of Joshua describes the Israelites' conquest and settlement of Canaan under the leadership of Joshua, Moses' successor. Traditionally, the book has been attributed to Joshua himself, but most modern scholars believe it was compiled over a longer period. Core material may date from early Israelite times, while the final editing likely occurred during the Deuteronomic reform movement in the time of King Josiah (ca. 620 B.C.) or during the Babylonian exile (586–539 B.C.).

The events described in the book are traditionally placed around 1400 B.C. (early dating) or 1250–1200 B.C. (late dating), depending on how one interprets biblical chronology and archaeological findings. This was a period of great upheaval throughout the Middle East, often called the "Late Bronze Age collapse," in which major empires such as the Hittites collapsed and Egypt lost control over Canaan.

Canaan at this time was a politically fragmented region with many small city-states, each ruled by a local king. These city-states had previously been under Egyptian dominance, but Egypt's power had weakened. This power vacuum created opportunities for new groups to establish themselves in the region.

## Literary Context

The Book of Joshua belongs to the genre of historiographical narrative with theological interpretation. It combines conquest accounts (chapters 1–12), detailed lists of land allocations (chapters 13–21), and farewell speeches (chapters 22–24). The book is characterized by Deuteronomic theology, in which obedience to God leads to blessing and victory, while disobedience leads to defeat.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), Joshua is the first of "the Former Prophets" (Neviim Rishonim), which includes Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings. Together these form the Deuteronomic history that spans from Israel's entry into the land to the fall of Jerusalem. In Christian tradition, Joshua is counted among the historical books. The book serves as a bridge between the Pentateuch and the subsequent Israelite history, showing the fulfillment of the promises given to the patriarchs regarding the land.

## Cultural Background

Canaanite society was urbanized around fortified cities with surrounding agricultural areas. Social stratification was clear, with kings, priests, artisans, and farmers. The Israelites who entered the land had a tribal structure with councils of elders and charismatic leaders. The transition from a semi-nomadic to a settled farming society was a gradual process that created cultural and religious tensions.

Canaanite religion was polytheistic, with El as the supreme god and Baal as the active storm and fertility god. Asherah, Astarte, and other deities were worshiped at local shrines (high places). Fertility rituals and sacrificial practices, including in some cases child sacrifice, were central. The strict prohibitions in Joshua against contact with Canaanite religion reflect concerns about syncretism.

Relations with neighboring cultures were complex. The Israelites adopted Canaanite technology, architecture, and to some extent language (Hebrew is closely related to Canaanite). At the same time, they defined themselves in opposition to Canaanite religion. The groups mentioned in the book (Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, Jebusites) represent the ethnic and cultural mosaic of the region.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Merneptah Stele (ca. 1208 B.C.)**
Found in Thebes, Egypt. This is the earliest known extra-biblical reference to Israel as a people in Canaan. The inscription states that "Israel is laid waste, its seed is no more," showing that Israel existed as a recognizable group in Canaan at this time.

**Amarna Letters (ca. 1350–1330 B.C.)**
Found in El-Amarna, Egypt. This collection of diplomatic correspondence between Canaanite city-state kings and the pharaoh shows the political fragmentation in Canaan and mentions "Habiru" – a socially marginalized group that some scholars have linked to the Hebrews.

**Archaeological Excavations at Jericho (Tell es-Sultan)**
Excavations show that the city had massive walls, but the dating of its destruction is disputed. Kathleen Kenyon's excavations (1950s) dated the destruction to ca. 1550 B.C., while Bryant Wood has argued for a later date closer to the biblical period.

**Hazor (Tell el-Qedah)**
Excavations led by Yigael Yadin and later Amnon Ben-Tor have uncovered a massive Bronze Age settlement that was destroyed by fire around 1230 B.C. This corresponds with Joshua's conquest described in chapter 11 and makes Hazor one of the strongest archaeological arguments for a historical conquest.

**Iron Age Village Settlements in the Highlands**
From about 1200 B.C., hundreds of new, small villages appeared in the central highlands. These have characteristic features such as four-room houses and an absence of pig bones, and they likely represent the emergence of early Israelite society, although their exact origin remains debated.
