# The Gospel of Mark – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

The Gospel of Mark is traditionally regarded as the oldest of the four Gospels, likely written between 65 and 70 CE. The church father Papias (c. 130 CE) identified the author as John Mark, Peter's interpreter in Rome, who recorded Peter's testimony about Jesus. Modern scholarship generally supports that the Gospel was written before the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, since the descriptions of the temple's fall in chapter 13 lack details that would have been natural after the event.

The Gospel describes Jesus' ministry during the period c. 27–30 CE, that is, about 35–40 years before it was written down. Jesus worked in Galilee and Judea under the rule of Emperor Tiberius (14–37 CE) and the unpopular prefect Pontius Pilate's administration (26–36 CE). This was a time of increasing political unrest and messianic expectations among the Jews.

Palestine was under Roman occupation, formally ruled through client kings such as Herod Antipas in Galilee and Roman prefects in Judea. Religiously, the temple cult in Jerusalem dominated, while the Pharisees represented a popular movement of piety. The Sadducees controlled the temple, whereas the Essenes lived in isolation. Zealot movements sought armed resistance against Rome. This religious and political tension forms the backdrop for Jesus' ministry.

## Literary Context

The Gospel of Mark represents a unique literary genre – the gospel – which combines biographical elements with theological proclamation. The style is direct and dramatic, characterized by frequent use of "immediately" (Greek: εὐθύς) which drives the action forward. The author writes in simple Greek with Semitic linguistic features, suggesting a Jewish background. The Gospel contains so-called "sandwich structures" where one story is framed by another to illuminate both.

Mark stands first among the Synoptic Gospels and was likely used as a source for both Matthew and Luke. The Gospel is structured geographically and theologically: Jesus' ministry in Galilee (chaps. 1–9), the journey toward Jerusalem (chap. 10), and the passion narrative in Jerusalem (chaps. 11–16). Peter's confession in chapter 8 constitutes a turning point where the focus shifts from Jesus' power to his suffering. The short, original ending (16:8) with the frightened women underscores the mystery and awe surrounding the resurrection.

## Cultural Background

Palestinian society at the time of Jesus was hierarchically organized. At the top stood the Roman occupying power and their local collaborators – Herodian princes, high priests, and wealthy landowning families. Below these came merchants, artisans, and fishermen such as the Galilean disciples. At the bottom were day laborers, the poor, slaves, and "unclean" groups such as tax collectors and lepers. Jesus moved strikingly freely between these layers, which created both fascination and offense.

Religious life centered on the temple in Jerusalem, synagogues in the villages, and the home. Jews practiced circumcision, Sabbath observance, purity laws, and dietary regulations as identity markers. Scribes discussed the application of the Torah, and the expectation of a Messiah – an anointed king or priest – was widespread, though with varying conceptions. Exorcisms and healings were well-known phenomena in both Jewish and Greco-Roman contexts.

Palestine lay at the intersection of Jewish, Greek, and Roman culture. Greek was the language of trade and lingua franca, while Aramaic was the vernacular. Hellenistic cities such as the Decapolis and coastal towns represented Greco-Roman culture with theaters, baths, and temples for emperor worship and Greek gods. Mark explains Jewish customs to his readers (e.g., 7:3–4), suggesting a non-Jewish audience, probably in Rome.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Peter's House in Capernaum (1st century CE)**
Found during excavations in Capernaum by the Sea of Galilee. Archaeologists have uncovered a house that was converted into a gathering place already in the first century, with Christian graffiti from later periods. This may be the house mentioned in Mark 1:29–34 and confirms Capernaum's role as the center of Jesus' Galilean ministry.

**The Pilate Inscription (26–36 CE)**
Found in Caesarea Maritima in 1961. A stone inscription mentioning "Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judea" – the only contemporary archaeological testimony about the man who condemned Jesus. The inscription confirms his historical existence and correct title.

**Josephus' Writings (c. 93–94 CE)**
The Jewish historian Flavius Josephus wrote "Jewish Antiquities" and "The Jewish War". His works describe John the Baptist, Herod Antipas, the high priests, and the political situation in detail, providing invaluable background for the Gospel narratives.

**Temple Warning Inscription (before 70 CE)**
Found in Jerusalem, now in Istanbul. A Greek inscription warning non-Jews against entering the inner courts of the temple under penalty of death. This illustrates the strict boundary between Jews and Gentiles that Jesus and the early church challenged.

**Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 250 BCE – 68 CE)**
Found at Qumran from 1947. These texts from a Jewish community reveal the diversity of Jewish belief and practice at the time of Jesus, including messianic expectations, apocalyptic ideas, and scriptural interpretation that illuminate New Testament language and thought.
