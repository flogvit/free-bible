# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus

## Historical Framework

The book of Exodus has traditionally been attributed to Moses as its author, a view that has been prominent in both Jewish and Christian tradition. Modern biblical scholarship often operates with the Documentary Hypothesis, which proposes that the Pentateuch (the five books of Moses) is composed of several sources: the Yahwist (J), the Elohist (E), the Priestly source (P), and the Deuteronomist (D). These sources are typically dated from the 10th to the 5th century BCE, with final editing occurring during the Persian period (500–400 BCE). At the same time, many scholars argue that the book contains much older oral traditions and core material.

The book describes events traditionally dated to either the 15th or 13th century BCE—the period when the Israelites are said to have been enslaved in Egypt and later wandered in the wilderness under Moses' leadership. The most widely accepted dating among scholars who accept a historical core places the Exodus from Egypt around 1250 BCE, during the reign of Pharaoh Ramses II or his successor Merneptah. Others argue for an earlier date around 1446 BCE.

The political situation in the second millennium BCE was characterized by Egyptian dominance in the Middle East. Egypt controlled large parts of Canaan and the Sinai Peninsula and employed Semitic slaves in construction projects. Religiously, Egypt was a polytheistic society with the pharaoh as a divine figure, providing an important backdrop for the conflicts in Exodus.

## Literary Context

The book of Exodus represents a complex literary composition that combines several genres: historical narrative (the story of Moses and the Exodus from Egypt), legal texts (the Ten Commandments and covenant laws), cultic regulations (instructions for the tabernacle), and poetry (the Song of Moses in chapter 15). The book serves as a foundational narrative about Israel's birth as a nation and the establishment of its covenant relationship with God.

In the biblical canon, Exodus is the second of the five books of Moses (the Torah/Pentateuch) and continues directly from the conclusion of Genesis. The book can be structured into three main parts: Deliverance from Egypt (chapters 1–18), the Covenant at Sinai with legislation (chapters 19–24), and the instructions for and construction of the tabernacle (chapters 25–40). The Hebrew title "Shemot" ("Names") comes from the book's first word, while "Exodus" (from Greek) means "departure."

## Cultural Background

The social structure of ancient Egypt was strictly hierarchical, with the pharaoh at the top as both political and religious leader. Semitic ethnic groups, including Hebrews, were present in Egypt and could attain various social positions—from slaves to high-ranking officials (such as Joseph in Genesis). Slave labor was common for major construction projects, and Egyptian sources confirm the use of Semitic workers in building cities in the Nile Delta.

The religious practice in Exodus shows a transition from the patriarchal family worship to an organized cultic system. The Israelites' worldview is shaped through divine revelation at the burning bush and at Sinai, where Yahweh (YHWH) is presented as the one true God in contrast to Egypt's many gods. The plagues can be understood as God's judgment against Egyptian deities—for example, darkness struck the sun god Ra, and the turning of the Nile into blood struck the river as a life-giving deity.

The relationship with surrounding cultures is central to Exodus. Middle Eastern legal traditions (such as the Laws of Hammurabi) form parallels to Israelite legislation, although there are significant differences in theological foundation. The design of the tabernacle has similarities with Egyptian and Canaanite sanctuaries but is theologized within a distinct Israelite framework. The golden calf episode (chapter 32) illustrates the tension between loyalty to Yahweh and influence from surrounding fertility religions.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Merneptah Stele (ca. 1208 BCE)**
Found in Pharaoh Merneptah's mortuary temple in Thebes, Egypt. This is the earliest undisputed extra-biblical reference to Israel, where Pharaoh Merneptah boasts of having "destroyed Israel." The stele confirms that a group called Israel existed in Canaan at the end of the 1200s BCE.

**Papyrus Anastasi (13th century BCE)**
Egyptian administrative documents mentioning "Apiru" (possibly related to "Hebrew") as workers in Egypt, and describing slaves who fled into the wilderness. This supports the general picture of Semitic laborers in Egypt.

**Tell el-Dab'a / Avaris (ca. 1800–1550 BCE)**
Archaeological excavations in the eastern Nile Delta have revealed a significant Semitic settlement with distinctive burial customs and pottery that differ from Egyptian culture. Some scholars link this to the Israelites' stay in Goshen.

**Sinai Inscriptions, Serabit el-Khadim (ca. 1800–1500 BCE)**
Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions found in Egyptian mines on the Sinai Peninsula show early alphabetic writing used by Semitic workers. These demonstrate a Semitic presence in the Sinai region and an early writing culture among Semitic groups.

**Brooklyn Papyrus (ca. 1740 BCE)**
An Egyptian document listing slaves with Semitic names, including names resembling biblical ones (such as Shiphrah, cf. Exodus 1:15). This confirms the presence of Semitic slaves in Egypt and the authenticity of naming traditions in Exodus.
