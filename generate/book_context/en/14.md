# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles

## Historical Framework

Second Chronicles is traditionally attributed to Ezra, and Jewish tradition links the book to his work as a scribe after the exile. Modern scholarship places the final editing of the book in the Persian period, likely between 400–300 BCE, although it draws on older sources. The Chronicler (as the author is often called) used the Books of Kings as the main source but supplemented them with material from archives, genealogies, and prophetic writings that are no longer preserved.

The book covers Israel's history from Solomon's reign (around 970 BCE) to Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE and Cyrus's decree in 538 BCE, which allowed the Jews to return. This period of over 400 years includes the united kingdom under Solomon, the division of the kingdom, and the history of the southern kingdom of Judah up to the exile. The Chronicler focuses almost exclusively on Judah and the Davidic dynasty, while largely ignoring the northern kingdom of Israel.

The political situation during the period described in the book was marked by shifting great powers: Egypt, Assyria, and Babylon dominated the Middle East. Religiously, the period was a struggle between the worship of Yahweh and the influence of Canaanite religion and other foreign cults. When the book was written, the Jewish people lived under Persian rule without their own king, and the temple and priesthood had become the religious and social center.

## Literary Context

Second Chronicles belongs to the genre of theological historiography. The Chronicler does not merely retell history but interprets it from a clear theological perspective: faithfulness to Yahweh and his temple leads to blessing, while apostasy leads to judgment. The style is characterized by long speeches, prayers, and detailed descriptions of worship and temple rituals. The book also contains unique material on religious reforms and the service of the Levites that is not found in the Books of Kings.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), the Chronicles form one book called "Divrei HaYamim" (The Words of the Days/Chronicles) and stand at the very end of the Writings (Ketuvim). In the Christian canon, 2 Chronicles follows 1 Chronicles and precedes Ezra, to which it is closely related—the last verses of 2 Chronicles are almost repeated word for word in the beginning of Ezra. The book is structured around the kings of Judah, with special emphasis on the reforming kings Solomon, Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah, and Josiah.

## Cultural Background

Society in ancient Judah was organized around family, tribe, and village, with the king as the supreme political and military leader. Jerusalem functioned as both the political and religious capital, and the temple was the hub of the nation's religious life. The priests and Levites made up a significant portion of the population and were responsible for sacrifices, teaching the law, and temple music. After the exile, when the book was compiled, the importance of the priesthood and the temple had increased even further.

Religious practice in the kingdom of Judah centered on the temple in Jerusalem, with its daily sacrifices, festival celebrations, and pilgrimages. The Chronicler places great emphasis on proper worship performed by the right people (priests and Levites) in the right place (the temple). The worldview was theocentric: Yahweh directed the course of history, and the nation's fate was tied to its obedience to the covenant. The prophets played an important role as messengers of God's will to kings and people.

The relationship with surrounding cultures was complex. Judah was a small state surrounded by great powers and neighboring peoples with other religions. The book shows constant temptations to form political alliances and to introduce foreign cults. Assyrian and Babylonian religious influence was particularly strong in certain periods. The Chronicler's message to the post-exilic community was clear: Israel's identity and future lay in faithfulness to Yahweh and purity in temple worship, not in political power.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Sennacherib's Prism (Taylor Prism)** – around 691 BCE, found in Nineveh. This Assyrian cuneiform text describes Sennacherib's campaign against Judah and mentions how he besieged Jerusalem under King Hezekiah, confirming the account in 2 Chronicles 32. The prism states that Sennacherib shut up Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage".

**Siloam Inscription** – around 701 BCE, found in Hezekiah's Tunnel in Jerusalem. This Hebrew inscription describes the completion of the water tunnel mentioned in 2 Chronicles 32:30 and confirms Hezekiah's engineering work to secure the water supply before the Assyrian siege.

**Lachish Letters** – around 588–586 BCE, found at Lachish (Tell ed-Duweir). These ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) are military correspondence from just before the fall of Jerusalem and provide a vivid glimpse into the desperate situation described in 2 Chronicles 36.

**Babylonian Chronicles** – around 605–594 BCE, found in Babylon. These cuneiform texts document Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns against Judah and the conquest of Jerusalem in 597 BCE, as mentioned in 2 Chronicles 36. They confirm the biblical chronology for this period.

**Cyrus Cylinder** – around 539 BCE, found in Babylon. This Persian inscription describes Cyrus's policy of allowing deported peoples to return and rebuild their sanctuaries, which forms the background for the decree in 2 Chronicles 36:22–23.
