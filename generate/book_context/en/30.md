# Amos – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

Amos was a prophet from Tekoa in Judah, who ministered in the Northern Kingdom of Israel during the reign of King Jeroboam II (ca. 786–746 B.C.). The book is usually dated to around 760–750 B.C., a period that marked the height of Israel’s material prosperity and territorial expansion. Amos was not a professional prophet, but a shepherd and tender of sycamore-fig trees who was called by God to prophesy against Israel (Amos 7:14–15).

This period was characterized by relative peace and stability. Assyria was temporarily weakened, allowing both Israel and Judah to expand their borders. Jeroboam II restored Israel’s boundaries "from Lebo-Hamat to the Sea of the Arabah" (2 Kings 14:25). However, economic growth led to increasing social inequality, corruption in the judicial system, and oppression of the poor—conditions that Amos strongly condemned.

Religiously, the period was marked by syncretism. The worship of Yahweh was mixed with Canaanite fertility cults, especially Baal worship. The sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan, established by Jeroboam I, remained active cult sites featuring golden calves. Amos sharply criticized this religious practice, arguing that it lacked genuine reverence for God and ethical substance.

## Literary Context

Amos belongs to the prophetic literature and is considered one of the first "writing prophets"—prophets whose messages were recorded in separate books. The book contains several literary genres: oracles of judgment against foreign nations (chapters 1–2), accusations and pronouncements of judgment against Israel (chapters 3–6), visions (chapters 7–9), and a concluding message of salvation (9:11–15). The language is poetic and powerful, employing rhetorical questions, imagery drawn from nature, and the characteristic "woe oracle."

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanak), Amos stands among the Twelve Minor Prophets (Tre Asar). In the Christian canon, the book is also placed among the Minor Prophets. The structure of the book moves from judgment against the nations, through Israel’s sin and punishment, to a final vision of restoration. This movement from judgment to hope is typical of prophetic literature.

## Cultural Background

Society in Amos’ time was marked by growing urbanization and social stratification. A wealthy upper class lived in luxury with "ivory houses" and held banquets featuring wine and music (Amos 3:15; 6:4–6), while the poor were exploited, sold into slavery for trivial debts, and denied justice in the courts. Merchants used false scales and weights, and farmers were crushed by debt and taxes. This social injustice is the central theme of Amos’ preaching.

Religious practice centered on the sanctuaries, where people brought offerings, celebrated festivals, and made pilgrimages. Amos did not criticize the sacrificial system itself, but rather its performance without ethical living. "I hate, I despise your feasts," says the Lord through Amos (5:21). The prophet emphasized that true worship requires justice and righteousness in daily life.

Israel was surrounded by powerful nations and small states such as Aram-Damascus, Philistia, Phoenicia, Edom, Moab, and Ammon—all mentioned in the oracles of judgment in chapters 1–2. These nations had similar religious practices involving sacrificial cults and fertility rituals. Assyria, which would become God’s instrument for judging Israel, was regaining its strength and would soon threaten the entire region.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Samaria Ostraca (ca. 784–783 B.C.)**  
Found in Samaria, the capital of Israel. Sixty-three pottery shards with inscriptions documenting deliveries of wine and oil to the royal household. They illuminate the administrative system and the wealth that Amos criticized, as well as mention place names known from the book.

**The Ivory Collection from Samaria (8th century B.C.)**  
Found in the ruins of the royal palace at Samaria. Carved ivory plaques that decorated furniture and walls. These directly illustrate Amos’ reference to "ivory houses" (Amos 3:15) and the luxurious lifestyle of the upper class.

**The Tel Dan Stele (ca. 841 B.C.)**  
Found at Dan in northern Israel. An Aramaic inscription mentioning the "House of David," confirming the conflict between Israel and Aram-Damascus. It provides background for the regional tensions that characterized the period before Amos.

**Assyrian Royal Annals (7th century B.C.)**  
From Nineveh and other Assyrian cities. They document the campaigns of Tiglath-Pileser III and later kings against Israel and Damascus, confirming the fulfillment of Amos’ prophecies concerning exile and destruction.

**Tell el-Kheleifeh (Ezion-Geber)**  
Located on the Gulf of Aqaba. A trading center and port city from the Israelite period. It sheds light on international trade and economic activity in the region, providing context for Amos’ social critique.
