# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians

## Historical Framework

First Corinthians was written by the apostle Paul around AD 53–55, most likely from Ephesus during his third missionary journey. The letter is one of the so-called "undisputed" Pauline epistles, with broad scholarly consensus that Paul himself is the author. The letter mentions the co-sender Sosthenes, but its content and style are clearly Pauline. It refers to a previous letter Paul had sent (5:9), which has likely been lost. Some scholars have suggested that fragments of it may be found in 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1, though this remains debated.

Paul had founded the church in Corinth around AD 50–51 during his second missionary journey, when he lived in the city for a year and a half (Acts 18:1–18). When he wrote this letter, he had received troubling reports from "Chloe's people" about divisions within the church, as well as a letter from the Corinthians themselves with questions regarding practical and theological matters.

The political situation was marked by Roman dominance. Corinth was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia and had been rebuilt as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar in 44 BC after lying in ruins for a century. Under Emperor Claudius (AD 41–54) and later Nero (AD 54–68), the city enjoyed relative stability, which facilitated trade, travel, and the spread of new religious movements such as Christianity.

## Literary Context

First Corinthians belongs to the genre of epistolary literature, specifically the ancient Greek letter with elements of both deliberative and corrective rhetoric. Paul follows the classical letter structure with greeting, thanksgiving, main body, and conclusion, but adapts the form to his pastoral purpose. The letter is practically oriented and addresses concrete problems point by point, often introduced with the phrase "now concerning" (Greek: *peri de*), suggesting responses to questions from the church.

In the biblical canon, 1 Corinthians stands as the second of Paul's letters after Romans, arranged according to the principle of length. The letter can be roughly divided into two parts: the first addresses reported problems (divisions, immorality, lawsuits), while the second answers the church's questions about marriage, food offered to idols, worship gatherings, and the resurrection. Chapter 13 (the hymn to love), chapter 15 (on the resurrection), and 11:23–26 (the institution of the Lord's Supper) are counted among the most influential texts in Christian theology and liturgy.

## Cultural Background

Corinth was one of antiquity's most cosmopolitan cities, strategically located on the isthmus between the Peloponnese and the Greek mainland, with two harbors that made it a hub for trade between east and west. The population was highly diverse: Roman colonists, Greeks, Jews, freed slaves, and travelers from across the Mediterranean world. Society was strongly stratified with sharp distinctions between rich and poor, free persons and slaves—tensions reflected in the letter's discussion of communal meals and the use of spiritual gifts.

Religiously, Corinth was characterized by diversity. The city had temples for numerous Greco-Roman deities, including Apollo, Aphrodite, Asclepius, and the imperial cult. Mystery religions with initiation rites and wisdom teachings were popular. This background shapes several themes in the letter: the question of meat sacrificed to idols, sexual ethics in a city known for moral laxity, and fascination with wisdom and spiritual experiences that could resemble the ecstasy of mystery cults.

The Jewish diaspora in Corinth formed the core of the first Christian community, but soon many Gentile believers also joined. This mixture created tensions concerning identity, food, sexual ethics, and worship practices. The Corinthians' desire to combine their new faith with elements from their Greco-Roman background—whether regarding rhetoric, social conventions, or religious enthusiasm—is a red thread running through the entire letter.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Bema Tribunal in Corinth (dated to the 1st century AD)**
Discovered during excavations of the ancient agora in Corinth. This is the public speaker's platform where Paul was brought before the proconsul Gallio according to Acts 18:12–17. The find confirms Corinth's role as a provincial capital and provides a concrete anchor point for Paul's stay there.

**The Gallio Inscription from Delphi (dated to AD 51–52)**
Found in Delphi, Greece. This inscription mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and makes it possible to date Paul's stay in Corinth with relative precision. It is crucial for the chronology of Paul's life and thus also for dating 1 Corinthians.

**The Erastus Inscription (1st century AD)**
Found near the theater in Corinth. The inscription mentions an Erastus who funded the paving of a square in exchange for his office as "aedile" (city official). He may be identical with the Erastus Paulus mentions as "the city's treasurer" in Romans 16:23, written from Corinth. The find illustrates that some Christians belonged to the city's wealthy elite.

**The Synagogue Inscription from Corinth (probably 2nd–4th century AD, but testifies to earlier Jewish presence)**
Found in Corinth. A fragmentary inscription with the words "synagogue of the Hebrews" confirms Jewish presence in the city. Although the inscription is later, it supports the biblical text's description of a Jewish community that Paul first preached to.

**The Asclepius Sanctuary in Corinth (active from the 4th century BC)**
Found during excavations north of the agora. The temple for the healing god Asclepius had dining halls where cult meals were held. This sheds light on Paul's discussion about eating in idol temples (chapters 8–10) and shows how integrated religious practice was within social contexts.
