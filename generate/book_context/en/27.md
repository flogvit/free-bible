# Historical and Cultural Context for the Book of Daniel

## Historical Framework

The book of Daniel takes place during the Babylonian exile (605–539 B.C.) and the early Persian period, with Daniel as a young Jewish nobleman taken captive to Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar. The book presents itself as written by Daniel, but there are differing views on authorship and dating. Traditionally, Jewish and Christian tradition has attributed the book to the prophet Daniel himself in the 6th century B.C. Many modern scholars date the final editing to the 2nd century B.C., during the Maccabean period, based on linguistic and historical arguments, but conservative scholars argue for an early dating based on the book's own claims and prophetic reliability.

The historical background spans several empires: The Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar (605–562 B.C.), the transition to the Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great (539 B.C.), and – in the prophetic visions – future kingdoms including the Greek Empire under Alexander the Great and its successor states. The Jews lived as a people in exile, deprived of their temple and homeland, but with some degree of self-governance within Babylonian society.

The religious situation was marked by tension between loyalty to the God of Israel and pressure to adapt to Babylonian/Persian religion. The Babylonians practiced a polytheistic religion with Marduk as the chief deity, and the king was seen as the representative of the gods on earth. For the Jewish exiles, preserving monotheistic faith and religious identity became an existential struggle.

## Literary Context

The book of Daniel is a unique blend of court narratives (chapters 1–6) and apocalyptic literature (chapters 7–12). The court narratives tell of Daniel and his friends at the Babylonian court, with dramatic trials and divine deliverances. The apocalyptic visions contain symbolic revelations about world empires, the end times, and resurrection. The book is also linguistically divided: Daniel 1:1–2:4a and 8:1–12:13 are written in Hebrew, while Daniel 2:4b–7:28 is in Aramaic.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), Daniel is placed among the "Writings" (Ketuvim), not among the prophets. In Christian Bibles, the book is counted as one of the Major Prophets. The structure follows a pattern where the court narratives demonstrate God's sovereignty over pagan kings, while the visions reveal God's plan for the course of history and his people's ultimate victory.

## Cultural Background

Babylonian society was highly developed with advanced bureaucracy, science, and culture. Babylon was the world's largest city with impressive architecture, including the Ishtar Gate and the ziggurat Etemenanki. The scholars ("Chaldeans") were known for astronomy, astrology, dream interpretation, and magic. Daniel and his friends were trained in this tradition but maintained their Jewish identity through dietary laws and prayer practices.

Religious practice in Babylon included extensive temple rituals, sacrifices, and festivals honoring the gods. The king had a central religious role, especially during the New Year festival (Akitu). For the Jews, prayer toward Jerusalem, observance of the Sabbath, and food regulations became important identity markers. Belief in one God who governed history stood in sharp contrast to Babylonian polytheism and astrology.

The relationship between the Jews and surrounding cultures was complex. On one hand, Jews like Daniel participated in the empire's administration and learned its wisdom. On the other hand, they faced direct conflicts when loyalty to God clashed with loyalty to the king, as in the stories of the fiery furnace and the lions' den. This tension between adaptation and resistance is a recurring theme throughout the book.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle (605–594 B.C.)**
Found in Babylon, now in the British Museum. This Babylonian cuneiform tablet confirms Nebuchadnezzar's campaign against Judah and the conquest of Jerusalem in 597 B.C., which aligns with the book's historical framework.

**Cyrus Cylinder (539 B.C.)**
Found in Babylon in 1879, now in the British Museum. It describes Cyrus's conquest of Babylon and his policy of allowing deported peoples to return home and rebuild their temples. This illuminates the political transition in Daniel 5–6 and the context for the Jews' return from exile.

**Nabonidus Chronicle (ca. 540–530 B.C.)**
A Babylonian cuneiform text describing Babylon's fall to the Persians. It provides historical background for the events in Daniel 5, where Belshazzar is mentioned.

**Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242) from Qumran (ca. 1st century B.C.)**
Found in the Qumran caves. An Aramaic text telling about King Nabonidus's illness and healing through a Jewish exorcist. This shows familiarity with traditions similar to Daniel 4 and indicates early circulation of the book.

**Dead Sea Scrolls with Daniel Texts (ca. 125 B.C. and later)**
Found in Qumran. Eight different Daniel manuscripts were discovered, demonstrating the book's authority and widespread use in late Second Temple Judaism and confirming that the text was established before the end of the Maccabean period.
