# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel

## Historical Framework

The book of 1 Samuel covers a crucial transitional period in Israel's history, from the era of the judges to the united monarchy, approximately 1100–1010 B.C. The book describes the shift from a loose tribal society led by charismatic leaders (judges) to a centralized kingdom. This period was marked by external pressure, particularly from the Philistines, who held technological superiority through iron weapons and threatened Israel's existence as a people.

The authorship of the book is traditionally attributed to the prophet Samuel himself, yet the text mentions events after his death. Jewish tradition (Talmud, Bava Batra 14b–15a) suggests that Samuel wrote the beginning, while the prophets Gad and Nathan completed the work. Modern scholarship views the book as the result of a lengthy editorial process in which various sources and traditions were gathered and edited, likely reaching its final form during or after the Babylonian exile (600–500 B.C.). The book bears the imprint of Deuteronomistic historiography theology, where Israel's fate is directly linked to the obedience of the people and their kings toward God.

The political situation in the region was changing. The great empires Egypt (after the fall of the New Kingdom around 1070 B.C.) and Assyria (in a decline period until Neo-Assyrian expansion) were temporarily weakened, creating a power vacuum where smaller peoples such as Israel, the Philistines, Moabites, and Ammonites competed for dominance. Religiously, the period was characterized by tension between Yahweh worship and Canaanite fertility cults, as well as the establishment of more permanent cult centers like Shiloh.

## Literary Context

The book of 1 Samuel belongs to narrative historiography and combines several literary genres: birth narratives, call stories, court chronicles, hero tales, and poetic passages such as Hannah's song. The book is characterized by vivid character portrayals and dramatic tension, especially in the presentation of the relationship between Saul and David. Hebrew storytelling artistry is evident in the use of repetition, contrasts, and dialogue to highlight theological points.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), the book belongs to "the Former Prophets" (Nevi'im rishonim), together with Joshua, Judges, 2 Samuel, and Kings. Originally, 1 and 2 Samuel formed one book; the division stems from the Septuagint, the Greek translation. The book serves as a bridge between the chaotic period of the judges ("there was no king in Israel") and the establishment of the Davidic dynasty. Structurally, the book can be divided into three main parts: Samuel's ministry (chapters 1–7), Saul's kingdom (chapters 8–15), and David's rise to the throne (chapters 16–31).

## Cultural Background

The society described is primarily agrarian, based on agriculture and livestock herding organized within extended families (bet av) and clans within the twelve tribes. Honor and shame were central values, and kinship defined social obligations. The book reflects the transition from a decentralized tribal society to state formation with a standing army, taxation, and centralized administration. This development met resistance, which is reflected in the text's ambivalence toward kingship (see Samuel's warning in chapter 8).

Religious practice centered on sacrifices at sanctuaries such as Shiloh, Gilgal, Mizpah, and Nob. The priests (here the family of Eli, later Abiathar) administered the sanctuaries and the ark of the covenant. The role of prophets was evolving; Samuel represents a new type of prophet who combined functions as judge, priest, and political advisor. The book also shows phenomena such as prophetic ecstasy, the use of Urim and Thummim to seek God's will, and Nazirite vows. The Spirit's role in equipping leaders is a recurring theme.

Relations with surrounding peoples were marked by both conflict and cultural exchange. The Philistines, a Sea People of Aegean origin, represented the greatest military threat and had established themselves in city-states along the coast. Their iron technology and military organization gave them significant advantages. The Israelites gradually adopted elements from Canaanite culture, including kingship as an institution, while prophets warned against religious syncretism.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Khirbet Qeiyafa (ca. 1020–980 B.C.)**
A fortified city in the Shephelah (Judean lowlands), systematically excavated since 2007 under the direction of Yosef Garfinkel. The site contains two gates, casemate walls, and cult objects without figurative representation. The find may support the existence of an organized society in Judah during David's time and sheds light on the conflict with the Philistines in the Elah Valley, where according to tradition David fought Goliath.

**Tell es-Safi (Gath) – excavations ongoing since 1996**
Identified as the Philistine city of Gath, home of Goliath and later a place of refuge for David. The excavations have revealed a massive city wall, Philistine pottery, and inscriptions with names resembling the name form "Goliath" (non-Semitic names of possible Aegean origin). This confirms the city's significance and that such non-Semitic names were in use among the Philistines during this period.

**Philistine Pottery and Iron Smelting (1200–1000 B.C.)**
Found at several excavations in the Philistine coastal area (Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron). The pottery shows Aegean influence, while remains of iron workshops confirm 1 Sam 13:19–22's description of a Philistine monopoly on iron.

**Tel Dan Stele (ca. 840 B.C.)**
Found in 1993–94 in northern Israel. An Aramaic inscription mentioning "the house of David" (byt dwd) – the oldest extra-biblical testimony to David. Confirms that David was remembered as the founder of a dynasty within 150 years after his death.

**Shiloh Excavations (Danish excavations 1926–32, Israeli excavations 1981–84)**
At Khirbet Seilun in the Samarian hills. Archaeologists found traces of destruction from around 1050 B.C., correlating with the account of the ark's capture by the Philistines and the downfall of the sanctuary (see Jer 7:12–14).
