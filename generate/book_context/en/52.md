# 1 Thessalonians – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

First Thessalonians is regarded by most scholars as the earliest writing in the New Testament, likely composed around AD 50–51. Paul wrote this letter from Corinth during his second missionary journey, together with his coworkers Silvanus (Silas) and Timothy. The letter is widely accepted as authentically Pauline and offers a unique glimpse into the earliest Christian preaching and church life, only about twenty years after Jesus’ death and resurrection.

Paul had founded the church in Thessalonica shortly before but was forced to leave the city abruptly due to opposition from Jewish groups who stirred up a mob against him (Acts 17:1–10). The book of Acts reports that he preached in the synagogue for three Sabbaths before unrest broke out. The letter was written after Timothy had returned to Paul with good news about the church’s faith and endurance.

The political situation in the Roman Empire was relatively stable under Emperor Claudius (AD 41–54). Thessalonica was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia and an important trading city along the Via Egnatia, the main road connecting Rome with the East. The city held the status of a "free city" with certain self-governing rights and its own politarchs (city council members). The Jewish community in the city was large enough to have a synagogue, and there were also many "God-fearing" Gentiles who sympathized with Judaism.

## Literary Context

First Thessalonians follows the typical form of ancient Greek letters but with distinctive Christian adaptations. The letter contains an extended section of thanksgiving that flows into personal memories and exhortations, followed by teaching on the resurrection of the dead and the day of the Lord. The style is warm, pastoral, and encouraging, marked by Paul’s deep care for his spiritual children. The letter lacks the intense theological debates found in later letters such as Romans and Galatians.

The letter is placed among Paul’s letters in the New Testament, traditionally arranged by length after Romans. Its structure can be divided into three main parts: thanksgiving and a review of the church’s founding (chapters 1–3), exhortations to holy living (4:1–12), and teaching on the resurrection of the dead and the Lord’s return (4:13–5:11), concluding with practical exhortations and greetings (5:12–28).

## Cultural Background

Thessalonica was a cosmopolitan port city with perhaps 100,000 inhabitants, characterized by trade, craftsmanship, and cultural diversity. Society was hierarchically organized, with a small elite of wealthy citizens, a significant group of artisans and merchants, and many slaves. Paul himself worked as a tentmaker during his stay (1 Thess 2:9) and encouraged church members to "work with their own hands" (4:11). Most Christians in Thessalonica appear to have been Gentiles who had turned away from idol worship (1:9).

The city was a religious center with temples dedicated to numerous Greco-Roman deities, including Zeus, Dionysus, and the Egyptian goddess Isis. The imperial cult was also prominent, and Thessalonica had a temple dedicated to Rome and Augustus. Mystery religions with their initiation rites and promises of life after death were popular. This religious context made it challenging for new Christians to break with previous social and religious obligations.

The relationship between the small Christian group and the surrounding society was tense. The letter repeatedly mentions "trouble" and "suffering" experienced by the church (1:6; 2:14; 3:3–4). According to Acts, the accusation against the Christians was that they acted "against Caesar’s decrees, saying that another is king, namely Jesus" (Acts 17:7) – a serious political charge in a city proud of its loyalty to Rome.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Politarch Inscriptions from Thessalonica (1st–2nd century AD)**  
Several inscriptions found in and around Thessalonica use the title "politarch" for the city’s rulers. This confirms Luke’s accuracy in Acts 17:6, where he uses precisely this unusual term. The most famous inscription was on the Vardar Gate (now in the British Museum).

**Via Egnatia – The Roman Main Road**  
Remains of this important road connecting Rome with the East are preserved in several places in Greece. The road passed through Thessalonica and explains the city’s strategic importance for trade and communication – and thus also for Paul’s missionary work.

**Serapis Temple in Thessalonica (3rd century BC onward)**  
Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of shrines dedicated to Egyptian deities in the city. This confirms the religious pluralism that early Christians had to navigate, as well as the context for Paul’s statement that they "turned to God from idols" (1:9).

**The Gallio Inscription from Delphi (AD 52)**  
This inscription, found in Delphi in Greece, mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and can be dated to AD 51–52. Since Acts 18:12 places Paul in Corinth during Gallio’s term, this provides a firm anchor for dating Paul’s stay in Corinth – and thus also First Thessalonians to around AD 50–51.

**Synagogue Inscriptions from Macedonia**  
Several inscriptions testify to Jewish presence in Macedonian cities during this period. They confirm the picture of an active Jewish diaspora community with synagogues that served as starting points for Paul’s preaching, as described in Acts.
