# Acts – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

Acts was likely written between 70 and 90 AD, although some scholars argue for an earlier date around 62 AD, since the book ends abruptly with Paul under house arrest in Rome without mentioning his further fate. Traditionally attributed to Luke, a physician and companion of Paul (Colossians 4:14), it forms the second part of a two-volume work, with the Gospel of Luke as the first. The "we-passages" in the book (chapters 16, 20–21, 27–28) suggest that the author was an eyewitness to some of the events.

The book covers the period from Jesus' ascension (c. 30–33 AD) to Paul's arrival in Rome (c. 60–62 AD), spanning approximately three decades of the church's earliest history. This period was marked by relative political stability under the Roman Empire, known as the Pax Romana. Judea was an unrestful Roman province with varying forms of governance—from Roman prefects such as Pontius Pilate to brief periods of Jewish client kingship under Herod Agrippa I (41–44 AD).

The religious situation was complex. Judaism was diverse, with Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and Zealots representing different movements. The temple in Jerusalem remained the center of Jewish worship and identity. During this period, Christianity gradually came to be seen as more than a Jewish sect, especially after the Council of Jerusalem (chapter 15), which opened full participation for Gentiles without requiring circumcision.

## Literary Context

Acts belongs to the genre of historiography in Hellenistic style, comparable to the works of Greek historians such as Polybius and Jewish authors like Josephus. The book contains speeches that make up about one-third of the text, travel narratives, miracles, courtroom scenes, and dramatic stories. Luke writes in refined Greek and addresses "Theophilus" (1:1), either a specific person or a symbolic name meaning "one who loves God."

The book serves as a bridge between the Gospels and the epistolary literature of the New Testament. It explains how the message about Jesus spread "from Jerusalem, throughout Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth" (1:8), and provides historical background for the churches Paul wrote to. Structurally, the book follows a geographical expansion: Jerusalem (chapters 1–7), Judea and Samaria (chapters 8–12), and the mission to Rome (chapters 13–28). Peter dominates the first half, while Paul dominates the second.

## Cultural Background

The Roman Empire at this time was a multi-ethnic society with Greek as the common language (koine) in the eastern provinces. Cities had developed infrastructure including roads, harbors, and public spaces that enabled rapid travel and communication. Society was hierarchical, with sharp distinctions between Roman citizens and non-citizens, free people and slaves, men and women. Paul's Roman citizenship (22:25–29) granted him legal rights that were crucial for his ministry.

Religiously, the world was characterized by syncretism. Alongside emperor worship existed Greco-Roman gods, mystery religions, philosophical schools such as the Stoics and Epicureans, as well as Jewish diaspora communities with their synagogues. Synagogues often served as Paul's starting point for preaching and also attracted "God-fearers"—Gentiles who sympathized with Judaism without fully converting. These were often receptive to the Christian message.

The relationship between Jews and the surrounding Greco-Roman culture was complex. Jews enjoyed special privileges within the Roman Empire, including exemption from emperor worship and the right to practice their religion. Acts shows how the early church navigated between Jewish heritage and openness toward Gentiles, between loyalty to Roman authorities and faithfulness to Christ as Lord.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Gallio Inscription (51–52 AD)**
Found in Delphi, Greece. This inscription mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and provides a firm chronological anchor for Paul's stay in Corinth (Acts 18:12–17), helping to date his missionary journeys.

**The Works of Josephus (c. 75–94 AD)**
The Jewish historian Flavius Josephus wrote about many of the persons and events mentioned in Acts, including Herod Agrippa I, the high priests Annas and Caiaphas, and the famine during the reign of Emperor Claudius (Acts 11:28).

**The Sergius Paulus Inscription (1st century AD)**
Found on Cyprus and in Pisidian Antioch. Inscriptions confirm the existence of a Roman official by this name, corresponding to the proconsul Paul met on Cyprus (Acts 13:7).

**The Theater of Ephesus and the Temple of Artemis**
Excavations in Ephesus have uncovered the large theater that hosted the riot described in chapter 19, as well as remains of the Temple of Artemis, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, which was central to the cult threatened by Paul's preaching.

**Caesarea Maritima**
Excavations have revealed the Roman administrative capital of Judea, including Herod the Great's palace where Paul was held captive and appeared before Felix, Festus, and Agrippa II (chapters 23–26). An inscription mentioning Pontius Pilate was also found here.
