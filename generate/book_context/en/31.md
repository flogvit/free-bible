# Obadiah – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament and is usually dated to the period following the fall of Jerusalem in 586 B.C.E. Most scholars place the book between 586 and 550 B.C.E., although the question of dating remains debated. This dating is based on the prophecy’s strong focus on Edom’s betrayal of Judah during the Babylonians’ destruction of Jerusalem. The author’s name means "servant of the Lord," but beyond this, we know nothing certain about the prophet’s identity or background. Some scholars suggest an earlier date in the 800s B.C.E., based on parallels with Jeremiah 49 and Joel, but the majority view the text as a response to the catastrophe of 586 B.C.E.

The historical situation addressed by the book is Edom’s role during Jerusalem’s destruction. The Edomites, who according to biblical tradition descended from Esau (Jacob’s brother), exploited Judah’s disaster by plundering refugees, blocking escape routes, and seizing the city’s wealth. This was experienced as a deep betrayal by a brother nation. Judah was now under Babylonian domination, the temple lay in ruins, and large parts of the population had been taken into exile.

## Literary Context

Obadiah belongs to the genre of prophetic judgment oracles, specifically an "oracle against foreign nations." The book is structured as poetry with characteristic Hebrew parallelism and uses powerful images of humiliation and retribution. The style is compact and intense, featuring direct address to Edom and rhetorical questions that reinforce the message.

The book is placed among the twelve minor prophets (the Book of the Twelve) in the Hebrew canon. In the Masoretic Text, it follows Amos. The book has a two-part structure: verses 1–14 focus on judgment against Edom, while verses 15–21 expand the perspective to "the day of the Lord" and Israel’s future restoration.

## Cultural Background

Edom was a kingdom southeast of the Dead Sea, in the mountainous region that today lies in southern Jordan. The Edomites were known as skilled merchants who controlled important trade routes, including the incense route from Arabia. The capital city of Bozrah (modern Busayra) and the famous mountain town of Sela were strategically located in inaccessible terrain, giving the Edomites a sense of invulnerability that Obadiah directly confronts. The relationship between Israel/Judah and Edom was marked by centuries of rivalry and conflict.

The religious worldview in Obadiah reflects a strong belief that Yahweh judges all nations, not only Israel. The concept of "the day of the Lord" points to a future cosmic judgment where injustice will be repaid and God’s kingdom established. This concept was central to prophetic thinking and gave hope to a people in crisis.

The Edomites’ religion was polytheistic, with the god Qaus (Qos) as the supreme deity, known from inscriptions and personal names. Culturally, Edom stood close to Canaanite and Arab traditions. The biblical portrayal of Edom as "brother" to Israel (through the Esau tradition) intensified the experience of betrayal—this was not merely an enemy, but family.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**The Arad Ostraca (ca. 600 B.C.E.)** – Found in the fortress of Arad in the Negev, Israel. These pottery shards with inscriptions mention Edomite threats against Judah in the period before Jerusalem’s fall and confirm the tension between the nations.

**Excavations at Busayra (800–500 B.C.E.)** – Busayra (biblical Bozrah) in Jordan was Edom’s capital. The excavations reveal a prosperous city with monumental architecture that was abandoned around the 500s B.C.E., which corresponds to the prophecy’s judgment against Edom.

**Babylonian Chronicles (600s B.C.E.)** – Found in Babylon (modern Iraq). These cuneiform texts describe Nebuchadnezzar’s campaigns against Jerusalem and confirm the historical framework for Obadiah’s prophecy.

**Tell el-Kheleifeh (900–500 B.C.E.)** – Excavated near the Gulf of Aqaba in Jordan. This trading center shows Edomite commercial activity and their control over trade routes, illuminating Obadiah’s references to Edom’s wealth and arrogance.
