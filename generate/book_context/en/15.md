# Ezra – Historical and Cultural Context

## Historical Framework

The book of Ezra covers the period from the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 B.C.E. to Ezra's arrival in Jerusalem around 458 B.C.E. The book describes two main phases of the return from the Babylonian exile: the first under Zerubbabel (around 538 B.C.E.) and the second under Ezra himself (around 458 B.C.E.). Between these phases lies a period of nearly 80 years, including the completion of the temple rebuilding in 516 B.C.E.

Tradition attributes authorship to Ezra himself, the priest and scribe. Modern scholarship views Ezra and Nehemiah as originally one book, possibly edited together with the books of Chronicles by the so-called "Chronicler" sometime in the fourth century B.C.E. The book contains several types of sources: official Persian documents, lists of returnees, and Ezra's own first-person narrative.

The political situation was characterized by the dominance of the Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great and his successors. The Persians pursued a more tolerant religious policy than the Babylonians, allowing deported peoples to return home and rebuild their sanctuaries. Judah was a small province (Yehud) within the western satrapy of the Persian Empire, subject to governors appointed by the Great King.

## Literary Context

The book of Ezra combines several literary genres: historical narrative, official documents (some in Aramaic), genealogies, and personal memoirs. The Aramaic section (Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26) reflects the administrative language of the Persian Empire. The alternation between Hebrew and Aramaic gives the book an authentic feel from that period.

In the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), Ezra-Nehemiah is counted as one book and placed in the Writings (Ketuvim), often alongside the books of Chronicles. In Christian Bibles, the book is placed among the historical books, following the books of Chronicles. The book is structured in two main parts: chapters 1–6 deal with the first return and the rebuilding of the temple, while chapters 7–10 focus on Ezra's arrival and his reforms.

## Cultural Background

The society in the province of Yehud was marked by reconstruction and an identity crisis. The returnees encountered a population that had remained in the land and developed its own religious practices. Conflicts arose between these groups over the right to participate in the temple rebuilding and over religious authority. Society was organized according to family and tribal affiliation, as evidenced by the many lists in the book.

Religiously, this period marks a transition from pre-exilic Israelite religion to early Judaism. The temple in Jerusalem was rebuilt as the center of worship, but now without the ark of the covenant. The Torah (Law of Moses) gained increasing significance as the foundation for Jewish identity, and Ezra is portrayed as the one who introduced systematic reading and exposition of the law.

Relations with surrounding peoples were complicated. "The people of the land" and the Samaritans were rejected as partners, laying the groundwork for later hostility. Intermarriage with women from other nations was seen as a serious threat to religious purity and national identity. Ezra's dramatic confrontation with this issue in chapters 9–10 reflects an exclusive understanding of Jewish identity.

## Archaeology and Historical Sources

**Cyrus Cylinder (539 B.C.E.)**
Discovered in Babylon in 1879, now in the British Museum. This clay cylinder documents Cyrus's policy of allowing deported peoples to return home and rebuild their temples. It confirms the biblical picture of Cyrus as favorable toward oppressed peoples, although the Jews are not mentioned directly.

**Elephantine Papyri (500–400s B.C.E.)**
Discovered on Elephantine Island in Egypt. These Aramaic documents from a Jewish military community mention the temple in Jerusalem and individuals known from Ezra-Nehemiah. They provide insight into Jewish life in the diaspora and confirm the use of Aramaic as an administrative language.

**Persepolis Archive (500s B.C.E.)**
Discovered in Persepolis, Iran. Administrative tablets in Elamite and Aramaic show how the Persian bureaucracy functioned. They illuminate the background for the official documents reproduced in the book of Ezra.

**Behistun Inscription (ca. 520 B.C.E.)**
Discovered at Behistun, Iran. Darius I's great inscription in three languages documents his rise to power and the organization of the empire. It provides important background for the period when the temple was rebuilt under his rule.

**Wadi Daliyeh Papyri (4th century B.C.E.)**
Discovered in a cave north of Jericho. Samaritan documents that shed light on relations between Samaria and Yehud during Persian times, relevant to the conflict described in Ezra 4.
