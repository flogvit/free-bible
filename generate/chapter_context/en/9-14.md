## People

The chapter introduces several important figures. **Jonathan** (יוֹנָתָן/יְהוֹנָתָן), Saul’s son, appears as the main character and a brave warrior who acts in faith in God. His **armor-bearer** (נֹשֵׂא כֵלָיו) was a trusted servant who accompanied his master into battle—a role that required both loyalty and courage. **Ahijah** (אֲחִיָּה), son of Ahitub, was a priest who carried the ephod; he was the brother of Ichabod and grandson of Eli, linking him to the priestly dynasty in Shiloh. The chapter also mentions **Abner** (אֲבִינֵר), son of Ner, as commander of the army. The genealogy in verses 49–51 lists Saul’s family: his sons Jonathan, Ishvi, and Malki-Shua; his daughters Merab and Michal; and his wife Ahinoam.

## Places

**Migron** (מִגְרוֹן) and the area near **Gibeah** (גִּבְעָה) in Benjamin form the starting point of the narrative, where Saul sat under the pomegranate tree (הָרִמּוֹן) with his 600 men. **Michmash** (מִכְמָשׂ) was the Philistine camp, and the pass between Michmash and Geba is geographically identified with Wadi Suweinit, a steep ravine with rocky walls. The two rocky peaks **Bozez** (בּוֹצֵץ) and **Seneh** (סֶנֶּה) (v. 4–5) marked the narrow passage that Jonathan climbed. The terrain here is extremely steep and difficult to traverse, underscoring the boldness of Jonathan’s attack. **Beth-Aven** (בֵּית אָוֶן, v. 23) marks how far the pursuit of the Philistines went, and **Aijalon** (אַיָּלוֹן, v. 31) shows that the Israelites pursued the enemy westward toward the coast.

## Events and Customs

The **ephod** that Ahijah carried (v. 3) was a priestly garment used to seek God’s will. In verse 18, the **ark of God** (אֲרוֹן הָאֱלֹהִים) is mentioned as being with the Israelites on that day, and Saul asked the priest to bring it forward for an oracle inquiry. Saul’s interruption of the oracle inquiry (v. 19: "withdraw your hand") shows that the process took time and could be halted. **The oath that Saul imposed on the people** (v. 24)—that no one should eat until evening—was a form of sacred fasting connected to warfare, but it proved unwise and nearly cost Jonathan his life. **Eating meat with its blood** (vv. 32–34) was forbidden according to Leviticus 17:10–14, since the blood belongs to God. Saul resolved this by raising a large stone as an improvised slaughter altar. **The altar that Saul built** (v. 35) is described as the first altar he built for the Lord. **The lot** (תָמִים, vv. 41–42) was cast to reveal guilt—an accepted method of seeking God’s judgment in difficult matters.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the trembling of God"** (חֶרְדַּת אֱלֹהִים, v. 15) describes a supernatural panic that God sent upon the Philistines—a known motif in Israel’s holy wars. Jonathan’s reference to the Philistines as **"these uncircumcised"** (הָעֲרֵלִים הָאֵלֶּה, v. 6) was a derogatory term marking them as outside the covenant with Israel’s God. The verb **עָכַר** (v. 29, "to bring trouble upon") is otherwise used concerning Achan in Joshua 7 and suggests that Jonathan’s criticism of his father’s oath was serious. The verb **פדה** (וַיִּפְדּוּ, v. 45) means "to ransom/deliver" and shows that the people rescued Jonathan from the death penalty Saul had prescribed.
