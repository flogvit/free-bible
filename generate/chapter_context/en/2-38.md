# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 38

## People

The chapter mentions several key figures in the construction of the tabernacle. **Bezalel, son of Uri, son of Hur, from the tribe of Judah** (v. 22) was the chief craftsman who led the work. **Oholiab, son of Ahisamach, from the tribe of Dan** (v. 23) was his assistant, described as an engraver, designer, and embroiderer. The fact that the master craftsmen came from both the tribe of Judah and the tribe of Dan shows the breadth of participation in this holy project.

**Ithamar, son of Aaron the priest** (v. 21), oversaw the work of the Levites and kept records of the materials. As one of Aaron's sons, he was given administrative duties related to the tabernacle.

## Events and Customs

Verse 8 contains a remarkable detail: the bronze basin for washing was made from **mirrors of the women who served at the entrance to the tent of meeting**. The Hebrew word "הַצֹּבְאֹת" (ha-tsov'ot) used here suggests organized service. Polished bronze mirrors were valuable possessions in ancient times, and the fact that these women gave up such personal items represents a significant sacrifice.

## Key Terms

**The sanctuary shekel** (שֶׁקֶל הַקֹּדֶשׁ, shekel ha-kodesh) is mentioned repeatedly as the standard measure (vv. 24–26). This was a unit of weight, not a coin, equivalent to about 11–14 grams. Having its own standard for the sanctuary ensured accuracy and prevented fraud.

**Beka** (בֶּקַע, v. 26) was half a shekel and represented the head tax that every man over twenty years old paid. This confirms the census: 603,550 men each giving half a shekel resulted in exactly 100 talents and 1,775 shekels of silver.

**Talent** (כִּכָּר, kikkar) was the largest unit of weight, equivalent to about 30–36 kilograms. The total amounts—29 talents and 730 shekels of gold, 100 talents and 1,775 shekels of silver, and 70 talents and 2,400 shekels of bronze—represent enormous values.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries have confirmed several details in this chapter. A **beka weight** inscribed with the word "beka" has been found in excavations in Jerusalem, and its weight matches the biblical information that a beka equaled half a shekel. This confirms the measurement system described in the text.

**Bronze mirrors** from ancient Egypt have been found in large numbers and show what such mirrors looked like. They typically had a polished metal disk with a handle, often decorated. The quality of Egyptian metalwork was high, and the Israelites who had lived in Egypt would have had access to such items.
