# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 25

## People

Chapter 25 opens with an important historical note: "These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied" (v. 1). King Hezekiah reigned in Judah around 729–686 BC (co-regent from about 729, sole ruler from about 715), approximately 250 years after Solomon's time. This verse testifies to a deliberate literary and wisdom tradition at the court in Jerusalem. "The men of Hezekiah" (אַנְשֵׁי חִזְקִיָּה) likely refer to a group of scribes or scholars who worked to preserve and edit older wisdom literature. Hezekiah's reforms and his interest in restoring Israelite tradition (see 2 Chron 29–31) evidently included the collection of Solomon's proverbs.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains several proverbs about court culture and royal etiquette (vv. 2–7, 15). The advice not to "exalt yourself before the king" or stand in "the place of great men" (vv. 6–7) reflects the strict hierarchical structure of ancient Near Eastern courts. Seating at meals and in assemblies was carefully ranked, and taking the wrong place could lead to public humiliation. Similar counsel is found in Egyptian wisdom literature, especially in "The Maxims of Ptahhotep," which also warns against inappropriate self-assertion toward superiors.

Verses 21–22, regarding giving food and drink to an enemy, resulting in "burning coals on his head," have been the subject of much discussion. A possible background is an Egyptian penitential ritual in which a repentant person carried a bowl of burning coals on their head as a sign of remorse and shame. Regardless of its origin, the proverb expresses that kindness toward enemies can lead to their repentance or shame.

## Key Terms

The verb הֶעְתִּיקוּ (he'tiqu) in verse 1, translated "copied" or "collected," is a rare word suggesting a formal process of transferring and preserving texts. This provides insight into how wisdom literature was transmitted in ancient Israel—not only orally but through deliberate written editorial work.

The expression תַּפּוּחֵי זָהָב בְּמַשְׂכִּיּוֹת כָּסֶף ("apples of gold in settings of silver") in verse 11 uses a word (מַשְׂכִּיּוֹת) whose meaning is uncertain. It may refer to carved or pierced silverwork, perhaps filigree, through which golden fruits (possibly apricots or quinces, since the Hebrew word תַּפּוּחַ likely does not refer to modern apples) were visible. The image describes how a fitting word spoken at the right time has aesthetic and artistic value.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from Hezekiah's time confirm the existence of an advanced scribal culture in Jerusalem. Seals and bullae (clay impressions) from this period, including seals mentioning "Belonging to Hezekiah [son] of Ahaz, king of Judah," testify to an active bureaucracy. The Siloam inscription from Hezekiah's tunnel also demonstrates the literary level at the court. These findings support the credibility of the chapter's heading that "the men of Hezekiah" were involved in collecting and copying older wisdom texts.
