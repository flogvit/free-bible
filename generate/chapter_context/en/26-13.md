# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 13

## People

The chapter addresses two groups: male prophets who "prophesy from their own hearts" (v. 2) and female prophets described as "your people's daughters who prophesy" (v. 17). These were individuals active among the Jewish exile communities in Babylon and Jerusalem, proclaiming false peace and hope at a time when God's judgment was unavoidable. The false prophets represented a widespread phenomenon in ancient Israel, where persons claimed divine authority without being sent by God. Jeremiah confronted similar prophets in Jerusalem during the same period (Jer 23:9-40; 28–29).

## Events and Customs

The women in verses 17–23 practiced a form of magic or divination involving "sleeves on all elbows" (כְּסָתוֹת, *kesatot*) and "veils over heads of every height" (מִסְפָּחוֹת, *mispachot*). These items were likely used in rituals to "catch souls"—possibly a form of binding or cursing against enemies and protecting clients. The practice combined elements from Canaanite and Mesopotamian magic. Payment in "handfuls of barley and pieces of bread" (v. 19) shows that these services were bought for modest sums, making them accessible to ordinary people. Such magical practices were strictly forbidden under Israelite law (Deut 18:10–12).

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word תָּפֵל (*tafel*) in verses 10–15, translated as "lime" or "whitewash," describes a weak mortar mixture lacking proper binding agents. The image of a poorly built wall that is whitewashed to hide its weaknesses illustrates how false prophets covered up the nation's spiritual decay with empty messages of peace. The phrase "peace, and there is no peace" (שָׁלוֹם וְאֵין שָׁלוֹם, v. 10, 16) is a central accusation—the false prophets promised *shalom* (peace, welfare, wholeness) when God's judgment was at the door. The words for "vision" (חָזוֹן, *chazon*) and "divination" (קֶסֶם, *qesem*) are used with the adjectives "empty" (שָׁוְא, *shav*) and "falsehood" (כָּזָב, *kazav*) to emphasize that their messages lacked divine origin.
