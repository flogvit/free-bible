# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 14

## People

**Hiram (Huram) of Tyre** was king of the Phoenician city-state of Tyre. His sending of craftsmen and cedar trees to David (v. 1) testifies to diplomatic relations between Israel and Phoenicia. The cedars from Lebanon were sought-after building materials and represented royal status and wealth.

**David’s sons born in Jerusalem** are listed in verses 4–7. The list includes Nathan, who became an ancestor in one of Jesus’ lines (Luke 3:31), and Solomon, who would succeed David as king. The name Beel-ada (בְּעֶלְיָדָע, "Baal knows") is notable because it contains the divine name Baal. In 2 Samuel 5:16 he is called Eljada (אֶלְיָדָע, "God knows"), which may indicate later editing to remove the Baal element.

## Places

**The Valley of Rephaim** (עֵמֶק רְפָאִים) lies southwest of Jerusalem and formed a natural invasion route toward the city. The name "Rephaim" refers to an ancient people group associated in the Bible with giants (cf. Genesis 15:20; Deuteronomy 2:11). The valley was strategically important, so it is not surprising that the Philistines chose this route to attack David.

**Baal-Perazim** (בַּעַל פְּרָצִים, "Lord of breakthroughs") was the site of David’s first victory over the Philistines. David named the place to commemorate how God broke through the enemy "as a flood breaks through" (v. 11). Place names marking military victories and divine intervention were common in ancient Israel.

**From Gibeon to Gezer** marks the extent of David’s pursuit of the Philistines. Gibeon was located about 9–10 kilometers northwest of Jerusalem, while Gezer was an important fortified city near the coast. This pursuit demonstrated a complete military victory that drove the Philistines back into their own territory.

## Events and Customs

**Seeking God before war** (vv. 10, 14) was an established practice in ancient Israel. David asked God twice and received different strategic answers each time. This underscores the Chronicler’s theological point that military success depended on obedience to God, not merely military skill. Such divine consultation could occur through prophets or the Urim and Thummim.

**Burning of enemy idols** (v. 12) stands in contrast to the parallel account in 2 Samuel 5:21, where it says David and his men "took them away." The Chronicler changes this to say the images were burned, in keeping with the commandment of the law in Deuteronomy 7:5; 25. This shows the Chronicler’s desire to portray David as one who fully obeyed the Mosaic Law.

## Key Terms

**Bekaim** (הַבְּכָאִים) in verses 14–15 refers to a type of tree, possibly balsam poplars or mulberry trees. The sound of "footsteps" in the treetops was to be the sign that God Himself was going before the army. This divine signal united natural elements with supernatural guidance—perhaps the sound of wind in the leaves as a sign of the advance of God’s invisible host.

**Parats** (פָּרַץ, "to break through") is used in verse 11 to describe God’s victory. The flood metaphor was powerful in the dry climate of the Middle East, where sudden floods could be devastating. The wordplay between the verb and the place name Baal-Perazim is typical of Hebrew naming traditions.
