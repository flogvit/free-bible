# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 19

## People

**Sennacherib** (Hebrew: סַנְחֵרִיב) was king of Assyria from 705–681 B.C. He is one of the best-documented Assyrian kings, with his own annals and prisms confirming his campaigns against Judah. His father Sargon II died in battle, and upon ascending the throne, Sennacherib had to deal with rebellions throughout the empire.

**Isaiah son of Amoz** (יְשַׁעְיָהוּ בֶּן־אָמוֹץ) served as God’s prophetic mouthpiece to King Hezekiah during this crisis. He delivered two messages to the king: first, a brief assurance that Sennacherib would return to his own land and fall by the sword (verses 6–7), then a longer poetic oracle against Assyria’s arrogance (verses 20–34). Isaiah chapter 37 contains a parallel account.

**Tirhakah** (תִּרְהָקָה), king of Cush, belonged to the 25th Egyptian dynasty (the "Nubian" dynasty). He formally reigned as pharaoh from about 690 B.C., but was already a military leader under his predecessor Shebitku in 701 B.C. His advance against Sennacherib is mentioned in verse 9.

**Eliakim, Shebna, and the elders of the priests** (verse 2): Eliakim (אֶלְיָקִים) was the palace administrator ("over the house"), Shebna (שֶׁבְנָא) was the scribe, and the elders of the priests (זִקְנֵי הַכֹּהֲנִים) represented the temple leadership. These men were sent by Hezekiah to Isaiah, dressed in sackcloth.

**Adrammelech and Sharezer** (אַדְרַמֶּלֶךְ וְשַׂרְאֶצֶר) were Sennacherib’s sons who killed their father while he was worshiping in the temple of Nisroch (verse 37). After the murder, they fled to the land of Ararat.

**Esar-haddon** (אֵסַר־חַדֹּן) was Sennacherib’s son who succeeded him as king of Assyria after the patricides (verse 37).

## Places

**Libnah** (לִבְנָה) and **Lachish** (לָכִישׁ) were fortified cities in the Judean lowlands (Shephelah). Lachish was the second most important city after Jerusalem. Sennacherib conquered Lachish and depicted the siege in detail in his palace at Nineveh. These reliefs, now in the British Museum, show Assyrian siege techniques and Judean captives.

**Gozan, Haran/Carrae, Resen, and Telassar** (verse 12) were regions in upper Mesopotamia conquered by Assyria. Gozan (גּוֹזָן, Tell Halaf) and Haran/Carrae (חָרָן) are well-known archaeological sites. The people of Eden (בְּנֵי־עֶדֶן) refer to the Aramean state of Bit-Adini near the Euphrates.

**Nineveh** (נִינְוֵה), Assyria’s capital from Sennacherib’s time, was located on the Tigris River (modern-day Mosul in Iraq). Sennacherib massively expanded the city, including the famous "Palace Without Rival."

**Ararat** (אֶרֶץ אֲרָרָט) was the mountainous region in eastern Anatolia (modern Armenia/eastern Turkey) to which Sennacherib’s murderers fled (verse 37).

## Events and Customs

**Tearing clothes and putting on sackcloth** (verse 1) was the strongest expression of mourning and humility in Old Testament culture. Sackcloth (שַׂק) was a coarse, dark fabric made from goat hair, used during national mourning, personal crisis, or as a garment of repentance. The fact that both the king and the priests dressed this way signaled a national crisis.

**Spreading out the letter before the Lord** (verse 14) was a ritual act in which Hezekiah symbolically presented the matter to God in the temple. This reflects an understanding of the temple as God’s earthly throne room, where he could "see" and "hear" his people’s prayers.

**The sign involving the agricultural cycle** (verse 29) describes three years: In the first year, the people will eat "aftergrowth" (סָפִיחַ, self-sown grain from the previous harvest); in the second year, what grows from that again; and only in the third year can normal sowing and harvesting resume. This indicates that the Assyrian siege and devastation had prevented normal agricultural activity.

## Key Terms

**"The Virgin Daughter Zion"** (בְּתוּלַת בַּת־צִיּוֹן, verse 21) is a poetic personification of Jerusalem. "Virgin" denotes a city that has never been conquered, and to "shake the head" behind an enemy expresses contempt and mockery. The image portrays Jerusalem confidently mocking the fleeing Assyrian army.

**"Hook in your nose and bit in your lips"** (verse 28) uses the imagery of how the Assyrians themselves treated captives—they led them with hooks through their noses or lips. The irony is that God will do the same to Assyria’s proud king.

**"The zeal of the Lord"** (קִנְאַת יְהוָה, verse 31) describes God’s passionate commitment to his people and his honor. The word "zeal" implies both jealousy and burning fervor.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Sennacherib’s Prism** (the Taylor Prism and similar examples) from about 691 B.C. describes the campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. Sennacherib boasts of conquering 46 fortified cities and besieging Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem, but remarkably never mentions capturing the city. This aligns with the biblical account that he withdrew without taking Jerusalem.

**The Murder of Sennacherib** (verse 37) is confirmed by Babylonian and Assyrian sources. Babylonian chronicles date the murder to 681 B.C. Assyrian records note that his son Esar-haddon seized power after a brief interregnum. The god **Nisroch** (נִסְרֹךְ) has not been identified with certainty but may be a distortion of Nusku (the fire god) or Marduk.

**The Lachish Reliefs** in Sennacherib’s palace at Nineveh depict detailed Assyrian warfare against Judean cities, including siege ramps, archers, and the deportation of captives. These provide visual context for the threats directed against Jerusalem.
