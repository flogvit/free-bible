# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 12

## Places

The chapter mentions two specific places in connection with the Israelites' departure: **Rameses** (רַעְמְסֵס, v. 37) and **Succoth** (סֻכֹּת, v. 37). Rameses was one of the store cities that the Israelites had built as slaves (Exod 1:11), and it was likely located in the eastern Nile Delta. Archaeologists have identified the site with Pi-Ramesse, the residence city of Ramesses II, which lay near modern Qantir. Succoth, the Israelites' first stopping place, is often associated with the Egyptian Tjeku in the Wadi Tumilat, a natural corridor eastward toward the Sinai Peninsula.

## Events and Customs

Chapter 12 contains detailed instructions for **the Passover meal** (פֶּסַח) and **the Festival of Unleavened Bread** (חַג הַמַּצּוֹת). The lamb was to be selected on the tenth day of the month, slaughtered on the fourteenth "between the two evenings" (בֵּין הָעַרְבָּיִם), and eaten that night with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. The blood was to be applied to the doorposts and the upper lintel using a hyssop branch. These elements—haste, travel clothing, and the prohibition against breaking any of the lamb's bones—all point to a swift departure. The festival was to be celebrated annually as an "everlasting statute" (חֻקַּת עוֹלָם), with seven days during which all leaven had to be removed from homes.

In surrounding cultures, spring offerings of firstborn animals were common, often linked to the beginning of lambing season. What sets Passover apart is its historical grounding in a specific act of deliverance: God passing over the houses of the Israelites during the tenth plague.

## Key Terms

**פֶּסַח (pesach)** – The word itself is traditionally translated as "to pass over" or "to spare", describing the Lord's action when he "passed over" (וּפָסַחְתִּי) the houses marked with blood. The verb can also mean "to protect" or "to shield".

**מַצּוֹת (matsot)** – Unleavened bread, baked without leaven (שְׂאֹר) or fermentation (חָמֵץ). The text gives a practical explanation: the dough did not have time to rise because the people were driven out in haste. In later Jewish tradition, leaven was also interpreted symbolically as pride or evil.

**מְזוּזֹת (mezuzot)** and **מַשְׁקוֹף (mashkof)** (vv. 7, 22–23) – The doorposts and the upper lintel respectively. The blood on these places marked the house as belonging to the covenant people. Note that the later Jewish custom of mezuzah scrolls (based on Deut 6:9) has a different content than the blood marking in this chapter.

**עֵרֶב רַב (erev rav)** – "A great mixed multitude" (v. 38) that went out together with the Israelites. This shows that the exodus included not only ethnic Israelites but also others who joined them.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian sources document Semitic workers in the Delta region during the second millennium BCE. Papyrus Anastasi V (circa 1200s BCE) mentions "shasu" (nomadic Semites) who were granted permission to pass through the border fortresses in the Tjeku area to find pasture—the same region identified with Succoth. Although this does not directly confirm the exodus, it shows that movements of Semitic groups between Egypt and Canaan were known and recorded by Egyptian authorities.
