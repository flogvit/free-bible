# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 2

## People

This chapter presents the genealogy of the tribe of Judah, with special emphasis on the lines leading to David and other significant families in Judah. **Tamar** (v. 4) was Judah's daughter-in-law who bore Perez and Zerah following the unusual event described in Genesis 38, where she secured the lineage by posing as a prostitute. **Achar** (Hebrew עָכָר, v. 7) is called "the one who brought trouble on Israel" (עוֹכֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל)—a play on his name and his sin involving devoted things from Jericho (Joshua 7). In Joshua he is called "Achan" (עָכָן), but Chronicles uses the form "Achar" to strengthen the wordplay with the verb עכר ("to bring trouble").

**Bezalel** (v. 20) was the craftsman who led the construction of the tabernacle (Exodus 31:1-5). His lineage through Hur and Caleb provides important legitimacy. **Nahshon** (v. 10) was the leader of the tribe of Judah during the wilderness journey and, according to Jewish tradition, the first to step into the Red Sea. The lineage from Ram to David (vv. 10-16) corresponds with the genealogy in Ruth 4:18-22 and establishes David's legitimate descent from Judah through Perez.

## Places

The chapter mentions several important locations in the region of Judah. **Gilead** (vv. 21-23) was the area east of the Jordan where Segub, son of Hezron and daughter of Machir, and his son Jair established twenty-three cities. **Kiriath-jearim** (vv. 50-53) was the city where the ark of the covenant was kept after it was returned from the Philistines (1 Samuel 7:1-2). **Bethlehem** (v. 51) is identified as the town of Salma's descendants—this is significant since David came from Bethlehem, and the text thus establishes the deep roots of David's family there.

**Hebron** (vv. 42-43) was one of the most important cities in Judah and later became David's first capital. **Beth-shur**, **Tekoa**, and **Gibeah** (vv. 24, 45, 49) were all towns in the hill country of Judah that later played roles in Israelite history.

## Events and Customs

The genealogies in this chapter reflect several cultural practices. **Marriage to Canaanite women** is explicitly mentioned (v. 3, "the daughter of Bath-shua the Canaanite"), which was usually condemned, yet here it is included without comment as part of the ancestors' history. **Concubines** (פִּלֶגֶשׁ, *pilegesh*, vv. 46, 48) were an accepted practice, and the status of children through concubines was recognized in genealogical records.

The remarkable case of Sheshan (vv. 34-35), who gave his daughter to his Egyptian slave Jarha because he had no sons, illustrates how inheritance rules could be adapted. This practice ensured that the family name and property were continued, even through unconventional means.

## Key Terms

The expression **אֲבִי** (*avi*, "father of") is often used in the sense of "founder of" when referring to cities (vv. 42, 45, 49-51). This indicates that the person either founded the city, was the patriarch of a clan, or was a significant leader there. **Families of scribes** (מִשְׁפְּחוֹת סֹפְרִים, v. 55) in Jabez is an early notable reference to a professional class of scribes—the Kenites who were associated with the house of Rechab and lived as nomads (Jeremiah 35).

The term **חֵרֶם** (*cherem*, v. 7) refers to "devotion" or "ban"—things or persons set apart for destruction and therefore forbidden to take. Achar's sin consisted in taking some of the devoted things from Jericho.
