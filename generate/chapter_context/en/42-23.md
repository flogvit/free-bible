## People

**Pontius Pilate**, the Roman prefect of Judea, declares three times that he finds no guilt in Jesus (vv. 4, 14, 22), but finally yields to the crowd's demands. His sending of Jesus to Herod may reflect political caution in a tense situation.

**Herod Antipas**, tetrarch of Galilee, was in Jerusalem for the Passover celebration (v. 7). Only Luke recounts Jesus' meeting with Herod. He had long desired to see Jesus perform a sign (v. 8), but when Jesus remained silent, Herod mocked him and dressed him in "bright clothing" (ἐσθῆτα λαμπρὰν) – likely white or shining royal garments, used mockingly (v. 11).

**Barabbas** was imprisoned for rebellion and murder (vv. 19, 25). The crowd chooses to release him rather than Jesus.

**Simon of Cyrene** was forced to carry Jesus' cross (v. 26). That he came "from the field" (ἀπʼ ἀγροῦ) may indicate he was entering the city, possibly as a Passover pilgrim.

**The two criminals** (κακοῦργοι) were crucified alongside Jesus (vv. 32-33). One mocked him, while the other rebuked his fellow prisoner and asked Jesus to remember him – receiving the promise of paradise (vv. 39-43).

**Joseph of Arimathea** is described as a "βουλευτής" (member of the council in the Sanhedrin) who had not consented to the decision against Jesus (v. 51). For a council member to dare request Jesus' body was socially risky.

**The women from Galilee** followed him to the cross and the tomb (vv. 49, 55) and prepared spices, but had to wait until after the Sabbath (v. 56).

## Places

**Golgotha** (Κρανίον, "the skull," v. 33) was the execution site outside Jerusalem's city walls, as required by Roman practice.

**The tomb** is described as a "μνήματι λαξευτῷ" (hewn out of rock, v. 53), typical for wealthy Jews. That it had never been used before underscores the honor Joseph showed Jesus.

## Events and Customs

**The trial before Pilate** – The charges against Jesus were political: misleading the people, refusing to pay taxes to Caesar, and claiming to be Messiah and king (v. 2). These were formulated to force Pilate into action.

**Pardon during the festival** (v. 17) is sometimes referred to as "paschal amnesty" in scholarly literature. Although this custom is not documented outside the Gospels, Romans occasionally practiced such pardons during festivals.

**Crucifixion** – The inscription (τίτλος) stating the crime was standard practice; for Jesus: "King of the Jews" (v. 38). Crucified persons were normally denied burial, but Jewish law required that bodies be taken down before sunset (Deut 21:22-23), which explains the haste in the burial.

**The day of Preparation** (παρασκευή, v. 54) was Friday, when everything was prepared before the Sabbath. That the Sabbath "began to dawn" (ἐπέφωσκεν) refers to Jewish timekeeping where the day began at sunset – thus Friday afternoon/evening.

## Key Terms

**"Paradise"** (παράδεισος, v. 43) is a Persian loanword originally meaning "enclosed garden" or royal park. In Jewish tradition during Jesus' time, it denoted the heavenly existence for the righteous. Jesus' promise to the repentant criminal was a radical statement about immediate salvation.

**"The curtain of the temple"** (τὸ καταπέτασμα τοῦ ναοῦ, v. 45) refers either to the outer curtain at the entrance to the Holy Place or the inner curtain separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, the place of God's presence. That it tore "in two from top to bottom" theologically signaled that the separation between God and humanity was removed.

**"Righteous"** (δίκαιος, v. 47) – The Roman officer's declaration that Jesus was "righteous" was both a legal acquittal and carried deeper theological meaning: in Jewish tradition, "the righteous one" could denote the Messiah.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

**Darkness** from the sixth to the ninth hour (about noon to 3 p.m., v. 44) and **the tearing of the curtain** (v. 45) are cosmic signs framing Jesus' death. Luke places the tearing of the curtain before Jesus' death, unlike Matthew and Mark.

**Jesus' last words** in Luke are unique: "Father, forgive them" (v. 34; whether this verse is original is debated) and "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (v. 46, from Psalm 31:6). This portrays a peaceful surrender, contrasting with the cry of despair in Matthew and Mark.
