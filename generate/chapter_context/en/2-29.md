# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 29

## People

The chapter focuses on Aaron and his sons (Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar; see Exodus 28:1), who are to be consecrated for priestly service. Moses leads the ceremony by God's command. The sons are mentioned as a group because the entire family was to be consecrated together in the same ceremony.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes a detailed consecration ceremony (*millu'im* – "to fill the hands") that was to last seven days (v. 35, 37). The ritual includes several elements: washing with water for purification, dressing in special garments, anointing with oil, and offering animals. Laying hands on the head of the sacrificial animal (*semikah*) (vv. 10, 15, 19) symbolized identification between the offerer and the sacrifice. Sprinkling blood on the earlobe, thumb, and big toe (v. 20) marked that the whole person – hearing, action, and conduct – was dedicated to God. Similar elements in consecration rituals are known from Mesopotamian and Egyptian sources, but the Israelite ceremonies had distinct theological meanings.

## Key Terms

**Millu'im** (מִלֻּאִים, "consecration/filling") – The word literally means "filling" and refers to "filling the hands" with the office. This was a technical term for priestly ordination in ancient Israel. **Tamid** (תָּמִיד, "continual/constant") – Used concerning the daily burnt offering (vv. 38–42), two one-year-old lambs each day, morning and evening. This offering became one of the most central in temple worship. **Tenufah** (תְּנוּפָה, "wave offering") and **terumah** (תְּרוּמָה, "heave offering") – Two types of sacrificial actions where the offerings were waved or lifted before the Lord as a symbolic presentation before parts were given to the priests. **Nesek** (נֶסֶךְ, "drink offering") – The wine offering that accompanied the daily sacrifice. A quarter hin of wine (about 1 liter) was offered with each lamb (vv. 40–41).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at several Canaanite and Israelite cult sites have uncovered altars with "horns" in the corners, confirming the description in verse 12 of smearing blood on the altar's horns. Such horned altars have been found, among other places, at Beersheba and Megiddo. Discoveries of bone remains at these altars show traces of sacrificial practices that align with biblical descriptions – including the burning of specific animal parts. Pottery finds from the Bronze and Iron Ages also reveal bowls and vessels likely used for handling blood and liquid offerings, shedding light on the practical aspects of the rituals described in this chapter.
