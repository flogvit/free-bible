# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 16

## People

**Hagar** (הָגָר) is introduced as an Egyptian slave woman (שִׁפְחָה מִצְרִית) belonging to Sarai. Her name may be of Egyptian origin, or it could be associated with the Hebrew/Arabic word for "flight" or "foreigner". Being Egyptian possibly links her to Abram’s earlier stay in Egypt (Gen 12:10–20). Hagar is remarkable in a biblical context as the first person to receive a visit from the angel of the Lord and the only person in the Bible who gives God a name.

**Ishmael** (יִשְׁמָעֵאל) means "God hears" and becomes the ancestor of the Ishmaelites, a people who according to tradition lived as nomads in the desert regions south and east of Canaan (cf. Gen 25:12–18).

## Places

**The Way to Shur** (דֶּרֶךְ שֽׁוּר) was a trade route leading from Canaan toward Egypt through the northern Sinai desert. The fact that Hagar is on this road shows she was fleeing back toward Egypt, her homeland. Shur is also mentioned as a desert area on the eastern side of Egypt.

**Beer-lahai-roi** (בְּאֵר לַחַי רֹאִי), which may mean "the well of the Living One who sees me" or "the well of the living one who sees", is located between Kadesh and Bered in the Negev desert. The place is mentioned again in connection with Isaac (Gen 24:62; 25:11).

## Events and Customs

**Surrogacy through a slave woman** was a widespread practice in the ancient Near East. Texts from ancient Babylon and Nuzi (ca. 1500–1350 BCE) contain marriage contracts specifying that a childless wife should give her husband a slave woman to bear children. The Laws of Hammurabi (§144–147) regulate this practice, stating that a slave woman who bears children cannot be sold, but the wife retains authority over her. Sarai’s words "perhaps I may build up" (אִבָּנֶה) use a wordplay on the Hebrew word for "son" (בֵּן).

**Change in status of a slave woman** is reflected in the text by Hagar beginning to look down on her mistress (וַתֵּקַל גְּבִרְתָּהּ) after she becomes pregnant. This often created conflicts, as also documented in Mesopotamian legal texts. The Laws of Hammurabi allowed that a slave woman who acted arrogantly toward her childless mistress could be demoted back to slave labor.

## Key Terms

**The angel of the Lord** (מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה) appears here for the first time in the Bible. This figure sometimes speaks as God’s messenger and at other times as God himself (verses 10–11, 13). The relationship between the angel and God himself is mysterious in the text and has been interpreted in various ways throughout history.

**Gebirah** (גְּבִרְתָּהּ), "her mistress", is a technical term for the relationship between a free woman and her slave woman. The same word is later used for the queen mother during the monarchy, showing the term’s connection to authority and rank.

**Pereh adam** (פֶּרֶא אָדָם), "a wild donkey of a man" (verse 12), describes Ishmael’s future way of life. The wild donkey was a symbol of freedom and independence in the desert, and the expression points toward a nomadic existence outside the control of established societies—not necessarily negative, but as a free and untamed lifestyle.
