# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 58

## Events and Customs

Chapter 58 criticizes fasting practices that involved "afflicting one's soul" (verses 3, 5), bowing the head like a reed, and lying in sackcloth and ashes. These were outward signs of repentance and sorrow that could become empty rituals. The prophet's critique reveals that fast days were combined with business activities and the exploitation of workers (verses 3–4)—a contradiction that undermined the spiritual purpose of fasting.

The description in verses 6–7 of "loosing the bonds of wickedness," "setting the oppressed free," and "sharing bread with the hungry" reflects concrete social problems. Debt slavery and loss of land were recurring challenges in Israelite society (cf. Nehemiah 5). The prophet redefines "true fasting" from ritual self-imposed suffering to active social justice.

## Key Terms

The word *motah* (מוֹטָה, verses 6, 9) means "yoke" and is used here metaphorically for oppressive burdens and debt slavery. To "break every yoke" refers to the release of debt slaves and the restoration of property, as prescribed by the Year of Jubilee (Leviticus 25). The expression *charutsuvvot resha* (חַרְצֻבּוֹת רֶשַׁע, "bonds of wickedness," verse 6) describes legal and economic structures that kept the poor captive.

The Sabbath command in verses 13–14 uses the term *oneg* (עֹנֶג, "a delight"), which later became central to Jewish Sabbath theology. The combination of fasting ethics (verses 1–12) and Sabbath holiness (verses 13–14) shows that the chapter addresses religious practice with the same principle: outward forms must be filled with righteous action and genuine worship.
