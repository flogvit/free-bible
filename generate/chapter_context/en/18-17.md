# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 17

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains several references to legal and economic customs in the ancient Near East. In verse 3, Job asks God to "stand as guarantor" (עָרְבֵנִי, *'orveni*) and mentions "striking the hand" (לְיָדִי יִתָּקֵעַ, *leyadi yittaqa'*). This refers to the ancient custom of a handshake as a binding guarantee in legal agreements and suretyship. A person who acted as guarantor for another would strike their hand against the other's hand as a sign that they assumed responsibility for the debt. This practice is warned against in several places in Proverbs (6:1; 11:15; 17:18).

In verse 6, Job says that God has "made me a byword among the peoples" (לִמְשֹׁל עַמִּים, *limshol 'ammim*) and that he has become "one whom people spit in the face" (וְתֹפֶת לְפָנִים, *vetofet lefanim*). Spitting in someone's face was an extreme form of public disgrace in ancient cultures, marking the outcast as socially dead (cf. Num 12:14; Deut 25:9).

## Key Terms

The word שְׁאוֹל (*she'ol*) appears twice in this chapter (verses 13, 16) and represents the Hebrew concept of the abode of the dead. In contrast to later developed theology concerning heaven and hell, *she'ol* in the book of Job describes a dark, silent existence beneath the earth where all the dead—both righteous and unrighteous—are found. Job personifies *she'ol* as his future "home" and "bed," which reflects the pessimistic tone of the chapter.

In verse 14, Job addresses "corruption" (שַׁחַת, *shachat*) as his "father" and the "worm" (רִמָּה, *rimmah*) as his "mother and sister." This use of kinship terminology for the grave and decay is a powerful poetic depiction of how close Job feels to death. Such personifications of the realm of the dead are also found in Mesopotamian literature, where the underworld is described as a "place of no return."
