# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 10

## People

**Tola son of Puah** (תּוֹלָע בֶּן־פּוּאָה) was from the tribe of Issachar, but settled in the hill country of Ephraim—something unusual since tribes normally stayed within their allotted territories. His name means "worm" or "scarlet" (the Hebrew word is also used for the scarlet dye derived from a certain insect). The expression בֶּן־דּוֹדוֹ can be understood as "his cousin" (literally "son of his uncle") rather than a proper name, which creates uncertainty about the exact family relationship.

**Jair of Gilead** (יָאִיר הַגִּלְעָדִי) represents a judge with significant social and economic status. His thirty sons who rode on thirty donkeys symbolized wealth and influence, since donkeys were the prestige animals for leaders in that era (cf. Judg 5:10; 12:14). The name Jair ("he enlightens/awakens") also appears in Num 32:41, where a Jair captured villages in Gilead—this chapter explicitly links those villages to the judge.

## Places

**Shamir** (שָׁמִיר) in the hill country of Ephraim is difficult to locate with certainty. Some scholars identify it with Khirbet Samara north of Shechem, while others suggest sites further south. The location's position in the Ephraimite hills, despite Tola being from Issachar, may indicate political instability or strategic considerations.

**Havvoth-Jair** (חַוֺּת יָאִיר, "Jair's village settlements") were located in the Gilead region east of the Jordan. The term *havvot* refers to small, unfortified villages or nomadic settlements, typical of the semi-nomadic life in Transjordan. Archaeologically, there are traces of Iron Age settlements in this area that may correspond to such village clusters.

**Kammon** (קָמוֹן), where Jair was buried, is traditionally identified with Qamm in northern Gilead, about 10 km northwest of Irbid in present-day Jordan. The place is also mentioned in Egyptian sources from the second millennium B.C.E.

**Mizpah** (מִּצְפָּה, "the lookout") in Gilead was a gathering place for the Israelites. This is likely a different location than Mizpah in Benjamin (Judg 20:1). Gilead-Mizpah can be identified with several possible sites, including Khirbet Jal'ad.

## Events and Customs

The chapter's list of foreign gods is the most extensive in the Book of Judges. The Israelites worshipped gods from **seven** different categories: the Baals (הַבְּעָלִים), the Ashtoreths (הָעַשְׁתָּרוֹת), the gods of Aram, the gods of Sidon, the gods of Moab (probably Chemosh), the gods of Ammon (Milcom), and the gods of the Philistines (Dagon and others). The number seven may symbolize complete apostasy. This religious syncretism reflects cultural pressure under which Israel absorbed cults from all its neighboring peoples.

The dialogue between God and Israel in verses 10–16 is unique in the Book of Judges. God's response—"Go and cry out to the gods you have chosen"—represents a form of "divine sarcasm" found in prophetic literature (cf. Isa 44:9–20). The people's response, removing foreign gods and humbling themselves before God's judgment, shows a deeper repentance than earlier in the book.

## Key Terms

The expression **"his soul was grieved over Israel's distress"** (וַתִּקְצַר נַפְשׁוֹ בַּעֲמַל יִשְׂרָאֵל, verse 16) is remarkably theological. The verb קצר literally means "to become short," and the expression describes an intense emotional reaction—here God's own pain over the people's suffering and distress. The same expression is used for human impatience (Num 21:4; Judg 16:16), but when applied to God, it reveals profound divine compassion.

The verb **"crushed and oppressed"** (וַיִּרְעֲצוּ וַיְרֹצְצוּ, verse 8) uses two synonymous verbs for crushing/oppressing in an intensifying construction. This literary device (paronomasia) emphasizes the severity of the oppression and was likely used orally for rhetorical effect.
