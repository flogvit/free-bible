# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 27

## Places

The psalm mentions several important places associated with worship. "The house of the Lord" (בֵית־יְהוָה, *bet-YHWH*) and "his sanctuary" (הֵיכָל, *hekal*) in verse 4 refer to the holy place where God was present. In David's time, this would primarily have been the tabernacle, since the temple was not built until Solomon's reign. "His tent" (אֹהֶל, *ohel*) in verses 5–6 reinforces this connection to the tabernacle as Israel's central place of worship, where the ark of the covenant was kept and God's presence dwelt.

## Events and Customs

The expression "offer sacrifices with joy" (זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה, *zivche teruah*) in verse 6 describes offerings accompanied by loud shouts of gladness or trumpet fanfares. In Israelite worship, such offerings were part of thanksgiving rituals following victory or deliverance. The combination of sacrifice, song, and music was typical for celebrations at the sanctuary. The phrase "to eat my flesh" (verse 2) is figurative language for hostile persecution and slander, a metaphor known from both Israelite and broader Semitic literature.

## Key Concepts

Verse 1 contains three central metaphors for God: "light" (אוֹר, *or*), "salvation" (יֶשַׁע, *yesha*), and "the stronghold of my life" (מָעוֹז־חַיַּי, *maoz chayyai*). The light metaphor represents divine guidance, truth, and life, in contrast to the chaos and death of darkness. "To seek the face of the Lord" (verse 8) is an expression for coming into God's presence for worship and prayer—the face symbolizes personal relationship and favor. "The land of the living" (אֶרֶץ חַיִּים, *erets chayyim*) in verse 13 refers to this world as opposed to the realm of the dead (Sheol), expressing hope to experience God's goodness in this life.

## Chapter-Specific Textual Features

Verse 13 has an unusual textual tradition: the word לוּלֵא (*lule*, "if not") is marked with dots above the letters in the Masoretic text. Such dots (*puncta extraordinaria*) indicate uncertainty among ancient scribes about whether the word belonged to the original text. The verse thus functions rhetorically: the psalmist suggests what would have happened without faith, but leaves the sentence unfinished—perhaps for dramatic effect, or because the thought is too frightening to complete.
