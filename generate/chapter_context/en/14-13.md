# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 13

## People

**Abijah** (also called Abijam in 1 Kings) was the second king of Judah after the division of the kingdom and reigned for three years (c. 913–911 B.C.). His mother Maacah was the daughter of Uriel from Gibeah. The book of Chronicles portrays him far more positively than 1 Kings 15, where he is criticized for sin. This chapter reflects the Chronicler’s theological perspective, in which faithfulness to the temple and legitimate worship are decisive.

**Jeroboam**, Israel’s first king after the division, had been an official under Solomon before leading the rebellion against Rehoboam. The passage describes him as a "servant" who rebelled against his master (vv. 6–7) and highlights his establishment of golden calves and an alternative priesthood as the main charges against the northern kingdom.

## Places

**Mount Zemaraim** (הַר צְמָרַיִם) in the hill country of Ephraim is the place where Abijah delivered his speech before the battle. The exact location is uncertain, but it must have been near the border between the two kingdoms. That Abijah spoke "from the mountain" gave him both an acoustic advantage and symbolic authority.

**Bethel, Jeshanah, and Ephron** were the cities that Abijah captured (v. 19). Bethel was especially significant because it was one of the two places where Jeroboam had placed golden calves (1 Kings 12:29). The capture of Bethel represented a blow against the very core of the northern kingdom’s alternative worship system.

## Events and Customs

**The Covenant of Salt** (v. 5) refers to an unbreakable covenant form in the ancient Near East. Salt was preservative and therefore symbolized permanence and immutability. The expression "covenant of salt" also appears in Numbers 18:19 and marks that God’s promise to David’s line is permanent and inviolable. This was a well-known covenant concept throughout the ancient Near East.

**Trumpet Blowing in War** (vv. 12–14) was prescribed in Numbers 10:9. The priests’ use of trumpets (*ḥatsotserot*) was not only a military signal but also a liturgical act that invoked God’s help. The Chronicler emphasizes that Judah had legitimate worship with them in the war—literally.

## Key Terms

**"Empty men, sons of Belial"** (v. 7, Heb. *anashim reqim bene beliyya'al*) is a strongly derogatory expression denoting worthless, lawless persons. "Belial" means "without worth" or "destruction" and was later used for evil personified. Abijah uses this to delegitimize Jeroboam’s entire rebellion.

**"To fill the hand"** (v. 9, Heb. *lemalle' yado*) was the technical term for priestly ordination. Abijah accuses the northern kingdom of having consecrated unauthorized priests—anyone who brought an ox and seven rams could become a priest for "non-gods." This stands in sharp contrast to the sons of Levi and the descendants of Aaron who served in Jerusalem.
