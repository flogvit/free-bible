## People

The chapter introduces several individuals with specific roles. **Jehoshaphat**, king of Judah, stands at the center as the godly leader who seeks the Lord in crisis. His response to the threat—fasting, prayer, and gathering the people—is presented as a pattern for godly leadership. **Jahaziel son of Zechariah** (יַחֲזִיאֵל בֶּן־זְכַרְיָהוּ), from the family of Asaph among the Levites, acts as a prophetic messenger. His genealogy through four generations underscores his legitimacy as a temple musician with a prophetic gift, in line with Chronicles' interest in the role of the Levites. **Eliezer son of Dodavahu from Mareshah** appears as another prophet who warns against the alliance with Ahaziah. Mareshah was a fortified city in Judah, and the prophet represents the local prophetic tradition outside Jerusalem.

## Places

**En Gedi** (identified with Hazazon Tamar) was an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, known for its springs and palm trees. The site lies about 45 km southeast of Jerusalem and was a natural entry point for enemies coming from the east. Archaeological excavations have revealed settlement from the Iron Age. **The slope of Sis** and the **wilderness of Jeruel** indicate the route the enemy took northward from En Gedi toward the Judean highlands. The **wilderness of Tekoa**, east of the village of Tekoa, was the area where Judah met the enemy. The **Valley of Blessing** (עֵמֶק בְּרָכָה, "valley of blessing") received its name from this event and has traditionally been located in the area between Tekoa and En Gedi. **Ezion Geber**, on the Gulf of Aqaba, was the harbor city where Jehoshaphat and Ahaziah built ships for trade to Tarshish.

## Events and Customs

Jehoshaphat's proclamation of a **fast** throughout Judah was an established practice in times of crisis, also known from other Old Testament texts (Joel 1–2; Esther 4). The fast involved collective humility before God and was often combined with prayer and gathering at the temple. Jehoshaphat's prayer follows a recognizable pattern: invocation of God's sovereignty, reference to past acts of deliverance, presentation of present distress, and appeal for divine intervention.

The use of **singers in front of the army** (v. 21) is unique in biblical war history. The song of praise, "Praise the Lord, for his steadfast love endures forever" (הוֹדוּ לַיהוָה כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ), was a liturgical formula known from the Psalms (Ps 136) and temple worship. The fact that singers went before the armed forces underscores Chronicles' theological point: victory belongs to God, not human military power.

## Key Terms

The expression **מְאָרְבִים** (me'arevim, "ambushers," v. 22) is theologically charged—it is the Lord himself who sets an ambush against the enemy, not Judah. The enemies' **self-destruction** follows a pattern known from other biblical narratives (Judg 7:22; 1 Sam 14:20). The Hebrew term **הַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ** (hadrat-qodesh, "majesty of holiness," v. 21) describes the awe-inspiring beauty of worship and links the song of praise to temple service.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The coalition of **Moab, Ammon, and people from Mount Seir** reflects the geopolitical reality in Transjordan during the 9th century BC. The inscription on the **Mesha Stele** (ca. 840 BC) from Moab mentions conflicts between Moab and Israel/Judah in this period and confirms the political tension in the region. The stele also describes how Moab regained territories and may shed light on the background of Moabite aggression against Judah.
