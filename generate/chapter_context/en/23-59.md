# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 59

## Key Concepts

The chapter contains several Hebrew expressions with deep theological significance. The expression **"the Lord’s hand is not too short"** (v. 1, hebr. *lo qatsrah yad-YHWH*) is an idiom meaning that God lacks neither power nor ability. A "short hand" symbolized powerlessness in Old Testament thought, while a "stretched-out arm" represented divine power and intervention.

The word **"mafgia"** (מַפְגִּיעַ) in verse 16, translated as "intercessor" or "mediator," describes someone who steps in between or intervenes on behalf of others. This term takes on messianic significance in Isaiah 53:12, where the suffering servant "makes intercession for transgressors." God’s surprise that no such person exists underscores the need for divine intervention.

## Events and Customs

The image of God putting on **armor** in verse 17 (breastplate, helmet, garments of vengeance, cloak of zeal) draws from Old Testament traditions concerning warrior kings. In the ancient Near East, kings were also military leaders, and gods were often depicted as warriors. Here YHWH is presented as the divine warrior who must fight the battle Himself because no human champion can be found. Paul later applies this imagery to the Christian in Ephesians 6:14–17.

The **serpent imagery** in verse 5 (hebr. *tsif'oni* – viper, and *ef'eh* – adder) was well known in Near Eastern cultures. Serpents symbolized evil, deceit, and deadly danger. The image of hatching serpent eggs and weaving spider webs illustrates how evil produces more evil and how human "work" apart from God is worthless and dangerous.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The chapter concludes with covenant language in verse 21 containing the formulation **"my Spirit who is upon you, and my words that I have put in your mouth"**. This linking of Spirit and Word within a covenant context has parallels in Hittite and Assyrian vassal treaties, where the king’s words were to be preserved and passed on to future generations. The eternal covenant extending to "your offspring and your offspring’s offspring" reflects formulations known from royal decrees in the ancient Near East, but here they are given a spiritual and eternal dimension that surpasses political agreements.
