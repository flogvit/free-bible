# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 16

## Places

**Caesarea Philippi** (v. 13) was a city at the foot of Mount Hermon, about 40 kilometers north of the Sea of Galilee. The city was originally called Paneas, named after the Greek god Pan, who was worshipped in a cave near the city's cliffs. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of a large temple dedicated to Pan and a shrine for the imperial cult. Herod the Great's son Philip (the tetrarch) expanded the city and renamed it Caesarea Philippi in honor of Emperor Augustus and himself. The fact that Jesus asked about his identity precisely here—surrounded by pagan worship and imperial cult—gives Peter's confession a strong symbolic contrast.

## People

**Simon Peter** plays a prominent role in this chapter. Jesus uses the Aramaic name "Barjona" (Βαριωνᾶ, v. 17), which means "son of Jonah." Some manuscripts and John 1:42 have "son of John." This patronym was common practice in Jewish naming. Peter's dual role in the chapter—from receiving blessing (vv. 17–19) to being rebuked as "Satan" (Σατανᾶ) and a "stumbling block" (σκάνδαλον, v. 23)—illustrates his complex character in the gospel tradition.

## Events and Customs

**The alliance of the Pharisees and Sadducees** (v. 1) is remarkable because these groups were normally theological opponents. The Pharisees believed in the resurrection and oral tradition, while the Sadducees rejected both and were linked to the temple aristocracy. That they act together against Jesus shows how threatening they perceived his activity to be.

**Leaven as a metaphor** (vv. 6, 11–12) builds on Jewish tradition where leaven often symbolized moral or doctrinal corruption, especially known from Passover's requirement for unleavened bread. Jesus uses this image to warn against false teaching.

## Key Terms

**"Sign from heaven" (σημεῖον ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ)** in verse 1 refers to the demand for a cosmic miracle as proof of divine authority. Jesus rejects this and instead points to "the sign of Jonah" (τὸ σημεῖον Ἰωνᾶ, v. 4), an allusion to the prophet Jonah's three days in the belly of the fish as a foreshadowing of Jesus' death and resurrection.

**"Christos" (ὁ χριστός)** in verse 16 is the Greek word for the Hebrew "Messiah" (the Anointed One). In Jewish expectation, the Messiah was primarily a political-military liberator from the line of David. Peter's confession was correct, but Jesus' immediate prediction of suffering (v. 21) shows that the understanding of the Messiah had to be redefined.

**"Ecclesia" (ἐκκλησία)** in verse 18 is the first time this word appears in the Gospels. In a Greek context, it denoted an assembly of citizens. In the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament), it translated the Hebrew "qahal," which referred to Israel's assembly before God. Jesus thus uses a term with deep biblical resonance.

**"Bind and loose" (δήσῃς/λύσῃς, v. 19)** were technical terms in rabbinic language for declaring something forbidden or permitted, or for excluding and including someone from the community. This gave Peter teaching and judging authority in the future church.

**"The gates of Hades" (πύλαι ᾅδου)** in verse 18 refer to the ancient conception of the realm of the dead as a fortified city with gates. The expression also appears in Greek literature and in the Old Testament (Isa 38:10; Job 38:17).

**"Take up his cross" (ἀράτω τὸν σταυρὸν αὐτοῦ)** in verse 24 uses an image familiar to the listeners: those condemned to death often carried the crossbeam to the place of crucifixion. Jesus uses this as a metaphor for radical self-denial and willingness to follow him even if it costs one's life.

**"Psyche" (ψυχή)** in verses 25–26 can be translated both "life" and "soul." The wordplay in these verses concerns the fact that whoever clings to their earthly life will lose eternal life, while whoever gives up their life for Jesus' sake will find true life.
