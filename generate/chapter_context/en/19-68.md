# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 68

## Places

Psalm 68 mentions several geographically and theologically significant places. **Sinai** (v. 9, 18) refers to the mountain where God revealed Himself to Israel and gave the law, and the psalm connects this foundational event with God's continued presence among His people. **Bashan** (v. 16, 23) was a fertile highland region east of the Jordan, known for its mighty mountains and fat livestock. The psalm contrasts Bashan's impressive mountain peaks with the mountain God has chosen as His dwelling—traditionally understood to be Zion. **Tsalmon** (צַלְמוֹן, v. 15) is possibly the mountain mentioned in Judges 9:48 near Shechem, but its identification remains uncertain. The name may mean "dark" or "shadow," and the context speaks of snow falling on Tsalmon when God scatters kings. The reference to **Egypt and Cush** (v. 32) points toward the great African nations expected to bring gifts to Israel's God. **Jerusalem** (v. 30) is mentioned as the place where kings shall bring gifts to God's temple.

## Events and Customs

The opening words of the psalm (v. 2) quote directly from Numbers 10:35, the words spoken when the ark of the covenant set out during the wilderness journey. This links the psalm to the ark and its role as a symbol of God's presence. The description of a procession (vv. 25–28) with singers, musicians, and young women with tambourines reflects Israelite festival practices, possibly connected to the ark's entry into Jerusalem or an annual feast-day procession. The listing of the tribes Benjamin, Judah, Zebulun, and Naphtali (v. 28) represents both the south (Benjamin, Judah) and the north (Zebulun, Naphtali), suggesting a united nation in worship.

## Key Terms

The expression **"He who rides on the clouds"** (בָּעֲרָבוֹת, *ba'aravot*, v. 5) is remarkable because the same title is used for the Canaanite god Baal in Ugaritic texts. The psalm polemically transfers this title to Israel's God. The rare word **שִׁנְאָן** (*shin'an*, v. 18) is uncertain. Possible interpretations include "thousands" (from שָׁנָה, to repeat), "warriors," or a loanword. The LXX translates it as "thousands of the wealthy." The term **יָהּ** (*Yah*, vv. 5, 19) is a short form of God's name YHWH and appears twice in this psalm—an unusually high number that underscores the psalm's intense focus on God's name and identity.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (ca. 1400–1200 B.C.) have shed significant light on Psalm 68. The title "He who rides on the clouds" (*rkb 'rpt*) is used repeatedly for Baal in these texts. This shows that the psalm deliberately uses Canaanite religious language to proclaim that it is YHWH, not Baal, who is the true sky god with power over storms and fertility. Such parallels help us understand the polemical dimension of Israelite worship.
