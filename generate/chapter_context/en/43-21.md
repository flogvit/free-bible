## People

**Simon Peter** stands central in this chapter, especially in the restoration conversation with Jesus (vv. 15-17). Peter's triple affirmation of his love for Jesus mirrors his triple denial before the crucifixion (John 18:15-18, 25-27). The chapter also mentions **Nathanael from Cana in Galilee** (Ναθαναὴλ ὁ ἀπὸ Κανὰ τῆς Γαλιλαίας, v. 2), who is mentioned only in the Gospel of John (1:45-49) and has traditionally been identified with Bartholomew in the other Gospels. **The sons of Zebedee** (οἱ τοῦ Ζεβεδαίου, v. 2) are James and John, who were fishermen by this sea before being called as disciples.

## Places

**The Sea of Tiberias** (θάλασσα τῆς Τιβεριάδος, v. 1) is the same as the Sea of Galilee or Lake Gennesaret. The name "Sea of Tiberias" comes from the city of Tiberias on the western shore, founded by Herod Antipas around AD 20 in honor of Emperor Tiberius.

## Events and Customs

Fishing at night (v. 3) was common practice on the Sea of Galilee because many fish species, especially tilapia (Arabic: musht), are more active and easier to catch in the dark. Night fishing with cast nets or drift nets was an established technique among Galilean fishermen. The meal Jesus prepares on the shore (vv. 9-13) – grilled fish and bread over charcoal fire (ἀνθρακιά) – reflects typical Galilean diet. The Greek word ἀνθρακιά appears only here and in 18:18 (during Peter's denial), creating a deliberate literary connection between the two scenes.

## Key Terms

The conversation between Jesus and Peter (vv. 15-17) uses two different Greek words for love: **ἀγαπάω** (agapaō) and **φιλέω** (phileō). Jesus asks twice with ἀγαπᾷς με ("do you love me?"), while Peter answers with φιλῶ σε. The third time, Jesus uses φιλεῖς, Peter's own word. Some interpret this as a meaningful nuance – either that Peter does not dare to claim the highest form of love, or that the words are used as synonyms for literary variation, which was common in Koine Greek.

The expressions **βόσκε τὰ ἀρνία μου** ("feed my lambs") and **ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατά μου** ("tend my sheep") use shepherd terminology deeply rooted in Jewish leadership understanding (cf. Ezek 34, Ps 23). The distinction between ἀρνία (lambs) and πρόβατα (sheep) may indicate care for the entire flock – both new and mature believers.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The number **153 fish** (v. 11) has generated many interpretations throughout history. Jerome (c. 347-420) claimed that Greek zoologists believed there were 153 species of fish, but this is not confirmed in ancient sources. Mathematically, 153 is a triangular number (the sum of 1-17) and was known in Pythagorean tradition. Others see it as a symbol of universality – all nations gathered in the church's net. Most likely, it is an eyewitness detail confirming the historicity of the narrative.
