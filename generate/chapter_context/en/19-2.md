# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 2

## People

The psalm mentions **kings** and **rulers** (*roznim*) who unite against the Lord and his **anointed one** (*mashiach*). The anointed one primarily refers to the Davidic king in Jerusalem. In ancient Israel, kings were installed through anointing with oil, a ceremony that marked divine selection and authorization. This term later became central to messianic expectations. The psalm's dramatic structure presents several "voices": the rebellion of the nations (vv. 1–3), God’s response from heaven (vv. 4–6), the king’s proclamation of the divine decree (vv. 7–9), and a wisdom exhortation to the rulers of the earth (vv. 10–12).

## Places

**Zion** (צִיּוֹן) is mentioned as "my holy mountain" where God has installed his king (v. 6). Zion was originally the name of the Jebusite fortress that David captured, but it later came to include the temple hill and all of Jerusalem. The place represented God’s earthly throne and was central to Davidic kingship ideology. Archaeological excavations in the City of David have confirmed significant settlement and fortifications from the Iron Age.

## Events and Customs

The psalm likely reflects a **coronation or enthronement ceremony** in which the king is proclaimed as God’s "son" (v. 7). This language has parallels in Egyptian and Mesopotamian royal installation rituals, where the king was regarded as the deity’s representative or adopted son. The phrase "Today I have begotten you" suggests a ritual "birth" into kingship on the day of coronation. This adoption formula established a special covenant relationship between God and the Davidic king, in line with the promise in 2 Samuel 7:14.

## Key Terms

**Mashiach** (מָשִׁיחַ, "the anointed one") in verse 2 is the basis for the Greek "Christos" and the Norwegian "Messias". In its original context, it referred to the reigning Davidic king, but during the intertestamental period it became increasingly associated with a future savior figure.

**Nasakh** (נָסַכְתִּי, "I have installed", v. 6) literally means "to pour out" or "to shed", possibly referring to anointing oil. The word can also mean to cast a metal image, emphasizing the king’s role as God’s "image" or representative on earth.

The expression **"kiss the son"** (*nashqu-bar*, v. 12) has been much debated, since *bar* is the Aramaic word for "son". Some understand it as an act of submission to the king, where kissing the feet or hands was a common form of homage to rulers in the ancient Near East.
