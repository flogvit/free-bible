# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 15

## People

**James** (v. 13) is James, the brother of Jesus, who at this time appears as the leader of the church in Jerusalem. He was known for his piety and, according to the Jewish historian Josephus, was called "the righteous." His authoritative role in the meeting reflects his position as the primary leader of the mother church. The fact that he delivers the final judgment (κρίνω, "I judge/decide") demonstrates his leading status. Notably, he uses the Semitic name form Συμεών (Simeon) when referring to Peter (v. 14), which reflects his Jewish background.

**Judas Barsabbas and Silas** (v. 22) were leading men (ἡγουμένους) in the Jerusalem church. Silas (Latin form: Silvanus) likely held Roman citizenship, making him a valuable travel companion. He is described as a prophet (v. 32) and later became Paul's coworker. The name "Barsabbas" means "son of the Sabbath" and may indicate membership in the same family as Joseph Barsabbas (Acts 1:23).

## Places

**Antioch in Syria** (vv. 22–23, 30, 35) was the third largest city in the Roman Empire after Rome and Alexandria, with a population of perhaps 500,000. The city lay on the Orontes River, about 25 km from the Mediterranean Sea. It had a significant Jewish population with certain civic rights. Antioch became a natural center for Gentile mission work due to its cosmopolitan character and strategic location on trade routes between east and west.

**Phoenicia and Samaria** (v. 3) formed the travel route from Antioch to Jerusalem. Phoenicia included the coastal cities of Tyre and Sidon, areas with both Jewish and Gentile inhabitants. The fact that the delegation reported on the conversion of the Gentiles and caused great joy shows that mixed churches already existed along this route.

**Cyprus** (v. 39) was the island in the eastern Mediterranean to which Barnabas and Mark sailed after their disagreement with Paul. Cyprus was Barnabas's homeland (Acts 4:36), which explains the choice of destination. The island had a significant Jewish population and had already been a target for mission work (Acts 13:4–12).

## Events and Customs

**The Apostolic Council** (ca. AD 49–50) represents the first known church assembly to take a stand on a doctrinal issue. The structure of debate (ζήτησις), testimony, and authoritative decision became a model for later church councils. The meeting addressed the fundamental question of whether Gentiles had to become Jews (through circumcision and law observance) in order to be saved.

**James's Scriptural Argument** (vv. 15–18) builds on a quotation from Amos 9:11–12 according to the Septuagint version. The prophecy concerning the rebuilding of "the fallen tent of David" (τὴν σκηνὴν Δαυὶδ τὴν πεπτωκυῖαν) is interpreted as fulfilled through the inclusion of Gentiles in God's people. The Septuagint text differs from the Hebrew and fits James's argument better.

**The Four Prohibitions** (vv. 20, 29) – food offered to idols (εἰδωλοθύτων), blood (αἵματος), what has been strangled (πνικτῶν), and sexual immorality (πορνείας) – have been interpreted in various ways. A common understanding is that they reflect the so-called "Noahide laws" or rules for "strangers residing in the land" (Lev 17–18). These regulations applied to non-Jews living among the Israelites. Practically, they made table fellowship between Jewish Christians and Gentile Christians possible, which was crucial for the unity of the church.

## Key Terms

**Περιτομή (circumcision)** was not merely a religious ritual but the very sign of the covenant with Abraham (Gen 17). For the Pharisees who had come to believe (v. 5), circumcision was inseparable from salvation – it marked inclusion in God's people. The question was therefore not only ritual but soteriological: Can Gentiles be saved without becoming Jews?

**Ἀλισγήματα τῶν εἰδώλων** (v. 20, "food offered to idols") refers to meat that had been sacrificed to idols and then sold in the market or eaten at cultic meals. In a city like Antioch such food was everywhere, and abstaining from it required conscious effort.

**Παροξυσμός** (v. 39, "sharp disagreement") describes the conflict between Paul and Barnabas concerning John Mark. The Greek word means an intense, distressing emotion. Luke does not hide conflicts among church leaders but also shows how the division resulted in two mission teams instead of one.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Verse 34** appears in some manuscripts with the wording "But Silas decided to stay there" (ἔδοξε δὲ τῷ Σίλᾳ ἐπιμεῖναι αὐτοῦ). This verse is missing from the oldest and most reliable manuscripts and is regarded by most textual critics as a later addition to explain how Silas was available in Antioch in v. 40.
