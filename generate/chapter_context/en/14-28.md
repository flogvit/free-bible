# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 28

## People

**Ahaz** reigned as king of Judah from approximately 732–716 B.C. and is one of the most negatively portrayed kings in the books of Chronicles. His name (אָחָז, 'Achaz) means "he has grasped/held fast" and is likely a shortened form of Jehoahaz ("the Lord has grasped"). Assyrian inscriptions from Tiglath-Pileser III mention him as "Jehoahaz of Judah" (Yauhazi) who paid tribute. King **Pekah son of Remaliah** ruled the northern kingdom of Israel (ca. 752–732 B.C.) and, together with King Rezin of Aram, led an attack against Judah known as the Syro-Ephraimite War. **Oded** is a prophet otherwise unknown in the Old Testament, but his role illustrates that prophetic activity continued in the northern kingdom even during a period marked by apostasy.

**Tiglath-Pileser III** (Hebrew: תִּלְּגַת פִּלְנְאֶסֶר, Tillegat-Pilneser) was the Assyrian king who dominated the Middle East from 745–727 B.C. His annals confirm contact with Ahaz and the tribute paid by Judah. The four Ephraimite leaders mentioned in verse 12—Azariah son of Johanan, Berechiah son of Meshillemoth, Hezekiah son of Shallum, and Amasa son of Hadlai—represent a conscientious remnant in the otherwise apostate northern kingdom.

## Places

**The Valley of Hinnom** (גֵּיא בֶן־הִנֹּם, "Ben-Hinnom's valley") lay just south of Jerusalem and was used for idolatrous worship, including child sacrifice. The place later became so notorious that the Greek word "Gehenna" (hell) derives from it. Archaeological investigations have uncovered traces of cultic sites in the area.

**Jericho** (יְרֵחוֹ), referred to as the "City of Palms," was located at an important crossroads in the Jordan Valley and served as a natural boundary region between north and south. Bringing the captives here represented a symbolic return to the border without requiring the Ephraimites themselves to enter Judah's territory.

**Beth-Shemesh, Aijalon, Gederot, Socoh, Timnah, and Gimzo** are mentioned among the cities captured by the Philistines (verse 18). These were located in the Shephelah (lowland) between the coastal plain and the highlands of Judah, a strategically important area that controlled trade routes between the coast and Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** (verse 3: וַיַּבְעֵר אֶת־בָּנָיו בָּאָשׁ, "he burned his sons in the fire") was a practice associated with the Canaanite god Molech. The books of Chronicles use a stronger expression here than the parallel account in 2 Kings 16:3. Both Assyrian and Phoenician sources confirm that such rituals occurred in the region, representing the ultimate deviation from Yahweh worship.

**The release of captives** (verses 8–15) describes a remarkable event where the northern kingdom freed captives from Judah following the prophet Oded's exhortation. The text notes that 200,000 women, sons, and daughters were taken captive. The detailed care described—providing clothing, food, drink, anointing oil, and transporting the weak on donkeys—reflects obligations later codified in rabbinic thought regarding the treatment of captives and enemies.

## Key Terms

**מַעַל** (ma'al) – "treachery" or "faithlessness" – is used repeatedly concerning Ahaz (verses 19, 22) and is a key term in Chronicles for describing breaches of the covenant relationship with God. The concept carries connotations of embezzlement or misuse of something belonging to another, here God's holy things and trust.

**הִכְנִיעַ** (hikhnia', verse 19) – "to humble" – is used concerning God's judgment upon Judah. Interestingly, Ahaz is referred to here as "king of Israel," likely an ironic comment that he behaved like the wicked kings of the northern kingdom rather than as a Davidic king.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Tiglath-Pileser III's annals, preserved on clay tablets found in Nimrud (ancient Calah), specifically mention tribute from "Jehoahaz of Judah." This confirms the biblical account that Ahaz sought Assyrian help and paid with temple treasures. The inscriptions also describe campaigns against Aram-Damascus and Israel, which form the backdrop for the military conflicts in this chapter. One text lists goods and wealth received from vassal kings, corresponding to the chapter's description of how Ahaz plundered the temple to pay the Assyrian king.
