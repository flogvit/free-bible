# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 7

## People

**The Pharisees and the scribes from Jerusalem** (v. 1) represent an official delegation sent to investigate Jesus' ministry. The fact that they came from Jerusalem underscores the seriousness of the situation – these were not local critics, but representatives from the religious center. The Pharisees were known for their exact observance of both the written Torah and the oral tradition ("the tradition of the elders").

**The Syrophoenician woman** (v. 26) is described with a dual identity: "Greek" (Ἑλληνίς) refers to her culture and religion (Gentile), while "Syrophoenician" indicates her ethnic origin from the Phoenician coast in the Roman province of Syria. This distinguishes her from Libyphoenicians in North Africa. As a Gentile woman, she represented a double barrier within the social norms of that time.

## Places

**The region of Tyre and Sidon** (v. 24, 31) consisted of ancient Phoenician port cities north of Galilee, located in Gentile territory. Tyre had a long history of both trade and tensions with Israel (cf. Ezek 26-28). Jesus' journey there marks his first expansion of ministry into non-Jewish areas.

**The Decapolis** (v. 31) was a federation of ten Greco-Roman cities east and south of the Sea of Galilee. The area was characterized by Hellenistic culture, with temples, theaters, and gymnasiums. The population was predominantly Gentile, although Jewish minorities existed. The route Jesus takes – from Tyre via Sidon to the Decapolis – is geographically unusual (a detour northward before heading southeast), which may indicate a deliberate ministry in Gentile regions.

## Events and Customs

**The handwashing ritual** (v. 3-4) was primarily not hygienic but ritualistic. The expression πυγμῇ (literally "with the fist") likely describes a specific washing method, although its exact meaning is debated among scholars. The Pharisees had expanded purity laws that originally applied to priests (Exod 30:19-21) to apply to all Jews in daily life. The later Mishnah tractate Yadayim ("Hands") contains detailed rules for this type of practice. Washing after visiting the marketplace (ἀγορᾶς) was due to fear of contact with unclean persons or objects.

**The Korban practice** (v. 11-13) illustrates a legal mechanism by which some could declare property as "a gift to God" (קָרְבָּן). According to rabbinic tradition, this made the property unavailable for other purposes, even though it could still be used by the one who made the vow. Jesus criticizes how this could be misused to avoid the duty of supporting parents, in violation of the fifth commandment.

**The healing rituals** (v. 33) – using saliva and touching body parts – were known healing techniques in antiquity. Both Greek and Jewish sources mention saliva as having healing properties. What is unique here is the combination with prayer and the Aramaic command word.

## Key Terms

**Κοινός** ("unclean," v. 2, 5, 15, 18, 20, 23) literally means "common" or "ordinary," but is used here in the sense of "ritually unclean" – the opposite of holy or set apart. This term was central to Jewish purity thinking and marked the boundary between what was acceptable for fellowship with God and what was not.

**Παράδοσις τῶν πρεσβυτέρων** ("the tradition of the elders," v. 3, 5) refers to the oral Torah – interpretive traditions that the Pharisees believed were given to Moses at Sinai along with the written law. This tradition was later written down in the Mishnah (around 200 AD).

**Εφφαθα** (v. 34) is Aramaic (אֶתְפְּתַח) and means "be opened." Mark preserves Jesus' own language and translates it for his Greek-speaking readers, just as he does with "korban" (v. 11). This gives the text authenticity and shows Jesus' native tongue.

**Κυνάριον** ("little dog," v. 27-28) is the diminutive form of "dog." While "dog" was often a derogatory term for Gentiles, the diminutive form refers to household pets – an important nuance that the woman grasps in her reply.
