# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Songs 5

## Characters

**Daughters of Jerusalem** (בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלַם, *benot Yerushalayim*) play an active role in this chapter. They respond to the bride's plea in verse 8 with the question, "What is your beloved better than another beloved?" (verse 9), which prompts the detailed description of the bridegroom.

**Watchmen** (הַשֹּׁמְרִים, *hashomrim*) are the city police who patrol at night. In contrast to their milder mention in chapter 3, here they treat the woman violently and take her veil.

## Places

The chapter mentions **the city** (עִיר, *'ir*) where watchmen patrol, as well as **the walls of the city** (חֹמוֹת, *chomot*). In ancient Israel, cities were surrounded by defensive walls, and night guards patrolled the streets to maintain order and security. Jerusalem and other fortified cities had organized guard systems that covered both the streets within the walls and the walls themselves. The woman encountering watchmen at night reflects a realistic urban setting from the Iron Age, where women moving alone after dark could be met with suspicion.

## Events and Customs

The description of **the feast** in verse 1—with honey, wine, and milk in a garden—reflects ancient intimate banquets. The invitation, "Eat, friends; drink deeply of love" (אִכְלוּ רֵעִים שְׁתוּ וְשִׁכְרוּ דּוֹדִים), expresses the generous hospitality that characterized such occasions in the ancient Near East.

The woman's **wasf** description of the bridegroom (verses 10–16) represents a well-known custom in Oriental love poetry. This detailed physical praise, moving from head to toe, has parallels in Egyptian love poetry from the New Kingdom, where lovers are described through poetic comparisons with precious materials and images of nature.

## Key Terms

**מוֹר עֹבֵר** (*mor 'over*, "flowing myrrh") in verses 5 and 13 refers to the finest quality of myrrh—that which drips naturally from the tree without incision. This was an extremely expensive perfume imported from South Arabia and East Africa, used both as cosmetics and in religious contexts.

**רְדִיד** (*redid*, "veil" or "shawl") in verse 7 was an outer garment worn by women, especially at night or on special occasions. The watchmen taking it from her represented both physical and social humiliation.

**תַּרְשִׁישׁ** (*tarshish*) in verse 14 likely refers to chrysolite or topaz—a yellow-green gemstone. The place Tarshish (possibly in Spain or Sardinia) was known as a source of valuable metals and gems.

**לְבָנוֹן** (*Levanon*, "Lebanon") in verse 15 was synonymous with majesty and beauty. The mountains of Lebanon, with their famous cedar trees, represented the pinnacle of natural grandeur.
