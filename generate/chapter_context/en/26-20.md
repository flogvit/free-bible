## People

The chapter opens with "some of the elders of Israel" (זִקְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל) coming to Ezekiel to seek the Lord's counsel (v. 1). This is the third time in the book that elders visit the prophet (cf. 8:1; 14:1). The elders served as representatives of the Jewish exilic community in Babylon and were responsible for maintaining social order and providing religious guidance. Their coming "to seek the Lord" (לִדְרֹשׁ אֶת־יְהוָה) suggests they desired an oracle or prophetic answer to a specific matter, but God refused to be consulted by them because of the people's persistent sin.

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical and theological locations. **Egypt** (מִצְרַיִם) is presented as the place where Israel's rebellion against God began—an unusual perspective compared to other biblical texts that often idealize the wilderness period. The **wilderness** (הַמִּדְבָּר) is described as the scene of repeated rebellion across two generations (vv. 10–26). **"The wilderness of the peoples"** (מִדְבַּר הָעַמִּים, v. 35) is a theological concept pointing toward a future purification of the people before restoration. **"My holy mountain, the high mountain of Israel"** (הַר־קָדְשִׁי... הַר מְרוֹם יִשְׂרָאֵל, v. 40) refers to Zion/Jerusalem as the future center for true worship.

## Events and Customs

The chapter mentions several cultic practices that God condemns. **High-place worship** (בָּמָה, vv. 28–29) involved offering sacrifices at open sacred sites, often associated with Canaanite religion. Archaeological excavations have uncovered numerous such high places in Israel, complete with altars, steles, and cultic objects. The wordplay in v. 29—"What is this high place (בָּמָה) you are going to?"—may be a folk etymological explanation of the name.

**Child sacrifice** is mentioned in vv. 26 and 31 with the expression "to make all your firstborn pass through the fire" (בְּהַעֲבִיר כָּל־פֶּטֶר רָחַם). This likely refers to Molech worship, documented both in biblical texts (2 Kgs 23:10; Jer 32:35) and in Phoenician and Punic inscriptions concerning *tophet* sanctuaries. Verses 25–26 are theologically challenging: God says he "gave them statutes that were not good"—probably a reference to how the people perverted the commandment regarding the firstborn (Exod 13:2) to include child sacrifice.

## Key Terms

**"I lifted my hand"** (נָשָׂאתִי יָדִי, vv. 5, 6, 15, 23) is an oath formula denoting a solemn covenant declaration. The expression occurs seven times in the chapter and emphasizes God's faithful promises contrasted with the people's unfaithfulness.

**"For my name's sake"** (לְמַעַן שְׁמִי, vv. 9, 14, 22, 44) is a central theological motif. God's "name" represents his reputation and honor among the nations. Israel's salvation is not based on their merit but on God's willingness to protect his name from being "profaned" (חִלֵּל) among the Gentiles.

**"Idols"** (גִּלּוּלִים) is a derogatory term that appears 39 times in Ezekiel—more than in the rest of the Old Testament combined. The word is likely derived from a root meaning "dung" or "filth," expressing deep contempt for idolatry.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The dating in v. 1—"in the seventh year, in the fifth month, on the tenth day"—corresponds to August 591 BC, about six years before Jerusalem's fall. Babylonian chronicles confirm that this was a period of relative stability within the exilic community, which may explain why the elders sought guidance about the future. Ezekiel's review of Israel's history from Egypt to his own time represents a "counter-narrative" to the traditional salvation history—instead of highlighting God's mighty acts, it emphasizes the people's persistent rebellion across four generations (the Egypt generation, the wilderness generation, their children in the wilderness, and the conquest generation).
