# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 9

## People

**Saul/Paul of Tarsus** (Σαῦλος, Ταρσέα, v. 11) is introduced here as a persecutor of Christians and undergoes his dramatic conversion. He was a Pharisee with Roman citizenship from Tarsus, a significant city in Cilicia.

**Ananias in Damascus** (Ἁνανίας, v. 10) was a Jewish disciple (μαθητής) whom the Lord spoke to in a vision and sent to heal and baptize Saul. His presence testifies that a Christian community already existed in Damascus, likely established by Jews who had heard the gospel in Jerusalem.

**Judas in Damascus** (Ἰούδα, v. 11) was the homeowner on Straight Street where Saul stayed after his experience on the road to Damascus.

**Barnabas** (Βαρναβᾶς, v. 27) was a Levite from Cyprus (see Acts 4:36) who introduced the newly converted Saul to the apostles in Jerusalem when other disciples feared him. His role as a bridge-builder was crucial for Saul's acceptance within the church.

**Aeneas** (Αἰνέας, v. 33-34) was a man in Lydda who had been bedridden and paralyzed for eight years. Peter healed him in the name of Jesus Christ.

**Tabitha/Dorcas** (Ταβιθά/Δορκάς, v. 36) in Joppa was a female disciple (μαθήτρια – the only place this noun is used in the New Testament). Her double name – Tabitha (Aramaic for "gazelle") and Dorcas (Greek translation) – indicates a bilingual Jewish-Christian context. She is described as a benefactor who made clothing for widows, an important deacon-like function in the early church.

**Simon the tanner** (Σίμωνι βυρσεῖ, v. 43) was Peter's host in Joppa. It is noteworthy that Peter stayed with a tanner, since the tanning trade was considered unclean due to contact with dead animals. Tanners had to live outside city walls, near water. This detail prepares for Peter's upcoming openness toward Gentiles in chapter 10.

## Places

**Damascus** was one of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, with a large Jewish population organized into several synagogues. The city lay about 240 km northeast of Jerusalem and at this time was under Nabatean influence, as seen from the fact that King Aretas' governor guarded the city (2 Cor 11:32). "The Straight Street" (ῥύμην τὴν καλουμένην Εὐθεῖαν, v. 11) was the city's main street running east-west through the center – a typical Hellenistic urban plan. The street still exists in Damascus today.

**Lydda** (Λύδδα, v. 32, modern Lod) lay about 40 km northwest of Jerusalem on the coastal plain, at an important crossroads. The city had a significant Jewish population and served as an administrative center. **The Plain of Sharon** (Σαρῶνα, v. 35) mentioned was the fertile coastal area between Joppa and Caesarea.

**Joppa** (Ἰόππῃ, v. 36, modern Jaffa/Tel Aviv) was Judea's most important port city with a long history dating back to Egyptian times. The city had a mixed population of Jews and Gentiles.

**Caesarea** (Καισάρειαν, v. 30) was the Roman capital of the province of Judea, featuring a large harbor built by Herod the Great. Saul was sent there by the brothers before he continued on to Tarsus.

## Events and Customs

**The high priest's jurisdiction** over Diaspora Jews (vv. 1-2) reflects the authority the Sanhedrin held over Jewish communities outside Judea. Introductory letters to synagogues granted the right to arrest and extradite suspects to Jerusalem for trial. This practice was recognized by Roman authorities as part of Jewish religious self-governance.

**Escape in a basket through the city wall** (v. 25) was a known escape method in ancient cities. Houses were often built into the city walls with windows facing outward. Damascus' massive walls had such dwellings. Paul himself mentions this event in 2 Cor 11:32-33, where he specifies that it was King Aretas' governor who guarded the city – a detail that helps date the period to 37-40 AD.

**Washing and placing the dead in an upper room** (v. 37) followed Jewish burial customs. The body was washed, anointed, and placed in the home's upper floor before burial, which normally occurred on the same day. That Tabitha's body was preserved longer suggests that the community held hope for intervention.

## Key Terms

**"The Way"** (τῆς ὁδοῦ, v. 2) was an early self-designation for the Christian movement. The expression alludes to "the way of the Lord" from Isaiah and emphasizes Christianity as a manner of life and journey, not merely a doctrine. The term is used several times in Acts (19:9, 23; 22:4; 24:14, 22).

**"A chosen vessel"** (σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς, v. 15) is a Semitic expression meaning "chosen instrument/vessel". The image of humans as vessels God uses also appears in the Old Testament (Isa 10:5; Jer 18). The term defines Paul's mission: to carry Jesus' name to Gentiles, kings, and the children of Israel.

**"The saints"** (τοὺς ἁγίους, vv. 13, 32, 41) is used here as a designation for Christians, emphasizing their status as God's chosen people.

**"Hellenists"** (Ἑλληνιστάς, v. 29) refers here to Greek-speaking Jews, the same group Stephen came from and debated with (Acts 6:9). That Paul now preached to those he had previously allied with created intense confrontation and a direct threat to his life.
