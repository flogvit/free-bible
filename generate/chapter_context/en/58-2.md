# Historical and Cultural Context for Hebrews 2

## Key Concepts

### The Law Delivered Through Angels (v. 2)
The expression "ὁ δι᾽ ἀγγέλων λαληθεὶς λόγος" (the word spoken through angels) refers to a widespread Jewish tradition that the Mosaic law was given through the mediation of angels at Sinai. This tradition is found, among other places, in the Septuagint version of Deuteronomy 33:2, in the Book of Jubilees (1:27–29), in the writings of Philo of Alexandria, and in Josephus. The New Testament also confirms this belief (Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19). For Jewish-Christian readers, this underscored the high authority of the law—and thus how much greater the message of salvation in Christ must be.

### "Signs and Wonders" (v. 4)
The phrase "σημείοις τε καὶ τέρασιν" (signs and wonders) is a fixed formula with roots in the Old Testament, especially connected to the exodus from Egypt (Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34). In Jewish tradition, such signs marked God’s confirmation of His message and His messengers. Here the formula is used to show that the apostles’ preaching carried the same divine authorization as the Mosaic tradition.

### "The World to Come" (v. 5)
The expression "τὴν οἰκουμένην τὴν μέλλουσαν" (the world to come) reflects the Jewish apocalyptic two-fold division between "this age" (olam hazeh) and "the age to come" (olam haba). In Jewish eschatology, it was common to speculate about the role of angels in the coming age. The author rejects the idea that angels will rule over this reality, instead attributing dominion to the "Son of Man"—a title with clear messianic overtones from Daniel 7.

### "Abraham’s Offspring" (v. 16)
The expression "σπέρματος Ἀβραὰμ" (Abraham’s offspring) had deep resonance for Jewish readers. It connected to the covenant promises in Genesis and the concept of Israel as God’s chosen people. The author uses this to emphasize that Christ’s salvation applies to the covenant people—not angels—while at the same time, in the early Christian context, the term was expanded to include all who believe (cf. Galatians 3:29).

## Events and Customs

### The High Priest’s Atonement Function (v. 17)
The verse introduces a central theme in Hebrews: Jesus as "ἀρχιερεύς" (high priest). In the Jewish temple system, the high priest was the only one who could enter the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) to make atonement (ἱλάσκεσθαι) for the people’s sins. The ritual is described in Leviticus 16. This comparison between Jesus and the Levitical high priest was immediately relevant and potentially controversial for Jewish-Christian readers.

### Fear of Death and Slavery (vv. 14–15)
The reference to those who "φόβῳ θανάτου διὰ παντὸς τοῦ ζῆν ἔνοχοι ἦσαν δουλείας" (through fear of death were subject to slavery all their lives) reflects both Jewish and Greco-Roman ideas. In the Hellenistic world, death was often associated with a shadowy existence in Hades. Among Jews there were varying views on life after death, ranging from the Sadducees’ denial of resurrection to the Pharisees’ belief in it. The devil’s power over death (v. 14) connects with apocalyptic traditions in which Satan had been granted delegated authority in this age.
