# Historical and Cultural Context for Nehemiah 6

## People

**Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem** are the main opponents who, in this chapter, use political manipulation rather than direct threats. Sanballat was the governor of Samaria, and his title "Horonite" may link him to Beth-Horon. Geshem (called Gashmu/גַשְׁמוּ in v. 6) was the leader of an Arab tribal confederation that controlled trade routes south of Judah. Tobiah, the Ammonite servant, had extensive family connections within the Jewish elite through marriage (v. 18).

**Shemaiah son of Delaiah son of Mehetabel** (v. 10) is presented as someone who was "confined" (Hebrew: עָצוּר, *'atzur*), which could mean ritual impurity, prophetic withdrawal, or house arrest. He pretends to be a prophet, but Nehemiah exposes him as bribed. **Noadiah the prophetess** (v. 14) is mentioned as one of several prophets working against Nehemiah—a rare example of a female prophet in this period, though here she plays a negative role.

**Shecaniah son of Arah** (v. 18) was Tobiah's father-in-law, and **Johanan**, Tobiah's son, had married the daughter of **Meshullam son of Berekiah**. These marriage alliances explain why many Jewish nobles had loyalty ties to Tobiah.

## Places

**Kefirim in the Valley of Ono** (v. 2) – the Hebrew בַּכְּפִירִים בְּבִקְעַת אוֹנוֹ can mean "in the villages of the Valley of Ono" or refer to a specific place called Kefirim. The area lay in the border region between Judah and Samaria, on the northwestern edge of Jewish territory. This was neutral ground outside Nehemiah's jurisdiction, making it a dangerous meeting place. That the opponents chose this location reveals their intention to lure Nehemiah away from Jerusalem's protection.

**The Temple sanctuary** (Hebrew: הֵיכָל, *heikhal*) that Shemaiah suggested hiding in (v. 10) refers to the holy area within the temple building itself. Only priests had access to this area, and Nehemiah, as a layperson, would have committed a serious sin by entering there (cf. Num 18:7).

## Events and Customs

**Open letter** (v. 5: אִגֶּרֶת פְּתוּחָה) was a deliberate diplomatic insult. In ancient times, official letters were sealed to protect their contents and the sender's honor. An open letter was meant to spread rumors publicly and pressure the recipient. The accusation that Nehemiah intended to make himself king (vv. 6–7) was extremely serious under Persian rule, since rebellion was punishable by death.

**False prophecy for payment** (vv. 12–13) was a known phenomenon in ancient Israel. The opponents tried to get Nehemiah to desecrate the temple, which would have ruined his reputation and authority. That Nehemiah recognized Shemaiah's "prophecy" as false shows his spiritual discernment.

**Four times** (v. 4: אַרְבַּע פְּעָמִים) the enemies sent the same invitation, and Nehemiah consistently gave the same answer. The fifth time (v. 5), they changed tactics with the open letter.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **"strengthen my hands"** (v. 9: חַזֵּק אֶת־יָדָי, *chazzeq et-yadai*) is a prayer for divine strength and endurance. The opposite phrase "let their hands grow weak" (יִרְפּוּ יְדֵיהֶם) describes discouragement and giving up.

**Elul** (v. 15) is the sixth month in the Jewish calendar (August–September). That the wall was completed in 52 days (לַחֲמִשִּׁים וּשְׁנַיִם יוֹם) demonstrated effective leadership, but also divine help—something even the enemies acknowledged (v. 16).

**"Those bound by oath"** (v. 18: בַּעֲלֵי שְׁבוּעָה, *ba'alei shevu'ah* – literally "lords/owners of an oath") shows that many Jewish nobles were tied to Tobiah through covenants and marriage alliances, creating conflicts of loyalty within Jerusalem.
