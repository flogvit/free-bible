# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 35

## People

**Deborah** (v. 8) was Rebekah’s nurse, mentioned only here and in Gen 24:59. Her presence with Jacob may suggest that Rebekah had already died, or that Deborah had joined Jacob’s household at an earlier time. In the ancient Near East, a nurse’s role extended far beyond infancy; they often remained trusted family members for life. The fact that her death and burial are specifically recorded, and that the place was named "Allon-Bachuth" (the oak of weeping), shows the deep attachment the family had to her.

**Benjamin** (v. 18) is the only one of Jacob’s sons certainly born in Canaan (the others were born in Paddan-Aram, see v. 26). Rachel called him "Ben-Oni" (son of my sorrow), but Jacob changed his name to "Benjamin" (son of the right hand). In Semitic culture, the right hand was associated with strength, honor, and blessing. The name change may be understood as Jacob’s desire to turn tragedy into something positive.

## Places

**Bethel** (vv. 1, 6–7, 15) means "house of God" and was formerly known as Luz. It is located about 17 km north of Jerusalem at an elevation of approximately 900 meters. Archaeological excavations at Beitin (the traditional identification, now debated) have revealed settlement from the Middle Bronze Age (ca. 2000–1550 BCE), which coincides with the patriarchal period. Bethel was a significant cultic site in Canaan before Israelite times, which may explain why God commanded Jacob to cleanse his household of foreign gods before going there.

**Ephrath/Bethlehem** (vv. 16, 19) is identified as the place where Rachel died and was buried. The name Ephrath probably means "fruitful land." It lies about 8 km south of Jerusalem. Tradition has associated Rachel’s tomb with this area for millennia, although the current structure known as "Rachel’s Tomb" dates from a later period. **Migdal-Eder** (v. 21), meaning "tower of the flock," was a watchtower for shepherds nearby, also mentioned in Micah 4:8.

**Mamre/Kiriath-Arba/Hebron** (v. 27) was Isaac’s residence and the family burial place of the patriarchs. Kiriath-Arba ("city of Arba") was the Canaanite name, while Hebron became the later Israelite name. Archaeological findings show that Hebron was continuously inhabited throughout the Bronze Age.

## Events and Customs

**Cleansing and removal of foreign gods** (vv. 2–4) reveals that Jacob’s household had brought along divine images (teraphim) and cultic objects, possibly including the household gods (teraphim) that Rachel stole from Laban (Gen 31:19). The earrings mentioned were likely amulets with religious significance, common in Canaanite religion. The command to change clothes was part of a cleansing ritual preparing them for holy worship. Burying these objects under the oak at Shechem was a way to permanently remove them from use—a practice known from several ancient cultures.

**Drink offering and anointing of the stone pillar** (v. 14) combines two ritual actions. The drink offering (Hebrew: נֶסֶךְ, *nesekh*) was probably wine, water, or oil poured out as a gift to the deity. Anointing with oil marked the place as holy and dedicated to God. Stone pillars (*matsevot*) were widespread in Canaanite religion, but here they are used to mark the site of divine revelation.

**Reuben’s sin** (v. 22)—that he lay with Bilhah, his father’s concubine—was a serious offense that in the ancient Near East was often interpreted as an attempt to seize his father’s authority and inheritance rights. Similar actions are seen when Absalom lay with David’s concubines (2 Sam 16:21–22). This explains why Reuben later lost his birthright (Gen 49:3–4; 1 Chr 5:1).

## Key Terms

**El Shaddai** (אֵל שַׁדַּי, v. 11) is a divine name traditionally translated "God Almighty." The etymology of the name is debated—it may be linked to Akkadian *šadû* (mountain) or Hebrew *šad* (breast, as a symbol of nourishment and blessing). This name is used especially in the patriarchal narratives and is connected with promises of fertility and land.

**"A people and an assembly of peoples"** (גּוֹי וּקְהַל גּוֹיִם, v. 11) expands the promise from earlier revelations. The word *qahal* (assembly) suggests not only numerous descendants but also an organized entity—a confederation of tribes to emerge from Jacob.

**"Gathered to his people"** (וַיֵּאָסֶף אֶל־עַמָּיו, v. 29) is an idiomatic expression for death that implies continued existence with the ancestors. It is distinguished from burial itself, which is mentioned separately, and points to an early belief in life after death.
