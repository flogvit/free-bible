# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 4

## People

**Moses** is presented here with his objections to the call, revealing his self-understanding after a long period in exile. His claim of being "heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue" (כְבַד־פֶּה וּכְבַד לָשׁוֹן, v. 10) has been interpreted in various ways—as a speech defect, a lack of eloquence, or simply uncertainty after being away from Egyptian court culture for so long.

**Aaron** is introduced as Moses' brother and "the Levite" (הַלֵּוִי)—a designation that here primarily identifies his tribal affiliation rather than a cultic function, since the Levitical service had not yet been established. Aaron is described as one who "can speak" and will serve as Moses' spokesperson. This pattern of prophet and spokesperson has parallels in the ancient Near East, where kings often had official spokesmen.

**Zipporah**, Moses' Midianite wife, plays a crucial role in the mysterious episode at the lodging place (vv. 24-26). Her quick action with circumcision shows familiarity with Israelite practice, despite her non-Israelite background.

## Places

**Midian** was the area east of the Gulf of Aqaba, in northwestern Arabia. This was a trade region where caravans crossed, and the Midianites were known as merchants. Here Moses had stayed as a shepherd for Jethro.

**The mountain of God** (הַר הָאֱלֹהִים) where Aaron meets Moses (v. 27), refers to Horeb/Sinai. The fact that Aaron travels there to meet Moses shows that the mountain already had a certain status as a holy place.

## Events and Customs

**The three signs** that God gives Moses—the staff turning into a snake (vv. 2-4), the leprous hand (vv. 6-7), and water turning to blood (v. 9)—function as legitimating signs. In Egyptian culture, magic and signs were important for proving divine authority. The serpent was a well-known symbol of power in Egypt (the uraeus cobra on Pharaoh's crown), so Moses' staff becoming a snake had direct relevance to Egyptian symbolism.

**The circumcision episode** (vv. 24-26) is one of the most enigmatic texts in the Bible. The expression "bridegroom of blood" (חֲתַן־דָּמִים) appears only here in the Bible (vv. 25 and 26). The episode emphasizes the absolute necessity of circumcision—even Moses, God's chosen one, could not exempt his own son. In the ancient world, flint knives (צֹר) were traditional tools for circumcision, even after metal tools became available.

**The Israelites' reaction** of "bowing down and prostrating themselves" (וַיִּקְּדוּ וַיִּשְׁתַּחֲווּ, v. 31) describes the standard form of reverence in the ancient Near East—first bowing the head, then full prostration.

## Key Terms

**"My firstborn son"** (בְּנִי בְכֹרִי יִשְׂרָאֵל, v. 22) is a powerful metaphor for Israel's relationship with God. In the ancient Near East, the firstborn had special rights and a unique position. This language prepares for the tenth plague and establishes a theological foundation for the Passover sacrifice.

**"I will harden his heart"** (אֲחַזֵּק אֶת־לִבּוֹ, v. 21) introduces a central theme in the Exodus narrative. The Hebrew verb חזק means "to strengthen" or "make hard." In Egyptian thought, the heart was the seat of will and decisions, not emotions. This expression foreshadows the coming conflict.

**"God's staff"** (מַטֵּה הָאֱלֹהִים, v. 20) marks a transition—the shepherd's staff introduced in verses 2-4 is now explicitly called an instrument of divine power. This is the first time in the Bible that the staff receives this designation.
