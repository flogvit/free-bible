## People

### Elihu

Elihu continues his speech to Job in this chapter. He addresses Job’s claim of personal righteousness before God and argues that God is too exalted to be influenced by human actions.

## Key Concepts

### God’s Transcendence and Human Limitation

The chapter contains important concepts related to God’s exaltation. The expression "שְׁחָקִים" (shəḥaqim) in verse 5 refers to the clouds or the firmament, used poetically for God’s dwelling high above humanity. This word appears several times in wisdom literature and emphasizes the distance between the divine and the human. Elihu’s argument is that God is so exalted that human sin or righteousness cannot directly affect Him.

### Songs in the Night

The expression "זְמִרוֹת בַּלָּיְלָה" (zemirot ballaylah, "songs in the night") in verse 10 carries rich cultural resonance. In an ancient Near Eastern context, nighttime praise and prayer were established practices, as also attested in the Psalms (Psalm 42:9; 77:7). The term points to the fact that true reverence for God involves seeking Him even during life’s dark periods, not only when things go well. Elihu criticizes those who suffer for crying out for help without seeking God as the giver of comfort and hope.

### Wisdom from Creation

In verse 11, the verb "מַלְּפֵנוּ" (malpenu, "He teaches us") is used in connection with animals and birds. This reflects a wisdom tradition in which nature serves as a teacher (cf. Proverbs 6:6–8). The point is that even animals instinctively honor their Creator, while humans, in their pride, fail to do the same.
