# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 23

## People

**Joash** (יוֹאָשׁ) was the son of King Ahaziah and the rightful heir to David’s throne. As an infant, he had been saved from Athaliah’s massacre by his aunt Jehosheba and hidden in the temple for six years (2 Chron 22:11-12). In this chapter, at the age of seven, he is brought forward and crowned king over Judah.

**Jehoiada** was the high priest in Jerusalem and the central figure behind the coup against Athaliah. He was married to Jehosheba, daughter of King Joram, who had rescued young Joash from Athaliah’s massacre (2 Chron 22:11). Jehoiada emerges as the true leader of the uprising, illustrating how powerful the high priest could be in Judah.

**Athaliah** was the daughter of Ahab and Jezebel from the northern kingdom and the mother of King Ahaziah. When her son died, she seized power and nearly wiped out the entire royal house—she was the only woman to reign as queen in Judah. She represented the Baal cult that had spread from the northern kingdom, and her six-year rule (approximately 841–835 B.C.) posed a serious threat to the Davidic dynasty.

**The five officers** named by name (Azariah son of Jeroham, Ishmael son of Jehanan, Azariah son of Obed, Maaseiah son of Adaiah, and Elishaphat son of Zichri) were "captains of hundreds"—military leaders with whom Jehoiada made a covenant. The fact that Chronicles names them all underscores the historical significance of this event.

## Places

**The house of the Lord** (the temple) was the center of the entire coup. The young king had been hidden there for six years, and his coronation took place within the temple complex. The chapter mentions several specific locations: "the Foundation Gate" (שַׁעַר הַיְסוֹד, *sha'ar hayesod*, v.5), which was likely one of the temple entrances, and the people gathered in "the courts of the house of the Lord." That the coronation occurred at the temple highlighted Joash’s religious legitimacy as a descendant of David.

**The entrance of the Horse Gate near the king’s palace** (v.15) was where Athaliah was executed. Jehoiada ordered that she be led out of the temple area before being killed, to avoid defiling the holy place with bloodshed.

**Baal’s temple** (v.17) had been built in Jerusalem under Athaliah’s influence. The existence of such a temple in Judah shows how far the Canaanite religion had spread. Its destruction was a central part of the religious purification.

## Events and Customs

**The coronation ritual** is described in detail in verse 11: the king’s son was brought forward, given "the diadem" (נֵזֶר, *nezer*—the royal crown) and handed "the testimony" (עֵדוּת, *edut*). This "testimony" was likely a scroll containing the law or the covenant document that the king was to follow in his rule (cf. Deut 17:18-20). He was then anointed—an act signifying God’s choice and granting the king a holy status.

**The Sabbath watch rotation** (vv.4-8) was strategically utilized. On the Sabbath, the temple guards changed shifts, meaning twice as many men were available. Jehoiada kept both the outgoing and incoming guards, thus assembling a larger force without arousing suspicion.

**The covenant renewal** (vv.3, 16) had two parts: first, a covenant between the leaders and the king in God’s house; then, a broader covenant among Jehoiada, the people, and the king to be "a people for the Lord." This pattern of renewing the covenant at each change of ruler was important in Judah’s theology.

## Key Terms

**"The people of the land"** (עַם הָאָרֶץ, *am ha'arets*) is mentioned several times (vv.13, 20, 21). In this period, the term referred to the free, landowning population of Judah—a political force capable of influencing royal accessions. Their joy at Joash’s coronation shows that Athaliah’s rule lacked popular support.

**"The king’s column"** (עַל־עַמּוּדוֹ, *al-ammudo*—"by his column") in verse 13 was a fixed position near the temple entrance where the king stood during ceremonies. This column was likely one of the two bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz, that Solomon had erected (2 Chron 3:17), and its location marked the king’s special role as the link between God and the people.

**Mattan** (v.17), the priest of Baal who was killed, bears an ironic name—it means "gift" in Hebrew and was paradoxically a common theophoric name associated with Yahweh. His execution before the altars recalls the prophet Elijah’s killing of the prophets of Baal at the Kishon brook (1 Kings 18:40).
