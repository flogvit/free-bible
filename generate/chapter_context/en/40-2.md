# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 2

## People

**Herod the Great** (Greek: Ἡρῴδης) ruled as a client king under Rome from 37–4 BC. He was known for his grand building projects, including the reconstruction of the temple in Jerusalem, but also for his brutal paranoia. Josephus documents that Herod executed several of his own sons and his wife Mariamne out of fear of rivals. This characterization aligns well with the massacre in Bethlehem described in verse 16. Herod was Idumean by descent, which made his legitimacy as "King of the Jews" controversial – this explains his strong reaction to the wise men's question about the "born" king.

**Archelaus** (verse 22) inherited Judea, Samaria, and Idumea after Herod's death in 4 BC, but without the royal title – Rome gave him only the title "ethnarch." He was notorious for his brutality and was deposed by Rome in AD 6 following complaints from Jewish and Samaritan leaders. Joseph's fear of settling under his rule was therefore well-founded.

**The Wise Men** (Greek: μάγοι) were likely Persian or Babylonian scholars who practiced astronomy and astrology. In antiquity, these fields were not separated. The Babylonian tradition had existed since the Jewish exile, and some scholars believe that expectations of a Jewish king may have been known in eastern scholarly circles.

## Places

**Bethlehem** (Βηθλέεμ) is located about 8 km south of Jerusalem. The name means "house of bread" in Hebrew. The city had deep messianic significance as the birthplace of King David (1 Sam 16:1–13), and the prophet Micah had identified it as the birthplace of the Messiah (Micah 5:1). In Jesus' time, it was a small village. Archaeological excavations under the Church of the Nativity have revealed caves that were used as homes and stables during this period.

**Ramah** (Ῥαμά) in verse 18 was a city north of Jerusalem, near Rachel's tomb according to tradition. The quotation from Jeremiah 31:15 originally referred to the Babylonian exile, where Ramah served as a gathering point for those deported. Matthew applies this typologically to the child massacre.

**Egypt** (Αἴγυπτος) had a large Jewish diaspora, especially in Alexandria. Egypt was easily accessible from Judea and outside Herod's jurisdiction as a Roman province. The flight there created a typological parallel to Israel's history – just as Israel came out of Egypt, so too did God's Son come from there.

**Nazareth** (Ναζαρέτ) was an insignificant village in Galilee that is not mentioned in the Old Testament or in Josephus' writings. Archaeological findings show a small agricultural village with 200–400 inhabitants. The fact that Galilee was under the milder rule of Herod Antipas (unlike Archelaus in Judea) made the area safer for the family.

## Events and Customs

**Astrology and Royal Births** were widespread in the ancient Middle East. Babylonian astronomers kept detailed observation logs, and unusual celestial phenomena were interpreted as signs of important events. In 7–6 BC, several astronomical events occurred, including a conjunction between Jupiter and Saturn in the constellation Pisces, which some scholars have proposed as the background for "the star."

**The Gifts of Gold, Frankincense, and Myrrh** (χρυσὸν καὶ λίβανον καὶ σμύρναν) were valuable trade goods with symbolic meaning. Gold was a royal gift, frankincense was used in temple worship and associated with divinity, while myrrh was used for anointing and embalming. These goods came along trade routes from Arabia and the East, which underscores the geographical origin of the wise men.

## Key Terms

**Μάγοι** (magoi) is the source of our word "magic," but in this context it denotes a class of scholars from the East, often associated with Persian-Median wise men. They were not "sorcerers" in a negative sense, but respected advisors at eastern courts, specialists in dream interpretation and celestial observation.

**Προσκυνέω** (proskynéō – "to worship," "to bow down") is used three times in the chapter (verses 2, 8, 11). The word describes the eastern custom of throwing oneself down before a superior – it could mean both political homage to a king and religious worship. Matthew leaves this ambiguity open, while the context suggests deeper meaning.

**Χρηματίζω** (chrēmatízō – "to be warned," "to receive divine instruction") in verses 12 and 22 is a technical term for receiving an oracle or divine revelation, often through dreams. It emphasizes that the actions of the wise men and Joseph were divinely guided.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Josephus confirms in *Antiquities of the Jews* (17.167–191) Herod's time of death and the political unrest that followed. Excavations at Herodium and inscriptions have confirmed many details about Herod's regime. No extrabiblical sources mention the child massacre directly, but this is not surprising given Bethlehem's modest size – it would involve a relatively small number of children in a small village, an event easily overshadowed by Herod's many other documented atrocities.
