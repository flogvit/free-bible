# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 3

## Places

**Mount Moriah** (הַמּוֹרִיָּה) is explicitly mentioned as the place where the temple was built. This is the only other reference to Moriah in the Old Testament besides Genesis 22, where Abraham was to sacrifice Isaac. The author of Chronicles deliberately links the temple site to this patriarchal tradition, emphasizing the holy continuity of the location. The identification with "the threshing floor of Ornan (Arauna)" also connects it to David’s altar following the census (1 Chron 21).

**Parvaim** (פַּרְוָיִם) in verse 6 is a place known for its gold. Its location is uncertain – some scholars associate it with Arabia, others with East Africa or India. The name appears only here in the Bible.

## Key Terms

**"The first standard measure"** (הַמִּדָּה הָרִאשׁוֹנָה) in verse 3 likely refers to the older, "royal" cubit, which was longer than the common cubit – approximately 52 cm compared to about 44–45 cm. This was the standard used for major construction projects.

**The Cherubim** (כְּרוּבִים) in the Most Holy Place were massive sculptures with a total wingspan of 20 cubits (about 10 meters). Unlike the cherubim on the ark’s cover, which were part of the lid, these were freestanding statues whose wings covered the entire room.

**Jachin and Boaz** – the two pillars in front of the temple – had symbolic names: Jachin (יָכִין) means "he will establish" and Boaz (בֹּעַז) means "in him is strength". Freestanding columns at temple entrances were common in Phoenician and Canaanite architecture, known from excavations at Hazor and Tell Tayinat.

## Events and Customs

Construction began "in the second month" (Ziv/Iyyar, April–May), the same month Solomon’s temple building is mentioned in 1 Kings 6:1. This was after the spring rains and before the summer heat – ideal for construction work. The date in Solomon’s fourth regnal year places this around 966 BC according to traditional chronology.

The curtain (פָּרֹכֶת) made of blue, purple, and crimson wool and fine linen with cherub motifs separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place. The same colors and materials were used in the tabernacle (Exod 26:31), showing continuity in Israelite cultic tradition.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of temples from the same period at Ain Dara (Syria) and Tell Tayinat show striking similarities to Solomon’s temple: a three-part plan (porch, main hall, inner room), freestanding columns at the entrance, and richly decorated walls. This confirms that the temple followed established Northwest Semitic architectural conventions.
