# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 9

## People

The chapter mentions several key individuals by name. **Cephas** (v. 5) is the Aramaic name for Peter, and the reference indicates that Peter was married and took his wife with him on missionary journeys. This aligns with the Gospels' mention of Peter's mother-in-law (Mark 1:30). **Barnabas** (v. 6) was Paul's former coworker from the first missionary journey and is mentioned here as someone who, like Paul, worked for his own support. **The Lord's brothers** (οἱ ἀδελφοὶ τοῦ κυρίου) refers to Jesus' brothers, including James, who led the church in Jerusalem, showing that several of them were active in missionary work with their wives.

## Events and Customs

Paul refers to temple service (v. 13) and the principle that priests live from the offerings. This was standard practice both in Jewish temple service, where priests received portions of the sacrificial meat and firstfruits, and in Greek temples, where priests had similar rights. The Corinthians would recognize this pattern from both cultural contexts.

**The quotation from Deuteronomy 25:4** about the ox that treads out grain (v. 9) was a well-known Mosaic law forbidding muzzling an ox while it works so that it may eat during its labor. Rabbinic tradition had already expanded the principle of this law to apply to the fair treatment of all workers, and Paul follows this interpretive tradition.

## Key Terms

The term **ἐξουσία** (exousia – "right/authority/freedom") appears six times in the chapter and is central to the argument. It describes the legitimate right Paul has but voluntarily refrains from exercising. **Apologia** (v. 3) is a legal term for a defense speech in a trial, suggesting that Paul's apostolic authority was under attack. The expression **σφραγίς τῆς ἀποστολῆς** ("seal of my apostleship," v. 2) uses the image of an official seal that authenticated documents—the Corinthians themselves are proof of Paul's legitimacy.

The word **οἰκονομία** (v. 17) denotes a steward's responsibility over a household or estate—Paul sees himself as a slave with entrusted responsibility, not a free agent.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Isthmian Games** (vv. 24–27) were held every two years in Corinth in honor of Poseidon and were among the four great Panhellenic athletic competitions. Archaeological excavations at Isthmia, only 15 km from Corinth, have revealed a stadium, gymnasium, and remains of training facilities. Crowns made of wild celery or pine (**φθαρτὸν στέφανον**) were given as prizes—a striking contrast for the Corinthians who knew these games well, since the crown quickly withered. Athletes had to undergo ten months of mandatory training with a strict diet (**ἐγκρατεύεται**) before they could compete.

**Boxing** (πυκτεύω, v. 26) was a brutal Olympic event where competitors used leather straps (later reinforced with metal). The expression "beat the air" describes a beginner who misses his opponent—an image the Corinthians would immediately understand as a sign of amateurish effort.
