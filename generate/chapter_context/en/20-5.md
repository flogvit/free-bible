# Context for Proverbs 5

## Key Concepts

The chapter contains several Hebrew words that require cultural explanation. The term **זָרָה** (*zarah*, "foreign woman") in verses 3 and 20 does not primarily refer to a foreigner by nationality, but to a woman who stands outside the young man's legitimate marital sphere. The parallel term **נָכְרִיָּה** (*nokriyyah*) reinforces this—she is "foreign" in the sense that she belongs to another man's household or operates outside honorable social structures.

The water imagery in verses 15–18 is culturally charged. **בּוֹר** (*bor*, "cistern") and **בְּאֵר** (*be'er*, "well") were vital sources of water in the dry climate of Israel. To "drink water from your own cistern" became an established metaphor for marital faithfulness. **מָקוֹר** (*maqor*, "spring") in verse 18 is also used elsewhere in Proverbs to refer to the fountain of life (Prov 10:11; 13:14).

## Events and Customs

The warnings in verses 9–14 reflect concrete social and economic consequences of adultery in ancient Israel. The expressions "give your honor to others" (v. 9) and "let strangers be filled with your strength" (v. 10) allude to the fact that adulterous relationships could lead to financial ruin. An aggrieved husband could demand compensation, and a family's resources might be lost to outsiders. Verse 14 mentions "in the midst of the assembly and congregation" (*qahal we'edah*), suggesting public shame and possible legal proceedings before the city elders at the city gate.

The animal images in verse 19—**אַיֶּלֶת אֲהָבִים** ("the deer of love") and **יַעֲלַת־חֵן** ("the graceful gazelle")—were common in ancient Near Eastern love poetry. Similar images appear in the Song of Solomon (see Song 2:9,17; 4:5), where gazelles and does represent beauty, grace, and erotic attraction.
