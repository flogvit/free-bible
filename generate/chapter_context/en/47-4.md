# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 4

## Key Concepts

**"The god of this age" (ὁ θεὸς τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου, v. 4)**: This expression for Satan is rare in the New Testament and reflects Jewish apocalyptic thought that the present age stands under the influence of evil powers. The term "αἰών" (age/world) was central to the Jewish two-age worldview distinguishing between "this age" and "the coming age". In Greco-Roman religion, there were also notions of gods ruling over specific spheres, but Paul’s formulation is distinctly Jewish-Christian in its dualism.

**"Jars of clay" (ὀστράκινα σκεύη, v. 7)**: The image of clay jars was immediately recognizable to the Corinthians. Clay vessels were the most common and inexpensive household items in antiquity—fragile, easily broken, and of low value compared to containers made of bronze, silver, or gold. Archaeological excavations in Corinth have uncovered enormous quantities of ceramic fragments from all social classes. The contrast between the precious treasure (the glory of the gospel) and the fragile jar (the apostles/believers) was a powerful image of how God’s power works through human weakness.

**"Image" (εἰκών, v. 4)**: When Paul calls Christ "the image of God", he uses a term with deep resonance in both Jewish and Greco-Roman culture. In the context of emperor worship, the emperor’s image (εἰκών) was worshipped as a representation of his divinity. For Jews, the term pointed back to Genesis 1:26–27 where humanity is created in God’s image. Paul’s claim that Christ is the true image of God was therefore both a theological and political challenge.

## Events and Customs

Paul’s description of his sufferings in verses 8–9 uses four pairs of contrasts that reflect both rhetorical style and real experiences. The Greco-Roman world valued "peristasis catalogs"—lists of difficulties a person had overcome—as proof of character and endurance. Philosophers, especially Stoics and Cynics, presented their sufferings as evidence of philosophical integrity. Paul adopts this form but gives it radically new content: suffering proves not human strength, but God’s power through weakness.

The expression "the death of Jesus" being carried in the body (v. 10) and to be "handed over to death for Jesus’ sake" (v. 11) may reflect concrete persecutions Paul experienced. Paul details his sufferings later in the letter (2 Cor 11:23–27), which provides concrete background for the language used here. For readers in Corinth, living in a city where public honor and status were decisive, the apostle’s shameful sufferings paradoxically proved his authenticity.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

Verse 6 contains a clear allusion to the creation account in Genesis 1:3. Paul writes "Ἐκ σκότους φῶς λάμψει" (From darkness light shall shine), which paraphrases the words of creation rather than quoting them directly. Paul applies creation symbolism to the new creation that occurs in believers’ hearts. This connection between physical creation and spiritual renewal was known from Jewish wisdom literature and apocalyptic tradition.

The quotation in verse 13 is from Psalm 116:10 (LXX Psalm 115:1): "I believed, therefore I spoke." Paul identifies with the psalmist’s experience of distress and deliverance, showing how believers’ suffering and confession stand in continuity with Israel’s history of faith.
