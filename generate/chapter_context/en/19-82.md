## Key Concepts

Psalm 82 contains several Hebrew key terms that require special attention. The word **אֱלֹהִים** (*elohim*) is used in two ways in this psalm: In verse 1 and verse 8, it refers to the God of Israel, while in the middle of verse 1 and in verse 6, it is applied to other beings in the heavenly assembly. The expression **בַּעֲדַת־אֵל** (*ba'adat-el*, "in the divine council") describes a heavenly court where God presides as supreme judge. The term **בְּנֵי עֶלְיוֹן** (*bene elyon*, "sons of the Most High") in verse 6 designates these heavenly beings as God's "children" with delegated authority.

## Events and Customs

The concept of a **heavenly assembly** where God sits in judgment over other divine beings has clear parallels in the surrounding ancient Near Eastern culture. In Ugaritic texts from the second millennium BCE, the god El is described as the head of a council of gods. In Mesopotamian texts, we find similar scenes where the gods gather to make decisions. However, the psalm radically transforms this motif: The other "gods" are not independent powers but subordinate beings held accountable by the God of Israel. Their task was to ensure justice for the vulnerable in society—the weak (*dal*), the fatherless (*yatom*), the helpless (*ani*), the poor (*rash*), and the needy (*evyon*)—categories that recur throughout Old Testament social ethics and law.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (discovered from 1929 onward) have provided valuable background for understanding Psalm 82. These texts describe "El's assembly" (*pḫr 'ilm*) and the "sons of the gods" (*bn 'ilm*) in terms that closely resemble the language of the psalm. This shows that the psalmist employed familiar religious vocabulary from his time, but with a polemical purpose: to demonstrate that these so-called gods are mortal beings under the God of Israel, who alone holds absolute power over all nations (verse 8).
