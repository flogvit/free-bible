## People

### The Wise Woman of Tekoa
The chapter introduces an unnamed "wise woman" (אִשָּׁה חֲכָמָה, *ishah chakhamah*) from Tekoa. The term "wise" refers not only to intelligence but to a specific social role—individuals known for rhetorical skill and the ability to formulate persuasive arguments. Such "wise" men and women served as unofficial advisors and mediators in Israelite society. That Joab chose specifically a woman for this task may be because a woman's plea for protection of her son would evoke stronger sympathy from the king.

### Absalom
The chapter provides a rare physical description of Absalom: he was exceptionally beautiful "from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head" without any defect (v. 25). His hair was so heavy that it weighed two hundred shekels according to "the king's weight" when he cut it annually (v. 26). This weight is debated—according to the standard shekel, it would equal about 2.3 kg, but "the king's weight" was likely a different standard, possibly significantly lighter. His hair later became fatal for Absalom (chapter 18). Absalom's daughter was named Tamar (v. 27)—likely named after his disgraced sister (chapter 13).

## Places

### Tekoa
Tekoa was located approximately 16 kilometers south of Jerusalem in the hill country of Judah, near the desert area to the east. The city is later known as the hometown of the prophet Amos. Its location—near the capital yet still in a rural outskirts—made it credible that the woman could approach the king with her case without David immediately recognizing her or suspecting a setup.

## Events and Customs

### Blood Vengeance and the Avenger
The woman's fictional story concerns *go'el haddam* (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם), "the avenger of blood"—a close relative obligated to avenge a slain family member. This custom was deeply rooted in Israelite and broader Semitic culture. The woman appeals to the king to limit the right of the blood avenger so as to preserve her only surviving son and thus the family line. The metaphor of "quenching the last ember" (v. 7) refers to completely extinguishing a family's lineage.

### Signs of Mourning
Joab's instructions to the woman (v. 2) reveal standard mourning practices: wearing mourning clothes, refraining from anointing oneself with oil, and engaging in prolonged mourning behavior. Oil was part of daily hygiene and well-being; abstaining from anointing signaled deep grief or humiliation.

## Key Terms

### "The King's Weight" (אֶבֶן הַמֶּלֶךְ)
The expression in verse 26 indicates that there existed a royal standardized unit of weight, distinct from local variations. This testifies to a centralized administration under David that sought to standardize measures and weights—a feature known from other ancient monarchies.

### "God's Angel" as an Ideal of Wisdom
The woman compares David to "God's angel" (מַלְאַךְ הָאֱלֹהִים) who can "hear both good and evil" (v. 17) and has the wisdom to "know all that happens in the land" (v. 20). This was a supreme form of flattery, attributing superhuman wisdom and judgment to the king—a role the king ideally should fulfill as God's representative in legal matters.
