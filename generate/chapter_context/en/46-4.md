# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 4

## People

**Timothy** is introduced in verse 17 as Paul’s envoy to Corinth. He was Paul’s closest coworker and travel companion. Paul refers to him as his "beloved and faithful child in the Lord" (τέκνον ἀγαπητὸν καὶ πιστὸν ἐν κυρίῳ), indicating that Timothy became a believer through Paul’s ministry. His mission was to remind the Corinthians of Paul’s "ways in Christ"—the lifestyle and teaching Paul proclaimed in all the churches.

**Apollos** is mentioned in verse 6 alongside Paul as an example of the right attitude toward service. He was an eloquent Christian preacher who had previously ministered in Corinth. Paul uses himself and Apollos as illustrations to calm factionalism within the church, without directly naming the actual instigators of the division.

## Key Terms

**Ὑπηρέτης (hypēretēs)** in verse 1 originally referred to a rower on the lower deck of a warship—one who carries out orders under command. In a figurative sense, it describes a subordinate servant or assistant. Paul uses this term to emphasize that apostles are servants under Christ’s authority, not independent leaders deserving personal followings.

**Οἰκονόμος (oikonomos)**, "steward," was a household slave entrusted with managing the master’s property and other slaves. This position carried great trust but no personal ownership rights. The term underscores that apostles manage something belonging to another—God’s mysteries—and must give an account to Him, not to the church.

**Παιδαγωγός (paidagōgos)** in verse 15 was not a teacher in the modern sense, but a slave who accompanied children to school, supervised their behavior, and provided basic moral instruction. The role was important yet temporary and subordinate to the father’s authority. Paul contrasts the many guides with himself as the spiritual father of the church.

## Cultural Background

**The theater image** in verse 9 alludes to the Roman arena where condemned prisoners were led in last (ἐσχάτους, "last") during processions to fight to their deaths as public spectacle. The terms ἐπιθανατίους ("condemned to death") and θέατρον ("spectacle/theater") would have evoked strong associations among Corinthians, who lived near one of the largest theaters in Greece. Paul turns the Corinthian fascination with status and honor upside down by describing apostles as the lowest figures in the arena.

**Περικαθάρματα and περίψημα** in verse 13 ("scum" and "refuse") were technical terms from Greek purification rituals. In some city-states, socially outcast individuals were used as "scapegoats"—driven out or killed to cleanse the community of misfortune or plague. Paul’s use of these words is a powerful rhetorical device contrasting the apostles’ actual social standing with the Corinthians’ self-satisfaction.

**Manual labor** (verse 12: "toil and work with our own hands") was despised among the Greco-Roman elite. That Paul worked as a craftsman instead of receiving financial support was likely a source of criticism in Corinth, where wealthy members expected respected teachers to act as patrons or accept patronage. Paul turns this criticism into a testimony of authentic apostolic life.
