# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 6

## People

**The Twelve Apostles** form a formal group that Jesus chooses in this chapter (vv. 13–16). The term "apostle" (ἀπόστολος, *apostolos*) means "sent one" and parallels the Jewish *shaliach* institution, where an envoy had full authority to represent the sender. The list includes Simon, who receives the nickname "Peter" (Πέτρος, from Greek *petra*, "rock"), and Simon called "the Zealot" (Ζηλωτής). This designation may refer either to his religious zeal or to his association with the nationalist resistance movement later known as the Zealots. Judas Iscariot is already identified here as "the traitor" (προδότης).

## Places

**"The mountain"** (τὸ ὄρος, v. 12) where Jesus spends the night in prayer likely refers to the highlands near the Sea of Galilee. Luke then notes that Jesus goes down and stands on **"a level place"** (τόπου πεδινοῦ, v. 17) to speak—this distinguishes Luke’s account from Matthew’s "Sermon on the Mount." The crowds come from Judea, Jerusalem, and the coastal regions of **Tyre and Sidon** (v. 17), which were Phoenician cities north of Galilee. Mentioning Gentile areas underscores Jesus’ growing influence beyond the Jewish heartlands.

## Events and Customs

**The Sabbath conflict** (vv. 1–11) concerns interpretations of the Sabbath commandment. Picking grain was classified by the Pharisees as "reaping," one of the thirty-nine main categories of work forbidden on the Sabbath according to rabbinic tradition (Mishnah, Shabbat 7:2). Jesus’ defense, citing David eating the showbread (1 Sam 21:1–6), uses an accepted rabbinic argument: if a great figure like David could set aside ritual rules in times of need, then this principle applies here as well.

**Healing on the Sabbath** (vv. 6–11) touches upon a central debate topic in Jewish law. The Pharisees permitted medical treatment on the Sabbath only when life was at risk (*pikuach nefesh*). A withered hand was not life-threatening, so opponents believed healing could wait. Jesus’ question about whether it is lawful "to do good or to do evil" on the Sabbath (v. 9) fundamentally challenges this interpretation.

## Key Terms

**Ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως** ("the showbread," v. 4) were twelve loaves placed before God in the temple every Sabbath as an offering (Lev 24:5–9). Only priests were allowed to eat them. Jesus’ reference to this event establishes a principle that human needs may take precedence over ceremonial regulations.

**Μακάριοι** ("blessed," vv. 20–22) means "happy" or "fortunate" and is known from Greek literature and Jewish wisdom tradition (cf. Ps 1:1). Luke’s version is more direct and social than Matthew’s, focusing on concrete poverty and hunger rather than spiritual conditions. The four "woes" (οὐαί, vv. 24–26) that follow are unique to Luke and form a sharp contrast.

**Ἱμάτιον and χιτών** (v. 29) refer respectively to the outer cloak and the undergarment. The outer cloak was so important that according to Mosaic law (Exod 22:26–27), it could not be taken as collateral overnight—it served as a blanket for the poor.

**The Golden Rule** (v. 31) is presented in positive form: "As you wish that others would do to you, so do to them." This principle was known in both Jewish tradition (Tobit 4:15, Rabbi Hillel) and Greco-Roman ethics, but Jesus formulates it here as an active obligation rather than merely avoiding harm to others.
