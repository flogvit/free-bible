# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 15

## People

**Azariah/Uzziah** (vv. 1–7, 13, 17, 23, 27) is the same king, and the use of both names in this chapter reflects a common practice where kings had both a personal name and a throne name. Azariah means "The Lord has helped," while Uzziah means "The Lord is my strength." His long reign of 52 years marked a period of prosperity and territorial expansion in Judah. 2 Chronicles 26 provides more details about his leprosy, which was linked to his unlawful entry into the temple to burn incense.

**Jotham** (vv. 5, 7, 30, 32–38) was the son of Azariah/Uzziah and served as regent ("over the house, and he judged the people in the land") while his father was leprous. He later became king in his own right and reigned for 16 years. He "did what was right in the eyes of the Lord" and built the upper gate of the house of the Lord (v. 35). His mother Jerusha was the daughter of Zadok.

**Pul/Tiglath-Pileser III** (vv. 19, 29) is the same person. "Pul" was his Babylonian throne name, while Tiglath-Pileser III was his Assyrian throne name. He ruled Assyria from 745–727 B.C. and revolutionized Assyrian warfare and empire-building. His policy of mass deportations of conquered peoples was designed to break down national identity and resistance.

**The Line of Kings in Israel** – This chapter documents a dramatic period of five kings over approximately 30 years (Zechariah, Shallum, Menahem, Pekahiah, Pekah), four of whom were murdered. This illustrates the political instability that characterized the final decade of the Northern Kingdom.

## Places

**Tirzah** (vv. 14, 16) was Israel's capital before Samaria (from Jeroboam I to Omri), located at Tell el-Far'ah north of Shechem. The fact that Menahem came from there may indicate that he represented a rival power base or older royal traditions.

**Tibhath** (v. 16) is disputed. If it refers to the well-known trading city of Tibhath on the Euphrates, it would be geographically unlikely in this context. More likely, it was a nearby city, possibly Tappuah in Ephraim. Menahem's brutal treatment of the city—including ripping open pregnant women—demonstrates the horrors of war and his ruthlessness in establishing power.

**The Cities in Verse 29** (Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, Galilee, the land of Naphtali) represent the northern and eastern border regions of Israel. Tiglath-Pileser III systematically took control of these strategically important areas in 733–732 B.C., as confirmed by Assyrian annals.

## Events and Customs

**"Beth-hahofshith"** (בֵּית הַחָפְשִׁית, v. 5), translated "the house of freedom" or "the place of seclusion," was the place where Azariah lived after he was struck with leprosy. The term is disputed but likely refers to a separate house where the sick king could live in isolation according to the purity requirements in Leviticus 13–14, while his son Jotham served as regent.

**Tribute to Assyria** (vv. 19–20) – Menahem's payment of 1,000 talents of silver (about 34 tons) to secure Assyrian support, collected by taxing wealthy men 50 shekels each, suggests that at least 60,000 wealthy men were taxed. This shows both the kingdom's resources and the economic burden imposed on vassal states by the Assyrian empire.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The annals of Tiglath-Pileser III, found in Kalhu (modern Nimrud), confirm the events in this chapter. The texts mention both "Menahem of Samaria" who paid tribute and military campaigns against Israel. One inscription reads: "Menahem of Samara ... I received from him silver, dyed wool, linen garments..." The Assyrian king also mentions that he set up "Hoshea as king over them" after removing Pekah, confirming verse 30. These extra-biblical sources provide important confirmation of the chapter's historical accuracy.
