# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 24

## People

**Nebuchadnezzar II** (Hebrew: נְבֻכַדְנֶאצַּר) was the most powerful king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire (605–562 B.C.). In this chapter, he receives Jehoiakim’s oath of vassalage, sends punitive expeditions against Judah (verse 2), and personally besieges Jerusalem to take Jeconiah captive (verses 10–12).

**Jehoiakim** (Hebrew: יְהוֹיָקִים, "YHWH raises up") reigned as a vassal king first under Egypt, then under Babylon. His rebellion against Nebuchadnezzar after three years of vassalage (verse 1: וַיָּשָׁב וַיִּמְרָד־בּוֹ, "he turned and rebelled against him") triggered the catastrophe described in this chapter. The prophet Jeremiah strongly condemned him (Jer 22:18–19).

**Jeconiah** (Hebrew: יְהוֹיָכִין, "YHWH establishes") was eighteen years old when he became king and reigned only three months before surrendering to Nebuchadnezzar (verses 8, 12). His mother **Nehushta bat Elnatan of Jerusalem** (verse 8: נְחֻשְׁתָּא בַת־אֶלְנָתָן מִירוּשָׁלָם) is specifically mentioned and was taken into exile along with her son (verses 12, 15).

**Zedekiah** (Hebrew: צִדְקִיָּהוּ, "YHWH is my righteousness") was originally named **Mattaniah** (מַתַּנְיָה, "gift of YHWH"). He was Jehoiakim’s uncle (verse 17: דֹּדוֹ). The name change imposed by Nebuchadnezzar (verse 17: וַיַּסֵּב אֶת־שְׁמוֹ) demonstrated Babylonian dominance. He was twenty-one years old when he became king and reigned for eleven years. His mother was **Hamutal bat Jeremiah of Libnah** (verse 18).

## Places

**Wadi Mizraim** ("the Brook of Egypt") mentioned in verse 7 likely refers to Wadi el-Arish on the Sinai Peninsula, which marked the traditional boundary between Egyptian and Canaanite territory. The **Euphrates** (Hebrew: נְהַר־פְּרָת) formed the northern border of the area now controlled by Babylon. Verse 7 describes how the king of Egypt could no longer leave his land because the king of Babylon had taken everything from "the Brook of Egypt to the Euphrates"—a situation that arose after the Battle of Carchemish in 605 B.C.

**Jerusalem** was besieged and captured in 597 B.C. (verses 10–11). This was the first of two Babylonian conquests of the city. Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered layers of fire and destruction consistent with this period.

**Libnah** (verse 18: מִלִּבְנָה) was a town in the lowlands of Judah, the home of Zedekiah’s mother Hamutal.

## Events and Customs

The **vassal relationship** described in verse 1 was typical of the Neo-Babylonian Empire’s control over smaller kingdoms. Jehoiakim’s three years as a "servant" (עֶבֶד) likely involved paying tribute and maintaining political loyalty. Rebellion against such obligations was considered a serious breach of contract and punished severely.

The **punitive expeditions** in verse 2 involved not only Chaldean (Babylonian) troops but also forces from Aram, Moab, and Ammon. These were likely local vassals mobilized by Babylon to punish rebellious neighbors.

The **deportation policy** described in verses 14–16 was a deliberate Babylonian strategy. By removing the elite—"princes, warriors, craftsmen, and metalworkers"—they weakened the nation’s ability to resist. The phrase "the poorest of the land" (דַּלַּת עַם־הָאָרֶץ, verse 14) refers to those left behind to work the soil. The figure of 10,000 deportees (verse 14) aligns well with Babylonian administrative texts.

## Key Terms

**Charash and masger** (הֶחָרָשׁ וְהַמַּסְגֵּר) in verses 14 and 16 mean "craftsmen and metalworkers/smiths." These were strategically important because they could produce weapons and tools. By deporting them, the Babylonians stripped Judah of its military production capacity.

**The sins of Manasseh** (verses 3–4) refer to idolatry and the shedding of innocent blood during King Manasseh’s reign (2 Kgs 21). Verse 4 emphasizes that "YHWH was unwilling to forgive" (וְלֹא־אָבָה יְהוָה לִסְלֹחַ)—the fall of Jerusalem is seen as divine judgment for these sins.

**Elei ha-arets** (אֵילֵי הָאָרֶץ, verse 15) means "the mighty ones/leaders of the land" and refers to the political and economic elite deported along with the royal family.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Jehoiachin tablets** from Babylon are among the most important archaeological discoveries confirming this chapter. Discovered in the ruins of Nebuchadnezzar’s palace, these cuneiform tablets document rations of oil and barley given to "Ya’ukinu, king of Judah" and his five sons. This confirms both Jehoiachin’s exile and that he retained his royal status while in captivity.

The **Lachish letters**, found in the ruins of the Judean fortress city of Lachish, are ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from just before Jerusalem’s fall. They provide a unique glimpse into the desperate situation in Judah under Babylonian pressure, with references to prophets and military communications that correspond with events of this period.
