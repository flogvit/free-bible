# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 10

## People

**The Queen of Sheba** (מַלְכַּת־שְׁבָא) is one of the most well-known figures from ancient South Arabia. Sheba was a kingdom in what is now Yemen, famous for its immense wealth derived from trade in frankincense, myrrh, and other precious goods. Sabaean inscriptions confirm that women could hold high positions in this society, including religious and political roles. The queen's visit likely represented both diplomatic and commercial interests—she sought to establish or secure trade agreements with the Israelite king who controlled important trade routes.

**Hiram** is mentioned again in connection with the fleet that brought gold from Ophir. This refers to King Hiram of Tyre, who had an established partnership with Solomon regarding maritime expeditions. The Phoenicians were the premier sailors of antiquity, and this partnership gave Israel access to distant markets.

## Places

**Ophir** (אוֹפִיר) was a legendary wealthy region known for its gold. Its location is debated—scholars have proposed South Arabia, East Africa (Somalia/Ethiopia), or even India. An ostracon (potsherd) from Tell Qasile near Tel Aviv from the 800s BC bears the inscription "Gold of Ophir to Beth-Horon—thirty shekels," confirming that Ophir was a known term in ancient Israel.

**Tarshish** (תַּרְשִׁישׁ) refers either to a specific place (possibly Tartessos in Spain) or to a type of large merchant ship built for long voyages. The expression "ships of Tarshish" was often used for large seagoing vessels regardless of destination.

**The House of the Forest of Lebanon** (בֵּית יַעַר הַלְּבָנוֹן) was part of Solomon's palace buildings in Jerusalem, so named because of its many columns made from cedar trees imported from Lebanon.

## Events and Customs

**Riddle-solving** (חִידוֹת) was an important part of the wisdom tradition in the ancient Middle East. Solving riddles demonstrated not only intelligence but also divine wisdom and the ability to discern hidden truths. Such intellectual exchanges between kings were a diplomatic practice where each tested the other's worthiness as allies.

**Gift exchange** between monarchs was a formalized practice in the ancient Middle East, documented in texts such as the Amarna letters. The queen's gifts of 120 talents of gold (about 4 tons) and Solomon's return gifts "with royal generosity" established mutual obligation and alliance.

## Key Terms

**Almugim wood** (עֲצֵי אַלְמֻגִּים) is difficult to identify with certainty. It may be red sandalwood or a type of juniper imported from Ophir. The tree was so rare that the text emphasizes that "such wood has never been seen since."

**Shachut gold** (זָהָב שָׁחוּט) means "hammered" or "stretched" gold—thin sheets of gold used to cover shields. These were ceremonial objects, not battle shields.

**Shenhabim** (שֶׁנְהַבִּים) is ivory, literally "elephant tooth." Along with apes (קֹפִים) and peacocks or baboons (תֻכִּיִּים), this indicates trade in exotic goods from Africa or India.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from South Arabian cultures confirm the rich commercial activity described in the chapter. Sabaean inscriptions from the 900s to 700s BC document a well-organized kingdom with extensive trade in frankincense and myrrh along the "Incense Route." Excavations in Yemen have revealed monumental buildings and irrigation systems that testify to considerable wealth.

Regarding Solomon's wealth, archaeologists have found traces of copper mining in the Timna Valley and in Wadi Faynan in Jordan that may be linked to this period. Although the biblical description of 666 talents of gold annually (about 22 tons) is difficult to verify, archaeological findings confirm that the tenth century BC was a period of increased trade and wealth in the region.
