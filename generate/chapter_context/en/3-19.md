# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 19

## Events and Customs

Leviticus 19 is known as the "Holiness Code" and contains a broad collection of ethical, ritual, and social regulations. The chapter opens with the fundamental command: "You shall be holy, for I, the Lord your God, am holy" (v. 2), which sets the tone for the entire legal code. The laws concerning leaving grain and grapes for the poor and the foreigner (vv. 9–10) reflect an agricultural society where social justice was woven into the very practice of harvesting. This custom of leaving something in the field (*pe'ah*) and not gathering what fell during harvest (*leket*) was later systematized in rabbinic law and forms the background for the story of Ruth.

The prohibitions against eating anything with its blood, practicing divination or sorcery (v. 26), and seeking contact with mediums and spiritists (v. 31) stand in sharp contrast to widespread practices in Canaan, Egypt, and Mesopotamia. These cultures had extensive traditions for consulting the dead and interpreting signs. The prohibition against cutting oneself and tattooing (v. 28) is likely directed at mourning rituals and religious practices associated with death and fertility cults that were common among surrounding nations.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **קָדוֹשׁ** (*qadosh*, "holy") is central to this chapter and fundamentally means "separated" or "set apart." Holiness involves both being separated from what is unclean and being dedicated to God. The expression **כִּלְאַיִם** (*kil'ayim*, v. 19) refers to the prohibition against mixing different kinds—whether in breeding animals, sowing seeds, or weaving textiles. The word **שַׁעַטְנֵז** (*sha'atnez*) specifically describes the mixture of wool and linen, a practice possibly linked to pagan rituals.

The command **וְאָהַבְתָּ לְרֵעֲךָ כָּמוֹךָ** ("You shall love your neighbor as yourself," v. 18) was identified by Jesus as the second greatest commandment in the Torah (Matt 22:39; Mark 12:31). The word **רֵעַ** (*rea'*, "neighbor") is expanded in verse 34 to also include **גֵּר** (*ger*, "the foreigner"), which was remarkable in the ancient world. The term **פִּגּוּל** (*piggul*, vv. 7–8) refers to sacrificial meat that has become "detestable" or "abominable" because it was not eaten within the proper time.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of weights and measures from ancient Israel confirm that standardized units were important in a trading society, providing background for the commands regarding honest weights and measures in verses 35–36. Stones marked with Hebrew symbols for weight (*shekel*) have been found at several excavations in Israel, including at Lachish and Jerusalem. These findings also show that cheating with weights occurred, making such legislation necessary. The terms **אֵיפָה** (*efa*) for dry goods and **הִין** (*hin*) for liquids were standard measures confirmed through archaeological discoveries of marked jars and vessels.
