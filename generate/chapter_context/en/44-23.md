## People

**Ananias, the high priest** (v. 2) was the son of Nedebaeus and served as high priest from approximately 47–59 AD. He was known for his pro-Roman stance and violent temper. Josephus describes him as a greedy man who stole tithes from the lower priests. He was murdered by Jewish rebels (Sicarii) in 66 AD due to his policy of collaboration with the Romans. Paul's characterization "whitewashed wall" (τοῖχε κεκονιαμένε) plays on the image of a grave that looks clean on the outside but is full of impurity within.

**Claudius Lysias** (v. 26) was the tribune (χιλίαρχος) who commanded the Roman garrison in the Antonia Fortress. His name suggests he was a Greek freedman who had purchased his Roman citizenship under Emperor Claudius. As a tribune, he commanded a cohort of 600–1,000 soldiers and held significant legal authority in Jerusalem.

**Felix** (vv. 24, 26) was the Roman procurator (governor) of Judea from approximately 52–59 AD. The Roman historian Tacitus describes him as a man who "exercised royal power with the mindset of a slave." He was a freedman and brother to Pallas, the powerful advisor to Emperor Claudius. The title κράτιστος ("most excellent") was a formal form of address for high officials.

## Places

**Antipatris** (v. 31) was a city located about 60 km northwest of Jerusalem, at the foot of the Judean highlands where they flatten out toward the coastal plain. Herod the Great founded the city and named it after his father Antipater. The site was a natural stopping point on the Roman road between Jerusalem and Caesarea. The journey of about 60 km during the night was a significant marching feat and shows how seriously Lysias took the threat against Paul.

**Caesarea** (vv. 23, 33) was the provincial capital of Rome and the seat of the Roman procurator. The city had a large artificial harbor built by Herod the Great and served as the military and administrative center for Roman rule in Judea.

## Events and Customs

**The Sanhedrin** (τὸ συνέδριον) mentioned in this chapter was the highest Jewish council, consisting of 71 members under the leadership of the high priest. Its composition included both Pharisees and Sadducees, and the theological tension between these groups was well known. Paul strategically exploited this division by declaring himself a Pharisee and raising the issue of the resurrection.

**The Oath of Cursing** (ἀνεθεμάτισαν, vv. 12–14) was a self-imposed oath (herem) in which they pledged neither to eat nor drink until they had killed Paul. Such oaths were known in Jewish tradition, and rabbinic texts discuss how one could be released from such oaths if they proved impossible to fulfill. The fact that more than 40 men participated, involving the chief priests and elders, shows the extent of the opposition to Paul.

## Key Terms

**Συνείδησις ἀγαθή** ("good conscience," v. 1) was a term well known to Jewish audiences. Paul uses the verb πεπολίτευμαι ("I have conducted myself as a citizen"), which emphasizes his conduct as both a Jewish and Roman citizen.

**Ἀνάστασις νεκρῶν** ("resurrection of the dead," vv. 6, 8) was the theological dividing point between the Pharisees and Sadducees. The Sadducees accepted only the five books of Moses as authoritative and found no clear teaching on resurrection there, while the Pharisees included the prophets and oral tradition that supported belief in the resurrection.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Herod's palace in Caesarea, where Paul was held (v. 35), is partially excavated and reveals a magnificent building with frescoes, mosaics, and views of the sea. The Praetorium (πραιτώριον τοῦ Ἡρῴδου) served as both the official residence and courtroom for the procurator, which explains why Paul was held there awaiting trial.
