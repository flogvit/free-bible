# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 10

## People

**The Seventy-Two:** The number varies in manuscripts between 70 and 72. Both numbers carry symbolic significance in Jewish tradition. 70 corresponds to the nations listed in Genesis 10 and the 70 elders who assisted Moses (Numbers 11:16-25). 72 is the number found in the Septuagint tradition for the nations and the 72 translators of the Greek Torah. Regardless of which number was original, the figure suggests a mission with broader scope than that of the twelve disciples, possibly carrying universal implications.

**Lawyer (νομικός):** The lawyer in verse 25 was an expert on the Mosaic Law and its interpretation. Unlike the Pharisees, who were a religious movement, the lawyers were professional scribes who studied, copied, and taught the law. Their question about eternal life reflects a central debate in Judaism of that time.

**Martha and Mary:** These sisters lived in Bethany, near Jerusalem (cf. John 11:1). Martha (from Aramaic *marta*, "mistress" or "hostess") acts as the hostess, a role involving significant social responsibility. That Mary sat at Jesus' feet like a disciple was unusual, since rabbinic teaching was traditionally reserved for men.

## Places

**Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum:** These three cities formed the core of Jesus' Galilean ministry. Chorazin (Khirbet Kerazeh) lay about 3 km north of Capernaum. Archaeological excavations have uncovered a synagogue from the third to fourth century and remains of a Jewish village. Bethsaida ("house of fishing") was located at the mouth of the Jordan River on the Sea of Galilee. Excavations at et-Tell have revealed a city from the Iron Age and the Hellenistic-Roman period. Capernaum (Tell Hum) on the northwestern shore of the lake has yielded rich archaeological finds, including a synagogue and what is traditionally identified as Peter's house.

**Tyre and Sidon:** These Phoenician coastal cities represented Gentile territory and were known for their wealth and trade. In prophetic tradition, they stood as examples of Gentile arrogance (Ezekiel 26-28). Jesus' comparison is striking: Gentile cities would have repented more quickly than Jewish cities that actually witnessed his miracles.

**The Road from Jerusalem to Jericho:** This road was about 27 km long and descended from Jerusalem (about 750 m above sea level) down to Jericho (about 250 m below sea level). The route passed through desolate, rugged desert terrain in the Judean wilderness—ideal ground for robbers. The road was notorious for its danger, and the Roman historian Strabo described the area as a haunt of bandits. Ruins of an inn (Khan el-Hatrur, "the Good Samaritan's Inn") along the route may date from antiquity.

**Bethany:** The village where Martha and Mary lived was located on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, about 3 km from Jerusalem. The name likely comes from Hebrew *bet-anja* ("house of affliction" or "house of poverty"). Today the place is known as el-Azariyeh (after Lazarus).

## Events and Customs

**Shaking Dust from Feet:** This action (verse 11) was a well-known Jewish practice when leaving Gentile territory—one would shake off the unclean dust before stepping back into holy land. That the disciples were to do this toward Jewish cities that rejected them was a powerful symbolic act: these cities were being treated as Gentile and unclean.

**Sackcloth and Ashes:** The expression in verse 13 refers to traditional signs of mourning and repentance. Sackcloth (σάκκος) was a coarse, dark fabric worn directly against the body. Ashes were sprinkled over the head or one would sit in ashes. This combination marked deep sorrow and humility before God (cf. Job 42:6; Daniel 9:3).

**Priests and Levites:** In the parable of the Good Samaritan (verses 31-32), the priest and the Levite represent the religious elite. Priests descended from Aaron and performed sacrificial service in the temple. Levites were from the tribe of Levi and assisted with temple operations. Both groups had strict purity requirements. Contact with a corpse—and the man appeared to be dead—would have rendered them unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11). The irony is that they prioritized ritual purity over mercy.

**Samaritans:** The Samaritans were descendants of Israelites who remained in the land after Assyria's conquest (722 BC), mixed with incoming populations. They had their own version of the Torah and their own temple on Mount Gerizim. Relations between Jews and Samaritans were marked by mutual contempt and hostility. That Jesus makes a Samaritan the hero of the parable was deeply provocative for a Jewish audience.

**Sitting at Someone's Feet:** Mary's position "at the Lord's feet" (verse 39) was the classic posture of a disciple learning from his rabbi (cf. Acts 22:3, where Paul says he was trained "at the feet of Gamaliel"). That a woman assumed this position challenged contemporary norms regarding gender roles in religious instruction.

## Key Terms

**Υἱὸς εἰρήνης (hyios eirēnēs) – "son of peace":** This Semitic expression in verse 6 denotes a person who is receptive to the blessing of peace. In Hebrew idiom, "son of" describes a person's character or affiliation (cf. "sons of thunder" in Mark 3:17). A "son of peace" is thus someone whose heart is open to the peace of the gospel.

**Δηνάρια (dēnaria) – denarii:** The two denarii in verse 35 represented two days' wages for a laborer. This was sufficient to cover lodging at an inn for several weeks, demonstrating the Samaritan's generosity.

**Πανδοχεῖον (pandocheion) – inn:** This word literally means "a place that receives all." Unlike private guesthouses, this was a commercial establishment along main roads where travelers could obtain food, shelter, and care for their animals in exchange for payment.

**Ἐσπλαγχνίσθη (esplanchnisthē) – "he had compassion":** This strong verb in verse 33 comes from *splanchna* (innards), which in antiquity were regarded as the seat of deepest emotions. The word expresses a physical, visceral reaction of deep compassion—not merely sympathy, but a feeling that grips one's entire inner being.

**Διακονία (diakonia) – service:** Martha's "service" in verse 40 refers to practical household work and hospitality. The word is later used in the early church for various forms of Christian ministry and gives rise to the office of deacon. The tension in the story is not that service is unimportant, but about choosing between what is good and what is best.
