# Historical and Cultural Context for Revelation 11

## Places

The chapter focuses on "the holy city" (ἡ πόλις ἡ ἁγία) and the temple (ναός). Jerusalem is clearly identified in verse 8 as the place "where their Lord was crucified". The city is also given the symbolic names "Sodom" and "Egypt" – terms that point to moral decay and oppression of God’s people. The outer court, which is to be "left out" and given to the nations, reflects the actual architecture of the temple with a "court of the Gentiles" separated from the inner areas where only Jews had access.

The temple in Jerusalem was destroyed by the Romans in 70 AD. Thus, the image of the temple and its measuring serves as a symbolic picture of God’s people rather than a description of the physical building. The mention that the nations will "trample" the holy city for forty-two months may allude to Jerusalem’s historical destruction and has parallels with Jesus’ words in Luke 21:24.

## People

The two witnesses (οἱ δύο μάρτυρες) are central figures in the chapter. They are described with traits that clearly draw from Old Testament prophetic figures: Their power to shut up the sky so that no rain falls recalls Elijah (1 Kings 17:1), while the ability to turn water into blood and strike the earth with plagues points to Moses (Exodus 7:14-25). The image of the two olive trees and the two lampstands is taken directly from Zechariah’s prophecy concerning Zerubbabel and Joshua (Zechariah 4:1-14).

In Jewish tradition, there were expectations that Elijah would return before the coming of the Messiah (Malachi 4:5), and some texts also expected that Moses or a prophet like Moses would come (cf. Deuteronomy 18:15). The death, public disgrace, and resurrection after three and a half days of the two witnesses have clear parallels with Jesus’ own fate and reflect the theology of martyr tradition that faithful witness leads through death to vindication.

## Events and Customs

Measuring the temple with a measuring rod (κάλαμος) had deep symbolic meaning in prophetic literature. In Ezekiel 40-48, an angel measures the future temple as a sign of restoration and protection. The act of measuring in Revelation 11 indicates that what is measured belongs to God and will be preserved, while what is left out is destined for judgment.

Putting on sackcloth (σάκκοι) was standard practice for mourning, repentance, and prophetic proclamation in the ancient world. Sackcloth was made of coarse, dark fabric from goat hair. Prophets who proclaimed judgment often wore sackcloth as a visual sign of their message. That the dead bodies were denied burial (verse 9) was considered in antiquity to be the ultimate disgrace – a fate reserved for the worst criminals. In both Jewish and Greco-Roman culture, proper burial was essential for dignity in death.

## Key Terms

The time periods "forty-two months" and "1,260 days" (verses 2-3) both represent three and a half years – half of seven, which was the number of perfection. This period is drawn from Daniel 7:25 and 12:7, where it describes a limited time of trial and persecution. The number symbolizes a bounded period of suffering that God has set limits for.

The expression "the beast that ascends out of the abyss" (τὸ θηρίον τὸ ἀναβαῖνον ἐκ τῆς ἀβύσσου) in verse 7 is the first introduction of a figure further developed in chapters 13 and 17. The abyss (ἄβυσσος) was, in Jewish cosmology, the dwelling place of evil spirits and chaotic forces. The declaration of the seventh trumpet that "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (verse 15) uses "Christ" (χριστός) in its original meaning as "the anointed one" – a royal title with roots in Psalm 2:2.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The ark of the covenant (ἡ κιβωτὸς τῆς διαθήκης) is mentioned in verse 19 as visible in the heavenly temple. The original ark disappeared at the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC and was not present in the second temple. Jewish traditions, preserved in 2 Maccabees 2:4-8, reported that Jeremiah hid the ark on Mount Nebo. John’s vision of the ark in heaven suggests that the heavenly archetype of this central symbol of the covenant endures even though its earthly counterpart is lost – a powerful message about God’s faithfulness to his covenant.
