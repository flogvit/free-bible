# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 22

## People

**Herodians** (Ἡρῳδιανῶν, v. 16) were a political faction that supported the Herodian dynasty and thus indirectly Roman rule. It is remarkable that the Pharisees allied with the Herodians to trap Jesus in his words, since these groups were normally opponents—the Pharisees being religious nationalists, while the Herodians were political pragmatists. This unusual alliance underscores the desperation of the attempt to bring down Jesus.

**Sadducees** (Σαδδουκαῖοι, v. 23) were the aristocratic priestly elite who controlled the temple. They accepted only the books of Moses as authoritative and rejected belief in resurrection, angels, and spirits (cf. Acts 23:8). Their hypothetical question about a woman with seven husbands was designed to mock the belief in resurrection by showing its seemingly absurd consequences.

**The expert in the law** (νομικός, v. 35) was a Pharisee with specialized knowledge of the Mosaic Law. These experts could give authoritative interpretations of the law and were often consulted on difficult questions. The fact that he asked the question "to test Jesus" (πειράζων) suggests this was yet another attempt to trap Jesus in a controversy over legal interpretation.

## Events and Customs

**Royal wedding feast** (γάμους, vv. 2–14): This parable reflects the practice of eastern kings to arrange magnificent wedding celebrations that could last several days. Refusing a royal invitation was a serious insult to the king's honor. The host sending out servants multiple times aligns with the custom where an initial invitation was given, followed by a reminder when everything was ready.

**Wedding garments** (ἔνδυμα γάμου, vv. 11–12): It was common for hosts at large feasts to provide guests with appropriate clothing. The man without wedding attire had thus actively rejected the host's gift, not merely lacked his own clothes. This makes his silence understandable—he had no excuse.

**Levirate marriage** (ἐπιγαμβρεύσει, v. 24): The Sadducees' example is based on the law in Deuteronomy 25:5–6, where a brother was to marry his deceased brother's childless widow to "raise up offspring" for him. This custom was known throughout the ancient Near East and ensured family inheritance and support for the widow.

## Key Terms

**Kēnsos** (κῆνσον, v. 17) is a Latin loanword (*census*) referring to the annual head tax all Jews were required to pay directly to the emperor. This tax, introduced in AD 6, was deeply hated because it symbolized Israel's submission to pagan rule. Many nationalist Jews, including followers of Judas the Galilean who had led revolts against the tax, considered paying it treason against God.

**Denarius** (δηνάριον, v. 19): The coin shown likely bore the image of Emperor Tiberius with the inscription "Tiberius Caesar Divi Augusti Filius Augustus" (Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus) and on the reverse "Pontifex Maximus" (high priest). For devout Jews, both the image (a violation of the prohibition against graven images) and the claim to divinity were offensive.

**Shema and the commandment to love**: Jesus' quotation in verse 37 is from Deuteronomy 6:5, the core of the Shema prayer recited morning and evening by devout Jews. The second commandment (v. 39) is from Leviticus 19:18. Combining these two was not unique to Jesus—similar pairings exist in Jewish tradition—but Jesus' formulation that the entire law "hangs" on these was a striking summary.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Tiberius denarius**: Archaeological finds of denarii from Tiberius's reign confirm the accuracy of the description of the coin with the emperor's image and inscription. Such coins have been found in large numbers throughout Israel, including Jerusalem, showing they were in common circulation during Jesus' time. This supports both the historical background for the question about taxes and the religious issue concerning carrying and using coins bearing claims to divinity.
