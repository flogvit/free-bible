## Key Concepts

Psalm 37 is an **alphabetic acrostic psalm** in which approximately every other verse begins with a new letter of the Hebrew alphabet (with some variations in the pattern). This literary form was used as a memory aid and signaled completeness—from א (alef) to ת (tav)—covering all of life's wisdom.

The Hebrew verb **חרה** (*ḥarah*, "to become angry/resentful") in the hitpael form **תִּתְחַר** (*titḥar*) is used three times (vv. 1, 7, 8) and describes the inner unrest a believer may feel when the wicked seem to prosper. The psalm warns against this emotional reaction because it can lead to sin.

The expression **ירש ארץ** (*yarash eretz*, "inherit/possess the land") appears five times (vv. 9, 11, 22, 29, 34) and links the psalm to the covenant promises in Deuteronomy. In the wisdom tradition, this promise of land ownership became a symbol of God's blessing and security in general.

The word **ענוים** (*anavim*, "the meek/humble") in verse 11 does not describe weakness, but rather an attitude of dependence on God rather than self-assertion. Jesus quotes this verse directly in the Beatitudes (Matt 5:5).

## Events and Customs

The psalm's repeated contrast between the righteous who "lend" (vv. 21, 26) and the wicked who "borrow but do not repay" reflects the economic ethics of Israelite society. The Mosaic Law required generous lending to the poor without interest (Exod 22:25) and forgiveness of debts in the sabbatical year. A person's willingness to lend was seen as a sign of reverence for God and trust in His provision, while failure to repay indicated moral decay.

The image of the wicked who "draw the sword and bend their bow" (v. 14) to harm the poor reflects the social injustice often criticized in wisdom literature—powerful people using their position to oppress vulnerable groups in society.
