# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 13

## People

**Jehoahaz** (ca. 814–798 B.C.) was the son of Jehu and king over Israel (the Northern Kingdom) during a period of severe military weakening. His name means "The Lord has grasped" or "The Lord holds fast." During his reign, Israel's army was reduced to only 50 horsemen, 10 chariots, and 10,000 foot soldiers—a dramatic decline that testifies to the devastating campaigns of the Arameans.

**Joash of Israel** (ca. 798–782 B.C.) was Jehoahaz's son and demonstrated greater military skill. He regained cities that had been lost to the Arameans. His visit to the dying Elisha shows that even kings who "did what was evil" could seek prophetic guidance in times of crisis.

**Hazael** was king of Aram-Damascus and one of the most powerful rulers of antiquity at this time. Assyrian inscriptions from Shalmaneser III mention him as "Hazaʾilu of Damascus." Archaeological finds, including the Tel Dan Stele, confirm his military campaigns against Israel.

**Ben-Hadad III**, Hazael's son, inherited a weakened kingdom after Assyria had pressured Aram-Damascus. This made possible Israel's recapture of lost territories.

## Places

**Samaria** remained the capital of the Northern Kingdom and is mentioned as the burial place of both Jehoahaz and Joash. Archaeological excavations have revealed royal buildings and ivory decorations from this period. Verse 6 notes that the Asherah pole still stood in the city, testifying to ongoing syncretistic worship.

**Aphek** (v. 17) was a strategically important location, traditionally situated east of the Sea of Galilee, along the main route between Aram and Israel. It was here that Elisha prophesied that Israel would strike down the Arameans.

## Events and Customs

**The symbolic arrow ceremony** (vv. 15–19) represents a prophetic sign-act (Hebrew: *ʾôt*), in which physical actions carry prophetic and effective meaning. Elisha's laying of hands on the king's hands while he shot transferred divine power to the action. The fact that the king struck the ground with the arrows only three times limited the scope of future victory—an example of how human response affects prophetic promises.

**The touching of Elisha's bones** (v. 21), which revived a dead man, belongs to a tradition in which a prophet's power continues even after death. Moabite raids "at the beginning of the year" (*baʾ shanah*, v. 20) refer to spring, when roads were passable and raids were common. The use of family burial sites and reuse of graves was common practice in Israel.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the chariot and horsemen of Israel"** (*rekhev yisraʾel ufarashayw*, v. 14) is identical to what Elisha himself cried out when Elijah was taken up (2 Kgs 2:12). This honorific title recognizes the prophet as Israel's true defense—more important than military strength.

The term **"savior"** (*moshiaʿ*, v. 5) is used here of a human deliverer whom God raises up. The identity is debated—possibly the Assyrian king Adad-nirari III, who weakened Aram, or Joash himself. This is the same word later used for the Messiah.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Tel Dan Stele**, discovered in 1993–94, is an Aramaic inscription from Hazael boasting of victories over "the king of Israel" and "the house of David." This confirms the Aramean dominance described in the first part of the chapter and provides extra-biblical testimony to the conflict.

**Assyrian annals** from Adad-nirari III (810–783 B.C.) mention campaigns against "Mari", likely Aram-Damascus, where he received tribute. This weakened Aram and made possible Israel's recapture under Joash, which fits with the biblical account of a turning point in verses 22–25.
