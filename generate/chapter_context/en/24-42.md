# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 42

## People

The chapter introduces several key leadership figures among the remnant in Judah. **Johanan son of Kareach** (יוֹחָנָן בֶּן־קָרֵחַ) was the foremost military leader who had gathered the remaining Judeans after Gedaliah's murder (chapters 40–41). **Jezaniah son of Hoshaiah** (יְזַנְיָה בֶּן־הוֹשַׁעְיָה) is mentioned as another prominent officer. The name Jezaniah means "Yahweh hears"—an ironic detail given that he and the other leaders later refuse to listen to the Lord's answer. The expression "the commanders" (שָׂרֵי הַחֲיָלִים) refers to military officers who had avoided deportation, likely because they had operated as guerrilla groups outside Jerusalem during the siege.

## Places

Two geographical poles dominate the theology of this chapter: **this land** (הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת) and **Egypt** (מִצְרַיִם). At this time, the remnant was located at Gureth-Kimhan near Bethlehem (41:17), with plans to go to Egypt. In the people's argumentation, Egypt is portrayed as a refuge without war, without the sound of trumpets, and without hunger (v. 14). This reflects Egypt's historical role as a granary and place of safety in times of crisis. At the same time, fleeing to Egypt represented theological regression—a return to the land of slavery from which Israel had been delivered. Jeremiah warns that precisely the plagues they are fleeing will overtake them there.

## Events and Customs

The people's appeal to Jeremiah follows an established practice for seeking God's will through a prophet. The expression "let our plea fall before your face" (תִּפָּל־נָא תְחִנָּתֵנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ) uses a respectful formula that acknowledges the prophet's role as an intermediary. The ten days Jeremiah waits for an answer (v. 7) show that prophetic revelation could not be forced but had to be received in God's own time. That the people called upon the Lord as witness with the oath "May the Lord be a true and faithful witness against us" (v. 5) was a solemn commitment with serious consequences if broken.

## Key Terms

The chapter uses several theologically charged expressions. **"To build and to plant"** versus **"to tear down and to uproot"** (v. 10)—here God uses language of promise, showing that He intends to restore the people if they remain in the land. The verb נָחַם ("repent/comfort") in verse 10 expresses that God has "relented" from disaster—an anthropomorphic expression for God's willingness to change His course of action toward an obedient people. The threat of becoming "a curse, a horror, a reproach, and a taunt" (לְאָלָה וּלְשַׁמָּה וְלִקְלָלָה וּלְחֶרְפָּה, v. 18) is a fourfold formula describing total social and religious destruction.
