# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 7

## Key Terms

The chapter is dominated by the Hebrew word קֵץ (*qēṣ*, "end"), which is repeated several times in verses 2–6. This term denotes not merely a conclusion, but a definitive and irreversible judgment. The wordplay in verse 6 between קֵץ (*qēṣ*, "end") and הֵקִיץ (*hēqîṣ*, "has awakened") reinforces the message: the end has "awakened" over the people.

The expression צְפִירָה (*ṣəfîrâ*) in verses 7 and 10 is difficult to translate. This word appears in this form only in Ezekiel 7 in the Old Testament. It may mean "wreath" or "doom" and seems to refer to an approaching catastrophe that draws near those who dwell in the land.

In verse 22, צְפוּנִי (*ṣəfûnî*, "my hidden treasure") is mentioned, which likely refers either to the Most Holy Place within the temple or to the temple as a whole as God's precious possession.

## Events and Customs

Verses 12–13 contain references to the Jubilee legislation (Leviticus 25), under which sold land was to be returned to its original owner every fiftieth year. Ezekiel declares that this arrangement is now irrelevant—the buyer shall not rejoice, nor the seller mourn—because judgment overturns all normal economic relations.

Mourning rituals are described in verses 17–18: limp hands, trembling knees, sackcloth as clothing, shame on the face, and a shaved head. Shaving the head was a sign of deep grief in the ancient Near East. Such practices were forbidden for priests (Leviticus 21:5) and restricted for all people in times of mourning (Deuteronomy 14:1).

Verse 19 describes how silver and gold are cast into the streets as "unclean" (נִדָּה, *niddâ*). This word is commonly used to describe ritual impurity, especially menstrual uncleanness. The fact that precious metals become like something unclean reveals a total overturning of values.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered extensive destruction from the Babylonian siege in 586 B.C.E. In the ash layers from this period, valuable items left behind in the ruins have been found—jewelry, silver, and gold—which corresponds with Ezekiel's prophecy that wealth will be worthless on the day of judgment (verse 19). Letters discovered at Lachish from the same period (the Lachish letters) confirm the desperate situation in Judah just before Jerusalem's fall and mention that signal fires from other cities had ceased burning.
