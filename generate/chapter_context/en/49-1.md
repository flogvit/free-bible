# Historical and Cultural Context for Ephesians 1

## Key Concepts

This chapter introduces central theological concepts through Greek terms with special cultural significance. The word **υἱοθεσία** (hyiothesia, "adoption as sons", v. 5) was a well-known legal term in Roman law. When a person was adopted, they received full inheritance rights and all the privileges of a biological son. The adopted person was legally freed from their former family and given an entirely new identity. This image was especially powerful for readers living under Roman law.

The concept **ἀπολύτρωσις** (apolytrosis, "redemption/deliverance", vv. 7, 14) is drawn from the slave market, where it described the price paid to free a slave. **Ἀρραβών** (arrabōn, "down payment/pledge", v. 14) was a commercial term for an advance payment that guaranteed full payment later—a concept well known in the trading city of Ephesus. The word **σφραγίζω** (sphragizō, "to seal", v. 13) refers to the practice of sealing documents or goods with a seal that indicated ownership and authenticity.

## Events and Customs

The praise in verses 3–14 follows the structure of a **berakah**, a Jewish blessing formula that begins with "Blessed be God" (Εὐλογητὸς ὁ θεός). This liturgical form was well known from synagogue worship and Jewish prayer practice. Paul adapts this Jewish tradition to a Christian context by focusing on the blessings that come through Christ. The entire passage in Greek forms one long sentence (vv. 3–14), reflecting the elevated, praising style of Jewish liturgy.

## Textual Critical Notes

The textual critical brackets around **[ἐν Ἐφέσῳ]** ("in Ephesus") in verse 1 reflect that several early and important manuscripts, including Papyrus 46 (c. AD 200) and the original versions of Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, lack the place name. This has led to discussion about whether the letter was originally a circular letter intended for multiple churches in Asia Minor, where the place name could be filled in locally. Regardless of how this question is resolved, the content of the letter remains relevant to the church in Ephesus as one of its primary recipients.
