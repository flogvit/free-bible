# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 27

## Key Concepts

### Leviathan and the Sea Monster
The chapter opens with God’s judgment upon "Leviathan" (לִוְיָתָן), described as "the fleeing serpent" (נָחָשׁ בָּרִחַ) and "the twisting serpent" (נָחָשׁ עֲקַלָּתוֹן), as well as "the dragon" (הַתַּנִּין) – the sea monster. These mythological creatures were known from Canaanite and Mesopotamian literature. In Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (circa 1400–1200 BCE), Lotan/Litan is mentioned as a seven-headed serpent that the god Baal defeats. Isaiah uses this imagery theologically to describe God’s final victory over chaos and evil powers – possibly symbols of oppressive empires that threatened Israel.

### The Vineyard (כֶּרֶם)
The song of the vineyard in verses 2–6 is a deliberate contrast to the song of judgment in Isaiah 5:1–7, where the vineyard produced wild grapes. Here, God promises to care for His vineyard (Israel) with tenderness. The Hebrew expression "כֶּרֶם חֶמֶד" (a delightful vineyard) signals restoration. Vine growing was central to Judah’s economy, and this image communicated concrete hope for renewal.

### Asherah Poles and Incense Altars
Verse 9 mentions "אֲשֵׁרִים" (Asherah poles) and "חַמָּנִים" (incense altars/sun pillars). The Asherah poles were cult objects associated with the Canaanite goddess Asherah, often placed near sanctuaries. The incense altars were likely connected to sun worship. Archaeological finds from Judah, including pillar figurines and high places, confirm the widespread nature of such syncretistic practices. The text’s demand that these be broken reflects the Deuteronomic reform movement’s struggle against foreign cults.

## Places

### "The River" to "the Brook of Egypt"
Verse 12 describes a geographical area from "שִׁבֹּלֶת הַנָּהָר" (the stream of the Euphrates) to "נַחַל מִצְרָיִם" (the Brook of Egypt, likely Wadi el-Arish on the Sinai Peninsula). This marks the idealized extent of the Promised Land (cf. Gen 15:18). The image of God "threshing grain" (יַחְבֹּט) from this area uses agricultural metaphor – threshing to separate grain from chaff – as a symbol that God will gather His people from exile.

### The Holy Mountain in Jerusalem
The chapter culminates with the return to "הַר הַקֹּדֶשׁ בִּירוּשָׁלָם" (the holy mountain in Jerusalem), that is, Zion/the temple hill. This was the center of Yahweh worship and symbolized God’s presence among His people.

## Events and Customs

### The Great Trumpet
The "שׁוֹפָר גָּדוֹל" (great trumpet) in verse 13 had several functions in Israelite culture: calling assemblies for festivals, warning of danger, and proclaiming the Year of Jubilee (release). Here it signals the eschatological gathering of the "lost" (הָאֹבְדִים) in Assyria and the "driven out" (הַנִּדָּחִים) in Egypt. This reflects the historical reality of Israelites scattered to both great powers – the fall of the northern kingdom to Assyria (722 BCE) and refugees seeking refuge in Egypt during various crises.
