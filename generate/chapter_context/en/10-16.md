## People

**Ziba** (צִיבָא) was a servant of Saul’s household and the manager of the estate belonging to Mephibosheth, son of Jonathan. His role in this chapter is controversial – he comes to David with supplies and accuses his master Mephibosheth of staying in Jerusalem in hopes of regaining Saul’s throne. In chapter 19, Mephibosheth gives a completely different account and claims that Ziba betrayed him. David’s hasty decision to transfer all of Mephibosheth’s property to Ziba without investigating the matter further shows the king in a weak and vulnerable situation.

**Shimei son of Gera** (שִׁמְעִי בֶן־גֵּרָא) was from Bahurim and belonged to Saul’s clan. His cursing of David as a "man of blood" (אִישׁ הַדָּמִים) reflects an attitude that may have existed among the Benjamites – that David was responsible for the downfall of Saul’s house. The expression "worthless man" (אִישׁ הַבְּלִיָּעַל) was a serious insult implying worthlessness and evil.

**Hushai the Archite** (חוּשַׁי הָאַרְכִּי) is described as David’s "friend" (רֵעֶה), which was an official court position known from Egyptian and other Eastern courts. The title "king’s friend" denoted a trusted advisor with close access to the king.

## Places

**Bahurim** (בַּחוּרִים) was a Benjamite village east of Jerusalem on the road down toward the Jordan Valley. The place is mentioned several times in connection with David’s flight and was clearly a center of pro-Saul sentiment. Its geographical location – on David’s escape route eastward – meant he had to pass through this hostile area.

**The roof of the royal palace** (הַגָּג) in Jerusalem was where the tent was set up for Absalom. This was the same roof where David had seen Bathsheba (2 Sam 11:2). The roof was the most visible place in the city, and the action was intended to create maximum publicity.

## Events and Customs

Throwing stones and dust (verses 6, 13) expressed intense contempt and cursing. In ancient Israel, such actions were associated with mourning rituals and curses. That Shimei threw both stones and dust at the king and his entourage was an extreme insult that normally would have carried the death penalty.

Taking the fleeing king’s concubines (פִּלַגְשֵׁי) was a political act signaling complete takeover of power. In the ancient Near East, royal concubines belonged to the throne, not to the person. By taking them publicly, Absalom demonstrated that his break with his father was irreversible and that he was now the legitimate king. The public nature of the act – "in the sight of all Israel" – was crucial for its political effect.

## Key Terms

The expression **"man of blood"** (אִישׁ הַדָּמִים) in verses 7–8 refers to someone who is guilty of shedding blood. Shimei accuses David of being responsible for the blood of "Saul’s house," possibly referring to events connected with the conflict between David and Saul’s household.

**Ahithophel’s counsel** (עֲצַת אֲחִיתֹפֶל) is described as "asking God" (כַּאֲשֶׁר יִשְׁאַל אִישׁ בִּדְבַר הָאֱלֹהִים). This was the highest recognition of an advisor’s wisdom – his words were regarded as oracular and infallible. This description heightens the seriousness of the situation David faces.
