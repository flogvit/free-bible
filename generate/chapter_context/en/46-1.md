# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 1

## People

**Sosthenes** (v. 1) is mentioned as a co-sender of the letter. This is likely the same Sosthenes referred to in Acts 18:17 as a synagogue ruler in Corinth. According to Luke, he was beaten by the crowd before the tribunal of proconsul Gallio. If it is the same person, he later became a Christian and joined Paul—a remarkable conversion story from synagogue leader to Christian "brother."

**Chloe's people** (v. 11) are a group who brought news to Paul about divisions in Corinth. Chloe (Greek Χλόη, "green shoot") was a name associated with the goddess Demeter, suggesting a Greco-pagan background. The fact that the group is identified by her name indicates she was a female head of household—either a wealthy widow or a freedwoman who conducted business. Her "people" may have been slaves, freedpersons, or family members traveling between Corinth and Ephesus (where Paul was likely located).

**Crispus** (v. 14) is identified in Acts 18:8 as a synagogue ruler who came to faith along with his entire household. He was one of the few people Paul baptized personally. **Gaius** (v. 14)—one of the few individuals Paul baptized personally in Corinth. Possibly identical to the Gaius mentioned in Romans 16:23, who hosted the church. **Stephanas' household** (v. 16)—Paul recalls that he also baptized this group. They are referred to later in the letter (16:15) as "the firstfruits of Achaia."

## Events and Customs

**Factionalism** (v. 12)—members identifying themselves with different leaders ("I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos")—reflects a widespread practice in Greco-Roman culture. Philosophical schools and rhetorical teachers gathered followers around them, and students defined their identity through affiliation with a particular master. In Corinth, a city known for its rhetorical culture, it was natural that new converts transferred this mindset into church life. **Cephas** (Aramaic for "rock," Peter) may represent a Jewish-Christian faction, while the "Christ group" possibly consisted of those who claimed to stand above all human leaders.

**Baptism in someone's name** (vv. 13–15) was an act that created allegiance. Paul's concern that some might claim to be baptized "in Paul's name" shows that the baptismal rite could be misunderstood as a loyalty pact to the one performing it—similar to patron-client relationships in Roman culture.

## Key Terms

**Σχίσματα** (schismata, v. 10)—"divisions" or "splits"—is used here not of formal schisms but of partisanship and cliques within the church. The word could refer to tears in clothing or cracks in rocks, illustrating how the unity of the church was beginning to unravel.

**Σοφία λόγου** (sophia logou, v. 17)—"wisdom of words" or "eloquence"—refers to the rhetorical skill highly valued in Greek culture. Corinth was a center for sophistic rhetoric, and speakers competed for followers based on eloquence. Paul distances himself from this approach to protect "the message of the cross" (λόγος τοῦ σταυροῦ).

**Σκάνδαλον** (skandalon, v. 23)—"stumbling block" or "offense"—for Jews meant the idea of a crucified Messiah, since the law declared that "cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree" (Deut 21:23). **Μωρία** (mōria)—"foolishness"—was the Gentile assessment: a executed criminal as divine wisdom was absurd in Greek philosophical thought.
