# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 6

## People

**Noah** (נֹחַ, *Noach*) is introduced as the central figure in the flood narrative. His name derives from the Hebrew root נוח (*nuach*, "rest"). Noah is described with three characteristics: *tsaddiq* (righteous), *tamim* (blameless/perfect), and that he "walked with God"—the same expression used for Enoch in the previous chapter. These descriptions position Noah as a contrast to the moral decay that characterized his time.

**Noah's sons**—Shem, Ham, and Japheth—are mentioned as Noah's three sons (v. 10). The order of names likely reflects theological and narrative significance rather than birth order, with Shem (the ancestor of Israel) listed first.

## Key Terms

**"Sons of God"** (בְנֵי־הָאֱלֹהִים, *benei ha-elohim*) is a debated expression. In the Old Testament, it refers to angelic beings (Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7), but some interpretations view it as a reference to the godly descendants of Seth or powerful rulers. The term reflects a concept widespread in the ancient Near East regarding the blurring of boundaries between the divine and human spheres.

**The 120 years** (v. 3) are debated. The expression can be understood either as a limitation on human lifespan going forward, or as a period of grace before the judgment of the flood—a time for repentance. The latter interpretation is supported by the context in which God announces coming judgment.

**Nephilim** (הַנְּפִלִים) are often translated as "giants." Their etymology is uncertain but may be related to the root *nafal* ("to fall") or to the Akkadian *napālu* ("to destroy"). They are described as *gibborim* (mighty men) and "men of renown from ancient times" (אַנְשֵׁי הַשֵּׁם). This may reflect memories of a mythical past or serve as polemic against hero cults in surrounding cultures where semi-divine warriors were honored.

**Chamâs** (חָמָס) is translated as "violence" and describes the condition of the earth. The word covers a broad spectrum of injustice: violence, oppression, lawlessness, and violation of others' rights. It is the moral cause of the coming judgment.

## Events and Customs

**Construction of the ark** is described with specific dimensions: 300 × 50 × 30 cubits (approximately 135 × 22.5 × 13.5 meters based on a standard cubit of about 45 cm). The word for ark (תֵּבָה, *tevah*) is otherwise used only for the basket in which Moses was placed (Exodus 2:3). It is not a common ship term but denotes a closed box for protection. The material *gofer* wood (עֲצֵי־גֹפֶר) is unknown; various suggestions include cypress, cedar, or a resin-rich tree species. *Kopher* (כֹּפֶר, pitch/tar) used for sealing shares a root with the word for "atonement" (*kippur*/כִּפֻּר), a linguistic connection that may carry theological resonance.

**The covenant** (בְּרִית, *berit*) is mentioned here for the first time in the Bible (v. 18). This is a central concept in Israelite religion—a binding agreement between God and humanity. The fact that God establishes a covenant with Noah before judgment emphasizes that salvation precedes judgment.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Flood narratives exist in many ancient cultures. Mesopotamian parallels are especially relevant: the *Atrahasis* epic (c. 1700 BCE) and the *Epic of Gilgamesh* (tablet XI) contain stories about a great flood, a man who builds a vessel by divine instruction, and the saving of animal life. In the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, however, the ark is cube-shaped, and the gods' motive is noise from humanity rather than moral evil. The biblical and Mesopotamian narratives share a common cultural memory, but the biblical version has a clear theological profile: one God, moral judgment, and covenantal salvation.
