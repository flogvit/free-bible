## Places

### Egypt (מִצְרַיִם)
The chapter opens with "Woe to those who go down to Egypt" (v. 1), referring to the political situation in which Judah sought military support from Egypt against Assyria. Egypt was known for its cavalry, and the expression "rely on horses" reflects that the kingdom of Judah sought Egyptian chariots and riders as a counterweight to Assyrian military superiority. Archaeological sources, including Assyrian annals, confirm that Egypt sent troops northward during this period, but they were defeated at Eltekeh.

### Mount Zion (הַר־צִיּוֹן)
Verses 4–5 state that the Lord will "come down to fight on Mount Zion and on its heights" and "protect Jerusalem". This connects with Zion theology, the belief that God had chosen Jerusalem as his dwelling place and would protect the city. The prophecy of the Lord’s "fire on Zion" and "furnace in Jerusalem" (v. 9) contains motifs of judgment directed against Assyria—God’s consuming wrath will strike the enemy who threatens his city.

## Events and Customs

### Egyptian Military Aid and Horse Trade
Judah’s attempt to obtain Egyptian horses and chariots violated the Deuteronomic prohibition that the king should not "acquire many horses" or "send the people back to Egypt to acquire more horses" (Deut 17:16). Isaiah’s condemnation is therefore both theological and political—the alliance represented a lack of trust in the Lord and a return to the land of slavery.

### The Passover Motif
The verb פָּסֹחַ ("pāsōaḥ") in verse 5—"pass over and spare"—is the same word used concerning the angel of Passover in Exodus 12. Isaiah deliberately uses this Exodus image to suggest that God will again spare Jerusalem just as he spared the firstborn of Israel in Egypt. This creates an ironic contrast: the people seek help from Egypt, but God will save them in the same way he saved them from Egypt.

## Key Concepts

### "Flesh" versus "Spirit" (בָּשָׂר / רוּחַ)
Verse 3 contrasts Egypt as "human (אָדָם) and not God" and their horses as "flesh (בָּשָׂר) and not spirit (רוּחַ)". This word pair expresses a fundamental distinction in Hebrew thought between the perishable, weak, and earthly (basar) and the divine, life-giving, and powerful (ruach). Egypt’s military power belongs to the human sphere and therefore cannot provide true salvation.

### "Idols" (אֱלִילִים)
The word in verse 7 is a contemptuous pun—*'ĕlīlīm* resembles *'ĕlōhīm* (gods), but means "nothingnesses" or "empty things", possibly related to *'al* (not/nothing). The silver and gold images that the people had made, they would discard "on that day", which historically may be linked to Hezekiah’s religious reforms.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

### Sennacherib’s Siege and the Fate of the Assyrian Army
Verses 8–9 prophesy that Assyria will fall "by a sword not made by human hands". The historical event this points to—the sudden withdrawal of the Assyrian army from Jerusalem in 701 B.C.E.—is documented both in 2 Kings 19:35–36 and in Sennacherib’s own prisms. Remarkably, Sennacherib boasts of having confined Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage", but never mentions capturing Jerusalem—a telling omission that confirms the siege failed.
