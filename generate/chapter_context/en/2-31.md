# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 31

## People

The chapter introduces two craftsmen by name: **Bezalel son of Uri, son of Hur** from the tribe of Judah, and **Oholiab son of Ahisamach** from the tribe of Dan. The name Bezalel means "in the shadow of God" or "in the shadow of El" (בְּצַלְאֵל, from בְּ 'in', צֵל 'shadow' and אֵל 'God'), suggesting divine protection. According to 1 Chron 2:19-20, Bezalel was a great-grandson of Caleb through Hur. The fact that one craftsman came from the leading tribe of Judah and another from Dan may symbolize that representatives of the entire people participated in the sanctuary work.

## Events and Customs

**God-given craftsmanship:** The text describes how Bezalel is filled with "the Spirit of God" (רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים) for craftwork. This was unusual in the ancient world—while Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts often attributed artistic skill to the teaching of the gods, the direct divine equipping of a craftsman with "wisdom, understanding, and knowledge" is a distinct Israelite concept that elevated practical work to spiritual service.

**The Sabbath commandment:** The Sabbath legislation in verses 12-17 is placed strategically between the instructions for the tabernacle and its construction. Rabbinic tradition interpreted this placement to mean that even the holy work of building the tabernacle had to yield to the Sabbath. The thirty-nine categories of forbidden Sabbath labor in later Jewish law (Mishnah Shabbat 7:2) were derived precisely from the types of work required to build the tabernacle.

## Key Terms

**שַׁבַּת שַׁבָּתוֹן (shabbat shabbaton):** This intensified expression in verse 15, often translated "a Sabbath of complete rest" or "Sabbath rest," emphasizes the absolute character of the Sabbath. It is used only for the Sabbath and the greatest festivals.

**אוֹת (ot) – "sign":** The Sabbath is called a "sign" between God and Israel (v. 13, 17). In the ancient Near East, signs functioned as covenant symbols and markers of identity. Thus, the Sabbath became a weekly, visible proof of Israel's special covenant relationship with God.

**לֻחֹת הָעֵדֻת (lukhot ha'edut) – "tablets of the testimony":** The stone tablets in verse 18 are called "testimony" because they served as a covenant document. In Hittite and Mesopotamian vassal treaties, copies of the agreement were placed in temples—in the same way, Israel's covenant tablets were to be kept in the ark of the covenant within the sanctuary.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The expression "written with the finger of God" (כְּתֻבִים בְּאֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים) in verse 18 has interesting parallels. In Egyptian texts, hieroglyphs are referred to as "the words of the gods," and writing was attributed to the god Thoth. Stone tablets containing laws and treaties are well documented archaeologically from the second millennium B.C., including the Law Stele of Hammurabi and numerous cuneiform tablets from Mari and Ugarit. The fact that the law was written on stone emphasized its permanent and unchangeable character.
