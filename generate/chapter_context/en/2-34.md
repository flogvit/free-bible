# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 34

## People

Moses (מֹשֶׁה) stands at the center of this chapter as the mediator between God and Israel. He hews out new stone tablets (v. 1, 4) after the first ones were broken (chapter 32), emphasizing his role as covenant intermediary. Aaron (אַהֲרֹן) is mentioned as the one who first sees Moses' radiant face and reacts with fear (v. 30). The "leaders" (נְשִׂאִים, *nesi'im*) of the congregation are also noted—these were tribal chieftains who represented the twelve tribes and served as the people's administrative leaders.

## Places

Mount Sinai (הַר סִינַי) is the location of this second revelation. The exact location of the mountain is debated, but it has traditionally been identified with Jebel Musa on the Sinai Peninsula. The mountain is portrayed as a holy place where God meets Moses, and strict restrictions apply—neither people nor animals may approach (v. 3). This reflects the Old Testament concept of holy zones with graded access.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains a renewal of the covenant following the golden calf incident and presents what is often called "the ritual decalogue" or "cultic commands" (vv. 11–26). These commands focus on the festival calendar and cultic practice: the Feast of Unleavened Bread (חַג הַמַּצּוֹת), the Feast of Weeks (חַג שָׁבֻעֹת), and the Feast of Ingathering (חַג הָאָסִיף). The requirement that all men appear before the Lord three times a year was common in the ancient Near East, where regular pilgrimages to sanctuaries were central to religious life.

The prohibition against boiling a young goat in its mother's milk (v. 26) has parallels in Canaanite religion. Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra mention a similar practice in fertility rituals, which may explain why this is forbidden—it marks a distinction from Canaanite worship.

## Key Terms

The word קָרַן (*qaran*, vv. 29–35), describing Moses' face, comes from the root for "horn" and here means "to shine" or "to radiate light." The Vulgate translated this as *cornuta* ("horned"), which led to Michelangelo's famous statue of Moses having horns. This phenomenon expresses that Moses had been in direct contact with the divine.

God's self-revelation in vv. 6–7 is often called "the thirteen attributes" or "middot" (שְׁלֹשׁ עֶשְׂרֵה מִדּוֹת) in Jewish tradition and became central to synagogue liturgy. The expression אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם (*erekh appayim*, "slow to anger," literally "long of nose") refers to the ancient idea that anger was shown through rapid breathing through the nose.

The word מַסְוֶה (*masveh*, "veil/covering," vv. 33–35) occurs only here in the Old Testament. The veil served as a barrier between the holy (represented by Moses' radiant face) and the people, illustrating the biblical principle that humans cannot endure direct contact with God's glory.
