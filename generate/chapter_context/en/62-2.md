# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 John 2

## Key Terms

**Parakletos (παράκλητος)** – In verse 1, this Greek word is used of Jesus as an "advocate" or "defender" before the Father. The term has a legal background and denotes someone who speaks on another’s behalf in court, a lawyer or mediator. In the Greco-Roman legal system, a parakletos was a person of high social status who spoke for the accused. John uses the same word for the Holy Spirit in the Gospel of John (14:16, 26), but here it is uniquely applied to Christ in his heavenly ministry.

**Hilasmos (ἱλασμός)** – The word in verse 2, translated "atoning sacrifice" or "propitiation", has deep roots in both Greek religion and Jewish sacrificial worship. In the Septuagint, related words refer to the mercy seat on the ark of the covenant and to the atonement rituals on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur). For Greek readers, the word would evoke associations with appeasing the gods, but John reshapes the concept: God himself provides the atonement through Christ.

**Chrisma (χρῖσμα)** – "Anointing" in verses 20 and 27 refers to the gift of the Holy Spirit given to believers. The term plays on the Greek word "Christos" (the anointed one) and connects believers with the Messiah. In the Old Testament, kings, priests, and prophets were anointed with oil as a sign of God’s choice and empowerment. John democratizes this concept: all believers have received this anointing.

**Antichristos (ἀντίχριστος)** – This term, which appears for the first time in verses 18 and 22, is unique to John’s letters throughout the Bible. The prefix "anti-" can mean both "against" and "in place of". John applies it both to a expected end-time figure and to contemporary false teachers who have already left the community.

## Events and Customs

**Generations in the Church** – In verses 12–14, John addresses three groups: "children" (teknia/paidia), "fathers" (pateres), and "young men" (neaniskoi). This reflects the typical structure of a house church in the Greco-Roman world, where multiple generations gathered in private homes. The head of the household (paterfamilias) traditionally held authority, but John recognizes that all age groups have their own spiritual experience and role. The older ones have long-standing knowledge of Christ; the young have strength for spiritual struggle.

**The World’s Desires** – The triad in verse 16—"the desires of the flesh, the desires of the eyes, and pride in possessions"—describes typical temptations in Hellenistic urban culture. Ephesus and other cities in Asia Minor were marked by material wealth, status symbols, and sensual pleasure. Public baths, theaters, athletic competitions, and religious festivals promoted a lifestyle that often conflicted with Christian ethics. John does not warn against creation itself, but against the world system that stands in opposition to God.

## People

**"Those who went out from us"** – Verse 19 identifies a group that broke away from the church. These were likely followers of early Gnostic or docetic ideas that denied Jesus was the Messiah come in the flesh. The church father Irenaeus (c. AD 180) mentions that Cerinthus, an early false teacher active in the Ephesus area, taught that "Christ", as a spiritual being, descended upon the man Jesus at his baptism but left him before the crucifixion. Such divisions were painful for the early churches, and John interprets their departure theologically: they went out because they never truly belonged to the community.
