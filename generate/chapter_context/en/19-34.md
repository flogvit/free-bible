# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 34

## People

The superscription of the psalm links it to a specific event in David's life when he "pretended" (changed his behavior) before "Abimelech". This creates an interesting historical challenge: In 1 Samuel 21:11–16, we are told that David fled to King Achish of Gath and pretended to be insane to save his life. "Abimelech" (which means "my father is king") was likely a dynastic title for the Philistine kings, similar to "Pharaoh" in Egypt, while "Achish" was the king's personal name. Alternatively, this could be a scribal variation. David's desperate act—pretending to be insane by drooling on his beard and scratching at the doors—became his deliverance, which explains the psalm's intense gratitude.

## Events and Customs

The psalm is an acrostic poem, where each verse line begins with a new letter of the Hebrew alphabet in order. This literary form had a pedagogical function and aided memorization. Interestingly, the letter *vav* (ו) is missing from this psalm, while *pe* (פ) appears twice (verses 17 and 23), showing that acrostic patterns could be adapted to fit the content.

## Key Terms

The expression "טַעֲמוּ וּרְאוּ" (*ta'amu ur'u*, "taste and see") in verse 9 is remarkable because it invites a sensory, experience-based faith. This phrase was later quoted in 1 Peter 2:3. The verb "טעם" (*ta'am*) means both "to taste" and "to experience/understand," creating a rich double meaning.

The term "מַלְאַךְ־יְהוָה" (*malakh YHWH*, "the angel of the Lord") in verse 8 refers to a special divine messenger who often appears in the Old Testament as God's visible representative. The statement that the angel "encamps around" those who fear God uses military terminology that would have been meaningful to David as both a warrior and a fugitive.
