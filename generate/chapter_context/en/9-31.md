# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 31

## People

**Saul’s sons** who fall in this chapter are Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malki-Shua. Jonathan is well known from the narrative, while Abinadab (possibly the same as Ishvi in 1 Sam 14:49, though this is uncertain) and Malki-Shua are mentioned less frequently. A fourth son, Ish-Bosheth, is not mentioned here and survived the battle. The **armor-bearer** who refused to kill Saul and then took his own life remains unnamed, but his role was significant—armor-bearers were trusted men who accompanied their lord in battle and carried his extra weapons.

## Places

**Mount Gilboa** (הַר הַגִּלְבֹּעַ) lies southeast of the Jezreel Valley, strategically positioned between the Philistine coastal plain and the Israelite hill country. The mountain extends about 16 km and rises to 500 meters above sea level. This location gave the Philistines the advantage of fighting on open terrain where their chariots and organized infantry were superior. **Beth Shan** (בֵּית שָׁן, vv. 10, 12) was located at the junction between the Jezreel Valley and the Jordan Valley—one of the most important strategic cities in the region. **Jabesh Gilead** (יָבֵשׁ גִּלְעָד) lay east of the Jordan, about 15 km from Beth Shan—a night’s march for the brave men who retrieved the bodies.

## Events and Customs

**Desecration of enemy corpses** was widespread in the ancient Near East. Cutting off heads (v. 9), displaying bodies on city walls (v. 10), and placing weapons in temples were intended to humiliate the enemy and honor the victor’s gods. **Saul’s suicide** by falling on his own sword reflects a culture of honor where captivity and torture were considered worse than death. The term "uncircumcised" (הָעֲרֵלִים, v. 4) emphasizes the Philistines’ status as pagans outside the covenant. **Burning** of bodies (v. 12) is unusual in Israelite practice, where burial was the norm. The fact that they later buried the bones (v. 13) suggests the burning may have been partial, possibly to prevent further desecration of the mutilated bodies. A **seven-day fast** (v. 13) was a common mourning period in Israel.

## Key Terms

The expression **"beit atsabbeihem"** (בֵּית עֲצַבֵּיהֶם, v. 9)—"the house of their idols"—refers to the Philistine temple complex. The parallel account in 1 Chron 10:10 specifically mentions Dagon’s temple. **The temple of Ashtoreth** (בֵּית עַשְׁתָּרוֹת, v. 10), where Saul’s weapons were hung up, was dedicated to the Canaanite goddess of fertility and war.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Excavations at Beth Shan have uncovered two temples from the late Bronze Age/early Iron Age that may correspond with the account in v. 10. One temple has been associated with the cult of Ashtoreth. Archaeologist Amihai Mazar has identified layers corresponding to Philistine presence in the 10th century BCE, supporting the biblical narrative of Philistine control over the area.
