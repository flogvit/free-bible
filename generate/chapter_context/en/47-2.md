# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 2

## People

**Titus (v. 13)** is mentioned here as Paul’s close coworker and "brother" (τὸν ἀδελφόν μου). Titus was a Gentile believer who played a crucial role as Paul’s envoy to the church in Corinth. He likely delivered the painful letter that Paul refers to, or was expected to bring back a report on the congregation’s response. The fact that Paul left an open mission opportunity in Troas because he did not find Titus there shows how critical the situation with the Corinthian church had become, and how highly Paul valued this partnership.

**"The one who caused grief" (vv. 5–11)** refers to a specific person in the congregation who had acted against Paul or his authority. The identity of this individual is debated. Some scholars believe it is the incestuous sinner from 1 Corinthians 5, while others argue it concerns another person who directly offended Paul, possibly during his painful visit. The church has now disciplined this person, and Paul urges reconciliation.

## Places

**Troas (Τρῳάς, v. 12)** was an important port city on the northwest coast of Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey), near ancient Troy. Officially called Alexandria Troas, it was a Roman colony with significant commercial activity. It served as a hub for travel between Asia and Macedonia, and archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of harbor facilities, baths, and a large basilica. Paul had previously visited Troas and received the vision of the man from Macedonia there (Acts 16:8–10).

## Events and Customs

**The Triumphal Procession (v. 14):** Paul uses the image of the Roman triumphal procession (θριαμβεύοντι). In such a parade, the victorious general led his troops and captives through the streets of Rome while incense was burned along the route. For the conqueror’s soldiers, the fragrance was associated with honor and life; for the prisoners destined for execution, it meant death. This military image was well known throughout the Roman Empire and provided a powerful illustration of the gospel’s dual effect.

**Church Discipline (vv. 6–8):** The expression "the punishment inflicted by the majority" (ἡ ἐπιτιμία αὕτη ἡ ὑπὸ τῶν πλειόνων) reflects early Christian practice in which the congregation collectively made decisions regarding disciplinary matters. This practice had roots in Jewish synagogue custom, where exclusion from the community (herem) was a serious sanction.

## Key Terms

**Λύπη (lypē – grief/pain)** appears repeatedly throughout this chapter (vv. 1, 2, 3, 5, 7) and is a key word describing the emotional state between Paul and the church. Paul distinguishes between destructive grief that "swallows up" (καταποθῇ, v. 7) and grief that leads to repentance.

**Καπηλεύοντες (v. 17)** means "to peddle" or "to adulterate for profit." The term was used of wine merchants who diluted their wine with water. Paul uses it to distance himself from traveling teachers who exploited the gospel for personal gain—a known phenomenon in the Greco-Roman world, where philosophers and rhetoricians often accepted payment for instruction.
