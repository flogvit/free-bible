## Key Concepts

The Hebrew word **נָבָל** (*naval*) in verse 1 means more than just "fool" in the modern sense. In biblical Hebrew, it describes a person who is morally corrupt and denies God's existence or relevance—not out of intellectual doubt, but out of practical indifference that leads to immoral behavior. This kind of foolishness is tied to character and conduct, not a lack of intelligence. The same word is used about Nabal in 1 Samuel 25, who acted foolishly and wickedly.

The phrase **אֵין אֱלֹהִים** (*en Elohim* – "there is no God") does not necessarily represent theoretical atheism, which was rare in ancient times, but rather practical godlessness—living as if God neither sees nor cares about human actions. This was a widespread attitude that the prophets often criticized.

## Places

**Zion** (צִיּוֹן, *Tsiyyon*) is mentioned in verse 7 as the place from which salvation will come. In David's time, Zion was identified with the City of David, the oldest part of Jerusalem that David captured from the Jebusites. Later, the name was expanded to include the temple hill. The psalmist's longing for salvation "from Zion" reflects the belief that the Lord's presence dwelt there and that divine intervention would proceed from this holy place.

## Events and Customs

The expression "they devour my people as they eat bread" (verse 4) uses a powerful image from everyday life to describe social oppression. In ancient Israel, it was common for powerful people to exploit the poor through unjust debt practices, land grabs, and corruption in the legal system. The prophets Amos, Micah, and Isaiah use similar language to condemn such economic oppression. To "devour the people" became a standard expression for the systematic exploitation of vulnerable groups in society.
