## People

**Joab** appears in this chapter as a stubborn military leader who confronts the grieving David with political realities. His speech to the king (vv. 6–8) is remarkably direct and borders on insubordination, yet it reveals his understanding that the king’s public mourning threatens the army’s loyalty and the stability of the kingdom.

**Shimei son of Gera** was a Benjaminite from Bahurim who had previously cursed David during his flight from Jerusalem (2 Sam 16:5–13). His swift arrival with a thousand men from Benjamin to seek forgiveness (vv. 17–24) represents a calculated political move to secure his position under the restored king.

**Mephibosheth**, Saul’s grandson and Jonathan’s son, meets David with visible signs of distress—unkept beard, unwashed feet, and dirty clothes (v. 25). These were traditional signs of mourning or distress in ancient Israel and served to demonstrate his loyalty during David’s absence, contrasting with Ziba’s earlier accusations.

**Barzillai the Gileadite** was a wealthy man of eighty years from Rogelim in Transjordan. He had provided for David and his men while they stayed in Mahanaim (v. 33; cf. 2 Sam 17:27–29). His advanced age and wealth made him an influential supporter, and David’s offer to bring him to Jerusalem was a honor reserved for the king’s closest circle.

**Kimham** is mentioned in vv. 38–41 as the one Barzillai sends in his place to the king. The text does not specify their relationship.

## Places

**The Jordan River** forms the central geographical focus of the chapter. Crossing the Jordan marked David’s official return to his kingdom and carried symbolic significance as a boundary crossing. Ford crossings over the Jordan, likely near Jericho, were strategically important points that could control movement between Transjordan and the western highlands.

**Gilgal** was the place where the tribe of Judah met David to bring him across the Jordan (vv. 16, 41). This site held deep historical significance as Israel’s first camp after crossing the Jordan under Joshua (Josh 4:19–20) and as the place where Saul was anointed king (1 Sam 11:14–15). Choosing Gilgal for David’s return thus connected the event to Israel’s foundational traditions.

**Mahanaim** in Gilead had been David’s refuge during Absalom’s rebellion. The city lay east of the Jordan and had previously served as Ish-bosheth’s capital (2 Sam 2:8–9).

**Bahurim** was Shimei’s hometown, a Benjaminite village on the road between Jerusalem and the Jordan Valley, near the border between Judah and Benjamin.

## Events and Customs

**Signs of mourning and personal care** are described in connection with Mephibosheth: he had not cared for his feet (likely referring to nail trimming and washing), had not trimmed his beard, and had not washed his clothes since David left Jerusalem (v. 25). In the ancient Near East, neglect of personal hygiene and appearance was an established expression of deep grief or crisis. This practice is attested in several biblical texts and surrounding cultures.

**The royal kiss and blessing** (v. 40) were formal expressions of honor and gratitude from a king to a subject. David’s kiss to Barzillai marked a public acknowledgment of his loyalty and concluded their relationship with mutual respect.

**Eating at the king’s table** (vv. 29, 34–35) was more than just a practical arrangement—it represented inclusion in the king’s household and conveyed honor, protection, and economic security. This privilege is also mentioned earlier in connection with Mephibosheth (2 Sam 9:7).

## Key Terms

**"Adversary" (שָׂטָן, satan)** is used by David in v. 23 regarding the sons of Zeruiah: "Why would you be a satan to me?" Here the word is used in its original Hebrew meaning as "adversary" or "accuser", not as a proper name for a spiritual enemy. David rejects Abishai’s wish to execute Shimei by calling him one who opposes the king’s will on this particular day.

**"Ten shares" (עֶשֶׂר־יָדוֹת, eser yadot)** in v. 44 refers to the northern tribes’ claim to a larger share of the king. The expression "ten hands" or "ten parts" points to the ten northern tribes as opposed to Judah (and Benjamin). This exchange at the Jordan foreshadows the tribal division that would later culminate in the kingdom’s split under Rehoboam.

**"The Lord’s anointed" (מְשִׁיחַ יְהוָה, meshiach YHWH)** is used in v. 22 regarding David. The term emphasizes the king’s sacred status as God’s chosen representative. Abishai’s argument was that cursing the anointed one was a capital offense, but David chose mercy over punishment.
