# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 18

## People

The chapter addresses two main groups of religious leaders: **the Levitical priests** (הַכֹּהֲנִים הַלְוִיִּם) and **the prophet** (נָבִיא). The phrase "the priests, the Levites" in verse 1 refers to the entire tribe of Levi, which was set apart for holy service. They received no land inheritance because "the Lord is his inheritance" (v. 2). In ancient Israel's social structure, the Levites were scattered throughout the country in Levitical cities (see Numbers 35; Joshua 21), and this chapter regulates what happens when a Levite from the outskirts wishes to serve at the central sanctuary. Moses is mentioned implicitly as the model for the coming prophet ("a prophet like me," v. 15), highlighting his unique role as mediator between God and the people.

## Events and Customs

### Priests' Portions and Firstfruits
Verses 3–5 specify the priests' rightful share from the sacrificial animals: **the shoulder, the cheeks, and the stomach** (הַזְּרֹעַ וְהַלְּחָיַיִם וְהַקֵּבָה). This differs from Leviticus 7, which mentions the breast and the right thigh, possibly indicating different types of offerings or differing traditions. **The firstfruits** (רֵאשִׁית) of grain, wine, oil, and wool were also to be given to the priests, a practice that secured their livelihood and symbolized that God has the first claim on the people's harvests.

### Canaanite Religious Practices
Verses 9–12 contain the most comprehensive list in the Pentateuch of forbidden occult practices. **"Passing one's son or daughter through the fire"** (מַעֲבִיר בְּנוֹ־וּבִתּוֹ בָּאֵשׁ) likely refers to child sacrifices in the cult of Molech, archaeologically documented in Phoenician "tophet" sanctuaries. The other practices—divination, interpreting omens, sorcery, and consulting the dead—were widespread in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Canaanite religions. Both Assyrian royal texts and Egyptian magical papyri testify to the prevalence of these practices in the ancient Near East.

## Key Terms

**קֹסֵם קְסָמִים** (qosem qesamim) – "one who practices divination" – refers to using various techniques to predict the future, often with arrows, entrails, or other physical objects. **מְעוֹנֵן** (me'onen) may mean cloud-gazing or determining auspicious times. **אוֹב** ('ov) denotes a medium who conjures up spirits or the spirit itself that is summoned, while **יִדְּעֹנִי** (yidde'oni) likely refers to a "familiar spirit" or soothsayer.

**תָּמִים** (tamim) in verse 13 – "blameless" or "wholehearted" – expresses the requirement for exclusive loyalty to the Lord without mixing in foreign religious practices. The word is also used of sacrificial animals that must be "without blemish," indicating here a corresponding wholeness in one's relationship with God.

## Archaeological and Historical Background

Archaeological excavations in Carthage and other Phoenician colonies have uncovered "tophet" areas containing urns with burned children's bones, shedding light on the warnings against "passing children through the fire." At Ugarit (Ras Shamra), texts describing divination practices and consultations with deities similar to those forbidden in this chapter have been found. Furthermore, the Mari archives from Mesopotamia (c. 1800–1700 BCE) reveal an established prophetic institution at royal courts, where prophets spoke in the names of gods—both true and false prophets—providing background for the chapter's criteria for distinguishing between true and false prophets (vv. 20–22).
