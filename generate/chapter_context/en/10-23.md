# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 23

## People

This chapter contains two distinct sections with named individuals: David's last words as a prophetic oracle (vv. 1–7) and a detailed list of his elite soldiers called "gibbôrîm" (heroes/mighty men). Among "the three" most prominent are Josheb-Basshebeth the Tachmonite (also called Adino the Eznite), who struck down eight hundred men at one time; Eleazar son of Dodo, son of an Ahohite, who fought the Philistines until his hand grew stiff and clung to his sword; and Shammah son of Age the Hararite, who defended a field full of lentils while the rest of the people fled. Abishai, brother of Joab, was leader of a group of three and struck down three hundred with his spear (vv. 18–19). Benaiah son of Jehoiada distinguished himself through three feats: he killed two "ariel" (lion-like heroes) from Moab, went down into a cistern and killed a lion on a snowy day, and overcame an imposing Egyptian man by taking his spear from him (vv. 20–21). David placed him in charge of his bodyguard. Notably, the list concludes with Uriah the Hittite (v. 39)—the man David later had killed to take his wife Bathsheba. His placement among David's most loyal men underscores the tragedy of this story.

## Places

The cave of Adullam (v. 13) was an important refuge for David during his time as a fugitive from Saul, located in the Shephelah region west of Hebron. Bethlehem (vv. 14–16) was David's hometown, and the episode where three warriors broke through the Philistine camp to fetch water from the well at the city gate shows the emotional significance of this place for David. The Valley of Rephaim (v. 13) was a strategically important area south of Jerusalem where the Philistines had encamped. The list of warriors mentions hometowns from all over Israel—from Tekoa in the south to Gibeah in Benjamin—and even includes men from Ammon and other neighboring peoples, demonstrating the multinational character of the kingdom.

## Events and Customs

David's act of pouring out (וַיַּסֵּךְ) the water as a drink offering to the Lord (v. 16) reflects an ancient Semitic custom in which valuable liquids were poured out as gifts to the deity. By refusing to drink the water and instead offering it, David honored both God and the self-sacrificing loyalty of his men—he compared the water to their blood because they had risked their lives. The term "sheloshim" (the thirty) refers to a military elite unit known from other ancient cultures; similar bodyguard units existed in Egypt and Mesopotamia. The designation "who bore Joab's armor" (v. 37) refers to an honorable position that combined the roles of armor-bearer and personal adjutant.

## Key Terms

The word "ne'um" (נְאֻם) in verses 1–2 is a technical prophetic expression usually introducing divine oracles. That David uses this term for his own words places his farewell speech within the prophetic tradition. The phrase "the sweet psalmist of Israel" (וּנְעִים זְמִרוֹת יִשְׂרָאֵל) designates David as the preeminent psalmist. The term "beliyya'al" (v. 6) is a compound word that literally means "without worth" or "of no value," but in this context describes godless people like thorn bushes—useless and dangerous to touch.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Several places mentioned in the warrior lists have been confirmed through archaeological excavations. Tekoa, the hometown of Ira (v. 26), has been identified with Khirbet Tequ'a south of Bethlehem. The town of the Netophathites (vv. 28–29) is located near Bethlehem. The cultural practice of maintaining lists of elite soldiers has parallels in Egyptian and Mesopotamian sources from the same period, where royal annals regularly named prominent warriors along with their feats and hometowns, supporting the historical authenticity of this list.
