# Historical and Cultural Context for Zephaniah 1

## People

Chapter 1 opens with an unusually detailed genealogy that traces four generations back to Hezekiah (חִזְקִיָּה). Most scholars believe this refers to King Hezekiah of Judah (c. 715–686 B.C.), which would make Zephaniah a person of royal descent. This may explain his direct criticism of "the princes and the king's sons" in verse 8—he had access to the court and knew the conditions from within. The name Cushite (כּוּשִׁי), referring to Zephaniah's father, could mean either "from Cush" (Ethiopia/Nubia) or simply be a personal name, leading to discussion about the prophet's ethnic background.

King Josiah (יֹאשִׁיָּהוּ) is mentioned in verse 1 as the reigning king when Zephaniah received his message.

## Places

The chapter mentions several specific locations in Jerusalem. The **Fish Gate** (שַׁעַר הַדָּגִים) was located in the northern city wall and served as the entry point for fish from the Mediterranean Sea and the Sea of Galilee. It was also a vulnerable point during military attacks. **Mishneh** (הַמִּשְׁנֶה), often translated "the Second Quarter" or "New City," was a newer residential area west of the temple hill, expanded under Hezekiah to house refugees from the northern kingdom. Archaeological excavations have confirmed this city expansion.

**Maktesh** (הַמַּכְתֵּשׁ), meaning "the Mortar," was likely a valley formation in the city where merchants and traders gathered—hence the reference to "all who are loaded with silver" (verse 11). This area has been identified with the Tyropoeon Valley that cuts through Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes several syncretistic religious practices. **Worship on the housetops** (verse 5) was widespread in the ancient Near East and involved sacrifices and prayers to "the host of heaven"—the sun, moon, and stars. Housetops were flat and functioned as an extra room where such rituals could be performed undisturbed.

**Kemarim** (הַכְּמָרִים) in verse 4 refers specifically to idolatrous priests, distinct from the Levitical priests. The word is used exclusively in a negative sense throughout the Old Testament. **Malcam** (מַלְכָּם) in verse 5 may refer either to the god Milcom, the deity of the Ammonites (cf. 1 Kings 11:5), or be read as "their king"—possibly an idol they placed their trust in.

The custom of "jumping over the thresholds" (verse 9) may be linked to the practice of the Philistines described in 1 Samuel 5:5, where Dagon's priests avoided stepping on the threshold after the image fell. This indicates foreign religious influence in Jerusalem.

## Key Concepts

**The Day of the Lord** (יוֹם יְהוָה) is the chapter's central theological concept and appears several times (verses 7, 14, 18). While the people may have expected this day to bring victory over their enemies, Zephaniah reverses that expectation: the Day becomes judgment against Judah itself. The description in verses 15–16 with its characteristic word pairs (wrath, distress and anguish, ruin and destruction, darkness and gloom, clouds and thick darkness, trumpet and battle cry) has shaped later eschatological thought and inspired the Latin hymn "Dies Irae."

The expression **"settled on their dregs"** (הַקֹּפְאִים עַל־שִׁמְרֵיהֶם) in verse 12 is drawn from wine production. Wine that was left too long on the sediment without being fully transferred to new vessels became thick and ruined the flavor. The metaphor describes people who have become spiritually dull and indifferent.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered cultic objects from the 7th century B.C., including figurines and small incense altars found in residential areas, which may illuminate the syncretistic worship described in the chapter—particularly rooftop worship (verse 5) and foreign customs (verses 8–9).
