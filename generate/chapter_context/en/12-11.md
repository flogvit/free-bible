# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 11

## People

**Athaliah** (עֲתַלְיָה) was the daughter of King Ahab and Queen Jezebel of Israel, and she married King Joram of Judah. She is the only woman to have reigned as monarch over Judah, and her reign (c. 841–835 B.C.) represented an attempt to establish Baal worship in Jerusalem modeled after the northern kingdom. Her extermination of the royal family was both a political move to secure power and possibly motivated by religious zeal.

**Jehoiada** (יְהוֹיָדָע) was the high priest in Jerusalem and husband of Jehosheba, daughter of King Joram and sister of Ahaziah (v. 2). He appears as a political and religious reformer who organized the coup against Athaliah. His central role demonstrates the significant influence of the priesthood in Judah’s political life. His name means "the LORD knows," and his actions are described as a restoration of the Davidic covenant.

**Jehosheba** (יְהוֹשֶׁבַע, *Jehosheva*) was the daughter of King Joram and sister of Ahaziah (v. 2). She saved young Joash from Athaliah’s massacre by hiding him and his nurse in the "bedchamber" (חֲדַר הַמִּטּוֹת) in the temple.

**Joash** (יוֹאָשׁ, also called Jehoash) was the son of Ahaziah and was only one year old when he was hidden. His six years in hiding within the temple and his later coronation represented continuity in David’s dynasty, which according to God’s promise should not cease.

**Mattan** (מַתָּן) was the priest of Baal in Jerusalem and was killed before the altars when the temple of Baal was destroyed (v. 18).

## Places

**The house of the LORD** (בֵּית יְהוָה, the temple in Jerusalem) served both as a hiding place for young Joash and as the site of his coronation ceremony. The chapter mentions several specific locations within the temple complex: the "Sur gate" (שַׁעַר סוּר, v. 6), the "gate behind the runners" (v. 6), and the "pillar" (הָעַמּוּד, v. 14) where the king stood. The temple functioned both as a religious center and as a kind of political fortress where weapons from David’s time were stored (v. 10).

**The house of Baal** (בֵּית־הַבַּעַל, v. 18) is mentioned as a separate temple in Jerusalem, showing how institutionalized idolatry had become under Athaliah. This temple was destroyed by the people after the coup, and the priest Mattan was killed before its altar.

## Events and Customs

**The coronation ritual** is described in detail (v. 12): the king was brought out, given the "diadem" (הַנֵּזֶר, *ha-nezer*) and the "testimony" (הָעֵדוּת, *ha-edut*)—likely a scroll containing the law or covenant stipulations—and anointed. The people clapped their hands and shouted, "Long live the king!" (יְחִי הַמֶּלֶךְ). This ritual has parallels in other ancient Near Eastern cultures, but the inclusion of the "testimony" was unique to Israel and emphasized the king’s obligation to God’s law.

**The covenant renewal** (v. 17) describes a three-sided covenant between the LORD, the king, and the people. This was not merely a political alliance but a religious renewal of the relationship between God and his people, with the king acting as mediator. Such covenant renewals were important in Israelite theology.

## Key Terms

**The Carites** (הַכָּרִי, *ha-kari*) and **the runners/guards** (הָרָצִים, *ha-ratsim*) were special military units. The Carites were likely mercenaries, possibly of Cretan origin (related to "Cretans"), while the runners were royal bodyguards who ran ahead of the king’s chariot. These elite forces were crucial to the success of the coup.

**The people of the land** (עַם הָאָרֶץ, *am ha'arets*) appear as a significant group supporting the coup and the Davidic king (vv. 14, 18–20). In this period, the term probably referred to free landowning men in Judah who held political influence, perhaps a kind of landed nobility loyal to the house of David.
