## Places

Psalm 87 mentions several geographical and political entities that represented the known world in ancient Israel’s time. **Zion** (צִיּוֹן) refers specifically to the temple hill in Jerusalem, which was regarded as God’s dwelling place on earth. The expression "the holy mountains" (הַרְרֵי־קֹדֶשׁ) may point to the mountain range where Jerusalem is located, or more specifically to Zion and Mount Moriah, where the temple stood.

**Rahab** (רַהַב) is a poetic name for Egypt, literally meaning "the proud one" or "the stormer," and is used several times in the Old Testament as a symbol of Egypt’s pride and power (cf. Isa 30:7). **Babel** represents Mesopotamia and the eastern superpower. **Philistia** was the coastal region southwest of Judah, **Tyre** was the foremost Phoenician port city to the north, and **Cush** (Nubia/Ethiopia) represents the areas south of Egypt. Together, these nations form a representative overview of the known world around Israel—Egypt in the southwest, Babel in the east, Philistia in the west, Tyre in the north, and Cush in the far south.

## Key Concepts

The expression "born there" (יֻלַּד־שָׁם) is repeated three times in the psalm (vv. 4, 5, 6) and is central to the theological message of the text. In ancient times, place of birth was decisive for identity, rights, and belonging. The psalm presents the radical idea that people from Gentile nations can be counted as "born in Zion"—that is, incorporated into God’s people. This concept of spiritual citizenship was unusual in the ancient Near East.

The verb "to count" or "register" (יִסְפֹּר בִּכְתוֹב, v. 6) alludes to official population registers kept in ancient cities and kingdoms. Here, the Lord Himself is pictured as the one who keeps a register of the nations and their belonging to Zion, giving the image legal weight.

## Events and Customs

The conclusion of the psalm (v. 7) mentions "singers" and "dancers" (or "those who play the flute"), together with "my springs" (מַעְיָנַי). This reflects the liturgical practice of temple festivals, where professional singers and musicians performed songs of praise, often accompanied by processional dancing. The possessive phrase "my springs" suggests that the speaker identifies his sources of life with Zion. Water springs and wells had symbolic significance in Near Eastern religions as life-giving elements. Jerusalem’s Gihon spring may be the background for this image, since it was the city’s main water source and played a role in royal ceremonies (cf. 1 Kgs 1:33–40).
