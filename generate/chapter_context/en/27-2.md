## People

**Nebuchadnezzar** stands central in this chapter as the powerful Babylonian king troubled by a dream he cannot remember. The dating "in his second year" (v. 1) has sparked discussion regarding chronology with Daniel's three-year training period (chap. 1:5). The solution likely lies in different methods of calculating kings' reigns, where the Babylonians did not count the accession year as the first regnal year.

**Arioch** (אַרְיוֹךְ), "commander of the royal guard" (רַב־טַבָּחַיָּא), was a high-ranking officer responsible for executions. The title literally means "chief of the butchers" and refers to the military organization of the court where the royal guard also served as executioners.

## Events and Customs

**Dream interpretation in Babylon** was a highly developed discipline. The wise men were divided into several categories: *chartummim* (magicians who used magical techniques), *ashshafim* (enchanters), *mekashshefim* (sorcerers) and *kasdim* (Chaldeans – here referring to a priestly class with astrological expertise). That the king demanded to know both the dream and its interpretation was extremely unusual. Normally, the dreamer would recount their dream, and the wise men would consult extensive dream dictionaries for interpretation. Babylonian dream dictionaries contained thousands of interpretations in the formula "if a man dreams X, it means Y."

**The punishment** threatened – being "cut into pieces" and having houses turned into "dumps" (v. 5) – was well known in Oriental methods of punishment. Assyrian and Babylonian inscriptions mention similar brutal punishments for treason and disobedience.

## Key Terms

**Aramaic language shift**: From verse 4 ("arāmît") the text shifts from Hebrew to Aramaic. The change occurs naturally where the Chaldeans address the king, and Aramaic was likely the language actually used at court.

**Rāz** (רָז) – "secret" or "mystery" – is a Persian loanword that appears frequently in this chapter. The word emphasizes that the content of the dream is hidden from human wisdom but accessible to "the God of heaven" (אֱלָהּ שְׁמַיָּא), an expression Daniel uses repeatedly to contrast Israel's God with Babylonian gods.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The dream's image of a statue with a head of gold, chest of silver, belly of bronze, legs of iron and feet of iron mixed with clay reflects a widespread ancient concept of world history as a downward series of metallic ages. The Greek poet Hesiod (c. 700 BC) described similar ages of gold, silver, bronze and iron. This shows that Daniel communicated using images recognizable in the contemporary worldview, while at the same time the content was uniquely revealed by God.
