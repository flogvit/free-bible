# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 18

## People

**Jethro (יִתְרוֹ)** is a central figure in this chapter. He is referred to as "priest of Midian" (כֹהֵן מִדְיָן) and Moses' father-in-law. The name Jethro likely means "his excellence" or "abundance". In other texts, he is also called Reuel (Exod 2:18). As a Midianite priest, he was probably a religious leader for a tribal people living in northwestern Arabia and the Sinai region. The Midianites were descendants of Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:1-2) and thus shared a common ancestral tradition with the Israelites. His role as Moses' advisor in this chapter shows that wisdom and reverence for God could be found outside Israel.

**Zipporah** (צִפֹּרָה), Moses' wife, and their two sons **Gershom** and **Eliezer** are mentioned. The names are theologically significant: Gershom (גֵּרְשֹׁם) is explained by "foreigner" (גֵּר) – Moses was a foreigner in a foreign land. Eliezer (אֱלִיעֶזֶר) means "my God is help", pointing to God's protection against Pharaoh's sword.

## Places

**The mountain of God** (הַר הָאֱלֹהִים) in verse 5 refers to Horeb/Sinai, where Moses first met God in the burning bush. This mountain, traditionally located on the Sinai Peninsula, was a holy place in both Midianite and Israelite tradition. The fact that the meeting takes place here underscores the site's significance as a point of encounter between the divine and the human. Its exact location remains debated among scholars, with several candidates proposed in Sinai and northwestern Arabia.

## Events and Customs

**The greeting ceremony** in verse 7 describes a formal welcome: Moses "bowed down" (וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ) and "kissed him" (וַיִּשַּׁק־לוֹ). These were standard courtesy rituals in the ancient Near East to honor a respected guest or relative. Asking about each other's "shalom" (לְשָׁלוֹם) was more than a polite phrase – it involved a thorough exchange concerning well-being and life circumstances.

**The sacrificial act** in verse 12, where Jethro offers burnt offerings (עֹלָה) and peace offerings (זְבָחִים) to God, is remarkable. That a non-Israelite priest sacrifices to the God of Israel, and that Aaron and the elders of Israel participate in a shared meal "before God" (לִפְנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים), demonstrates a notable religious inclusivity. The communal meal following sacrifice was an important covenantal custom throughout the ancient Near East.

**The judicial system** that Jethro proposes (verses 21-22), with leaders over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, reflects a decimal hierarchical structure common in military and administrative organization in the ancient Near East. Similar structures are known from Egyptian and Mesopotamian administrative systems.

## Key Terms

**חֹתֵן (khoten)** – translated as "father-in-law" – is used repeatedly throughout this chapter (in verses 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12, 14, 15, 17, 24, 27). The word emphasizes the importance of family bonds and the respect a son-in-law owed to his wife's father in Semitic culture.

**וַיִּחַדְּ (vayyikhad)** in verse 9, often translated as "rejoiced", is a rare Hebrew verb. Some scholars suggest it may mean "shivered" or "was seized", implying a deep religious reaction, perhaps awe mixed with joy.

**אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל (anshei-khayil)** – "capable men" – describes the qualified leaders. The term implies both moral integrity and practical competence. The four criteria Jethro mentions – capability, reverence for God, honesty, and freedom from greed – constitute an early ethical standard for public servants.
