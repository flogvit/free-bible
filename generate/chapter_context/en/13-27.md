# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 27

## People

The chapter names twelve military commanders who led monthly divisions of 24,000 men each. Several are recognizable from David’s "mighty men" (2 Samuel 23; 1 Chronicles 11), including Benaiah son of Jehoiada, described as "chief among the thirty" (v. 6), and Sibbechai the Hushathite (v. 11), known from other texts as the one who killed the Philistine giant Saph (2 Samuel 21:18). Jashobeam, leader of the first month’s division, is identified as a descendant of Perez (v. 3) and thus from the tribe of Judah—highlighting Judah’s leading role in David’s kingdom.

Among the tribal leaders (vv. 16–22), Zadok is mentioned as head of the line of Aaron, and Elihu, "one of David’s brothers," as prince over Judah. Ahithophel is called "the king’s counselor" (v. 33)—the same man who had previously betrayed David during Absalom’s rebellion (2 Samuel 15–17). Hushai the Archite is titled "the king’s friend" (רֵעַ הַמֶּלֶךְ), an official title known from Egyptian and other Eastern court traditions, denoting a particularly trusted advisor.

## Events and Customs

The rotating military system with twelve divisions serving one month each represented a sophisticated organizational structure. With 24,000 men per division, this provided a total mobilized force of 288,000 soldiers (12 × 24,000). This type of militia arrangement allowed farmers to serve without neglecting agricultural work throughout the year, striking a balance between a professional army and a purely peasant-based militia.

David’s decision not to include those under twenty years old in the census (v. 23) is linked to God’s promise that Israel would become as numerous as the stars of heaven. The text notes that Joab began a census but did not complete it, and that "wrath came upon Israel" (v. 24)—a reference to the census in 2 Samuel 24, which was regarded as sinful. Chronicles explains here that because the number remained incomplete, it was therefore not included in "the book of the chronicles of King David."

## Key Terms

The term "nagid" (נָגִיד) is used for the tribal leaders and means "leader" or "prince"—a word also applied to kings, but here denoting administrative authority over the tribes rather than royal rank. The expression "the king’s friend" (רֵעַ הַמֶּלֶךְ) regarding Hushai was a formal court title, not merely a description of personal friendship.

"Shfela" (הַשְּׁפֵלָה) in verse 28 refers to the lowland region between the coastal plain and the hills of Judah, an area known for olive trees and sycamore-fig trees. These fruit trees held great economic importance—olive trees for oil production and sycamore-figs both for their fruit and timber.

## Places

The Sharon plain (v. 29) was the fertile coastal region north of Jaffa, ideal for cattle grazing. The area’s rich pastures made it a natural location for royal livestock. The contrast between cattle "in Sharon" and "in the valleys" (v. 29) illustrates geographically differentiated use of grazing land.
