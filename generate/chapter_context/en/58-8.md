# Historical and Cultural Context for Hebrews 8

## Places

The chapter contrasts two sanctuaries: the earthly tabernacle (σκηνή) and "the true tent sanctuary" (τῆς σκηνῆς τῆς ἀληθινῆς) in heaven, which the Lord himself has set up (v. 2). The reference to Moses on the mountain (v. 5) points back to Sinai, where Moses, according to Exodus 25:40, was shown the heavenly pattern (τύπος) for the tabernacle. The idea that earthly sanctuaries were copies of heavenly originals was widespread in antiquity, both in Jewish tradition (as in Wisdom of Solomon 9:8) and in Platonic philosophy. For first-generation Christians with a Jewish background, this way of thinking represented a familiar framework for interpreting Jesus’ priestly service.

## Events and Customs

The offering service of the Levitical priesthood forms the background for the chapter’s argument. The high priest (ἀρχιερεύς) had as his main task to present gifts and sacrifices (v. 3). This service took place daily in the temple and culminated annually on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur). The chapter assumes that readers knew priests had to belong to the tribe of Levi to serve lawfully "on earth" (v. 4) – a regulation that would have excluded Jesus, who was from the tribe of Judah.

## Key Terms

The word **διαθήκη** (diathēkē) appears six times in the chapter and is central to the argument. In Greek, the word means both "covenant" and "testament". When Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34 (vv. 8–12), it uses the Septuagint’s rendering of the Hebrew **בְּרִית** (berit). In an ancient Near Eastern context, a covenant was a binding relationship between two parties, often with divine witnesses and obligations. The term **μεσίτης** (mesitēs, "mediator") in verse 6 was a legal term describing someone who guaranteed and facilitated an agreement between parties.

The expression **ὑπόδειγμα καὶ σκιά** ("example and shadow") in verse 5 reflects Platonic thought that was well known in Hellenistic Judaism, especially through Philo of Alexandria. The shadow suggests something temporary and incomplete that points toward the true substance.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The long quotation from Jeremiah 31:31–34 (vv. 8–12) is one of the longest continuous Old Testament quotations in the New Testament. The text form follows the Septuagint closely, with some minor variations. The Qumran community at the Dead Sea understood itself as the people of the "new covenant" (cf. Damascus Document), showing that the expectation of a new covenant was alive in Judaism during Jesus’ time. The difference is that Hebrews links the fulfillment directly to Jesus’ person and work, not to a new interpretation of the law or a new social order.
