## People

In this chapter, Ezekiel encounters a mysterious figure described as "a man whose appearance was like bronze" (v. 3), holding a linen cord and a measuring rod. This figure serves as a guide throughout the temple vision, instructing the prophet to look carefully, listen closely, and take to heart everything he sees (v. 4). In verse 46, "the sons of Zadok" (בְנֵי־צָדוֹק) are specifically mentioned as the Levitical priests who have the right to approach the Lord to serve him at the altar—a distinction that becomes important in the following chapters.

## Places

In the vision, Ezekiel is brought to "the land of Israel" and set down on a "very high mountain," with something resembling a city structure to the south (v. 2). This "very high mountain" has traditionally been understood as Zion, the temple mount, although the actual topography of Jerusalem is more modest. The elevated description reflects theological significance rather than physical geography—in prophetic tradition, the Lord’s mountain is often portrayed as cosmically exalted (cf. Isa 2:2; Mic 4:1).

## Events and Customs

The chapter opens with a precise dating to the twenty-fifth year of the exile, on the tenth day of the first month of the year, fourteen years after the fall of Jerusalem (586 BC), placing the vision around 573 BC. The expression בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה ("at the beginning of the year," v. 1) likely refers to the religious new year (Nisan) according to the Babylonian spring-start calendar that also influenced Israelite practice. The tenth day of this month was designated in the Israelite sacrificial system for selecting the Passover lamb (Exod 12:3). The chapter also describes slaughter tables and rooms for washing burnt offerings (vv. 38–43), reflecting standard procedures in temple service where sacrificial animals had to be prepared, washed, and slaughtered in specific places before their blood and flesh were handled further.

## Key Terms

The unit of measurement used throughout is "a cubit and a handbreadth" (אַמָּה וָטֹפַח, v. 5), a "long cubit" that was approximately 52–53 cm (a common cubit of about 44.5 cm plus a handbreadth of about 7.5 cm). This was the royal or sacred standard of measurement, also known from Mesopotamian building instructions. The measuring rod (קְנֵה הַמִּדָּה) of six long cubits thus corresponded to approximately 3.1 meters. The term תָּא (guard rooms/niches, vv. 7–12) refers to small chambers along the gate passages where guards could be stationed—an architectural solution known from monumental gates throughout the ancient Near East.

## Archaeological Parallels

The detailed gate structure described—with three guard rooms on each side of a central passage—has striking parallels with archaeological finds from Israelite city gates, especially the six-chambered gates from Solomon’s time discovered at Megiddo, Hazor, and Gezer. These gates, dated to the 900s BC, show the same basic architectural principle described in Ezekiel’s vision, with three chambers on each side of a through-passage. This suggests that the prophet’s idealized temple drew upon known architectural conventions from Israelite monumental architecture, while dimensions and symmetry were perfected in accordance with the divine vision.
