# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 John 1

## Key Concepts

**The Word of Life (ὁ λόγος τῆς ζωῆς)** – The expression "the word of life" in verse 1 carries deep resonance in both Jewish and Greek thought. In the Jewish tradition, God’s word was the creative power (Gen 1; Ps 33:6), while in Greek philosophy, especially among the Stoics, *logos* was the ordering principle of reason in the universe. John uses the term in a distinctive way that surpasses both traditions by identifying the *logos* with a person who could be physically touched (ἐψηλάφησαν – "our hands have handled").

**Koinonia (κοινωνία)** – "Fellowship" in verses 3, 6, and 7 was an important concept in the Greco-Roman world. It described partnership in business as well as deep personal bonds. In a religious context, mystery religions used the term to refer to participation in the divine. John fills it with Christian content: fellowship with the Father and the Son creates horizontal fellowship among believers.

**Light and Darkness (φῶς / σκοτία)** – This dualism in verses 5–7 was widespread in antiquity. The Dead Sea Scrolls from Qumran (especially the Community Rule/1QS) show that contemporary Jewish thought operated with a sharp contrast between "sons of light" and "sons of darkness". Greek philosophy, especially Platonism, used light as a metaphor for truth and knowledge. John’s formulation "God is light" (verse 5) is unique and absolute.

## Events and Customs

Verses 8–10 address practices related to **confession of sin** (ὁμολογῶμεν τὰς ἁμαρτίας). In Jewish tradition, confession of sin was central on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) and in temple sacrifices (Lev 5:5; 16:21). In early Christian practice, as described in the Didache (a writing from the late first or early second century), confession was part of the church gathering: "In the assembly you shall confess your transgressions" (Did. 4:14). John’s teaching that Jesus’ blood "cleanses us from all sin" (verse 7) links Christian confession of sin to Christ’s finished work, not to repeated sacrifices.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The language of **physical testimony** in verse 1 ("what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked at and our hands have touched") can be understood in light of the early heresy called **docetism** (from Greek δοκέω, "to seem/appear"). This teaching claimed that Christ only appeared to have a physical body. Ignatius of Antioch (c. 35–108 AD) fought against the same error and wrote that Jesus "was truly born, truly ate and drank" (Trall. 9). John’s insistence on sensory testimony – hearing, seeing, looking at, touching – is a direct refutation of the teaching that salvation comes through spiritual knowledge (*gnosis*) independent of Jesus’ physical existence and suffering.
