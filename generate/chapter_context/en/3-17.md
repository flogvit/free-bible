# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 17

## Events and Customs

Chapter 17 addresses the centralization of all slaughter to the tent of meeting, which was a radical break from common practice in ancient times. In surrounding cultures, it was normal to slaughter animals anywhere, often with religious overtones. This legislation aimed to eliminate unauthorized sacrifices, prevent idolatry, and ensure that all blood was handled correctly at the altar. The verse concerning "the hairy ones/goats" (שְׂעִירִם, *se'irim*) in verse 7 refers to a form of idolatry that the Israelites had practiced, possibly influenced by Egyptian religion or beliefs about desert demons in goat form. The expression "whoring after them" (זֹנִים אַחֲרֵיהֶם) uses a marriage metaphor to describe spiritual unfaithfulness toward YHWH.

The prohibition against eating blood (verses 10–14) was absolute and applied to both Israelites and foreigners. This stood in sharp contrast to surrounding cultures where blood rituals were common. In Canaanite and Mesopotamian religion, blood was often drunk as part of religious ceremonies to gain contact with deities or the spirits of the dead. The command to cover the blood with dust/earth (עָפָר) after hunting wild animals and birds (verse 13) shows reverence for the life force that belongs to God alone.

Verses 15–16 deal with those who eat animals that died naturally (נְבֵלָה, *nevelah*) or were torn apart by predators (טְרֵפָה, *terefah*). Such animals had not been properly slaughtered and their blood had not been drained. Anyone who ate such food became unclean until evening and had to wash their clothes and bathe in water to become clean again.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word נֶפֶשׁ (*nefesh*) in verses 11 and 14 is often translated as "life" or "soul", but here it means the life principle or life force. The expression "כִּי נֶפֶשׁ הַבָּשָׂר בַּדָּם הִוא" ("for the life of the flesh is in the blood") expresses a fundamental biblical anthropology in which blood is seen as the carrier of life itself. The word כַּפֵּר (*kipper*, "atone") in verse 11 is central to the entire theology of sacrifice and means to cover, cleanse, or make atonement. This verse provides the theological basis for the whole sacrificial system: blood is given by God for atoning purposes and therefore must not be consumed.

The terms נְבֵלָה (*nevelah*) and טְרֵפָה (*terefah*) in verse 15 describe respectively animals that died of natural causes and animals killed by predators. Both categories lacked proper blood drainage and made the one who ate them ritually unclean.

The term גֵּר (*ger*, "foreigner/sojourner") mentioned in verses 8, 10, 12, 13, and 15 describes non-Israelites who lived permanently among the people. They were bound by the same laws concerning blood, showing that these rules had universal validity within the community.
