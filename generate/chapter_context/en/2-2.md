# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 2

## People

**Moses** (מֹשֶׁה, Moshe) is introduced in this chapter. His name is explained in verse 10 with a wordplay on the Hebrew verb מָשָׁה (mashah), "to draw out"—because he was drawn out of the water. The name also has an Egyptian parallel: *mose* or *mes* means "son" in Egyptian and appears in pharaonic names such as Thutmose ("Son of Thoth") and Ramses ("Son of Ra"). This double etymological background reflects Moses' identity between two cultures.

**Reuel** (רְעוּאֵל) was a priest in Midian (כֹהֵן מִדְיָן), indicating that he was a religious leader for the Midianites. He is also called Jethro (יִתְרוֹ) in other texts (Exod 3:1; 18:1). **Zipporah** (צִפֹּרָה), which means "bird," became Moses' wife.

## Places

**The Nile** (הַיְאֹר, hayeor) plays a central role in the narrative. This Egyptian word for the Nile (from Egyptian *itrw*) emphasizes the story's Egyptian setting. Papyrus-filled areas along the Nile were common, and baskets sealed with bitumen and tar were a well-known Egyptian technique for making objects waterproof.

**Midian** was located east of the Gulf of Aqaba, in northwestern Arabia. The area was known for trade and nomadic life. The well where Moses met Reuel's daughters was a typical meeting place in nomadic culture—a spot where strangers could be introduced to local families.

## Events and Customs

**The Papyrus Basket** (תֵּבַת גֹּמֶא, tevat gome) uses the same Hebrew word for "ark/basket" (תֵּבָה) as Noah's ark. This creates a theological parallel: both are vessels that save through water. Papyrus was a common material in Egypt for boats and baskets, and sealing with bitumen (חֵמָר) and tar (זֶפֶת) was standard practice.

**The Well Scene** follows a known literary pattern in the Bible called the "type-scene": a foreign man meets his future bride at a well. A similar pattern occurs when Abraham's servant meets Rebekah (Gen 24) and when Jacob meets Rachel (Gen 29). The scene signals to the reader that a marriage will follow.

## Key Terms

**"Hebrew"** (עִבְרִי, ivri) is used in the chapter (verses 6, 7, 11, 13). In verse 6, the expression "children of the Hebrews" (מִיַּלְדֵי הָעִבְרִים) is used; in verse 7, the feminine plural "Hebrew women" (הָעִבְרִיֹּת). This was a designation often used by foreigners to refer to Israelites, or when Israelites identified themselves before outsiders.

**Gershom** (גֵּרְשֹׁם) is explained as "a stranger there"—a wordplay on גֵּר (ger, "stranger"). Moses says: "I have been a stranger in a foreign land" (גֵּר הָיִיתִי בְּאֶרֶץ נָכְרִיָּה). The name expresses Moses' life of exile and alienation, a theme that permeates the chapter: Moses is a stranger both in Egypt (as a Hebrew) and in Midian (as an Egyptian, see verse 19).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian texts and reliefs from the New Kingdom (ca. 1550–1070 BCE) show scenes of royal women bathing in the Nile with handmaidens, confirming the cultural realism of the story about Pharaoh's daughter. Egypt's royalty often had private bathing areas along the river.

Archaeological finds from the Midian region, especially at Qurayyah in northwestern Arabia, have revealed a distinct pottery style from the late Bronze Age. This confirms that the area was inhabited and possessed its own cultural identity during the period when the story takes place.
