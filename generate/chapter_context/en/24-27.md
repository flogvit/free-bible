# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 27

## People

**Zedekiah, king of Judah** is mentioned as the recipient of the message, although verse 1 presents a textual difficulty where "Jehoiakim" is likely a scribal error for "Zedekiah" (as the rest of the chapter clearly concerns him). Zedekiah reigned from 597–586 B.C. as the last king of Judah, installed as a vassal king by Nebuchadnezzar following the deportation of his nephew Jeconiah (Jehoiachin). His original name was "Mattaniah" (gift from YHWH), but this was changed by Nebuchadnezzar to "Zedekiah" (YHWH is my righteousness) – a name that ironically served as a reminder of his oath of loyalty to Babylon.

**Jeconiah (Jehoiachin)** is mentioned in verse 20 as already deported to Babylon. This dates the chapter to after 597 B.C. Babylonian administrative texts (the Jehoiachin tablets) confirm his presence in Babylon and mention rations allocated to him and his sons.

## Places

**Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon** represent neighboring kingdoms that had sent delegations to Jerusalem. The context suggests a secret meeting where these small states planned a coordinated rebellion against Babylon, likely around 594/593 B.C. Archaeological and historical sources confirm that all these kingdoms were subjected to Babylonian supremacy during this period. Tyre and Sidon were Phoenician city-states with significant maritime power, while Edom, Moab, and Ammon were Transjordanian kingdoms with long-standing connections to Judah.

## Events and Customs

**The symbolic action of the yoke** (מוֹסֵרוֹת וּמֹטוֹת – *moserót umotót*, "straps and yoke bars") was a powerful visual message. The yoke was common in agricultural societies and symbolized submission and service. Prophets in the ancient Near East often used dramatic symbolic actions to convey divine messages. By wearing the yoke on his neck, Jeremiah physically communicated what he preached verbally: the nations must submit to Babylonian rule.

**Diplomatic envoys** (מַלְאָכִים – *mal'akim*) from neighboring kingdoms in Jerusalem testify to intense diplomatic activity. It was common practice in antiquity for smaller states to form coalitions against dominant powers. Such meetings often included religious consultations, and verse 9 suggests that each nation had its own prophets and diviners who advised on political decisions.

## Key Terms

**"My servant" (עַבְדִּי – *'avdí*)** used of Nebuchadnezzar in verse 6 is theologically striking. This title is otherwise used for the patriarchs, Moses, and David. By calling the pagan king God's servant, Jeremiah emphasizes that YHWH is the universal ruler who uses even foreign kings for His purposes – a radical claim in the context of that time.

**Temple articles** (כְּלֵי בֵית־יְהוָה) mentioned in verses 16–22 included valuable ritual objects. Some had already been taken to Babylon in 597 B.C. False prophets claimed these would soon return. Jeremiah prophesied the opposite: even the remaining articles, including the bronze pillars, the "Sea" (the large bronze basin), and the stands, would be carried away – which was fulfilled in 586 B.C.
