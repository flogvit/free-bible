# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 25

## Places

The chapter opens with the Lord speaking to Moses "on Mount Sinai" (v. 1). This is the only time in Leviticus that Mount Sinai is explicitly mentioned as the place of revelation, underscoring the special significance of this chapter. However, the laws concern life in the land of Canaan – "when you enter the land I am giving you" (v. 2) – and presuppose an established agricultural society with property rights, cities both walled and unwalled, and a functioning economic system.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains three interwoven institutions: the sabbatical year (every seventh year), the Year of Jubilee (the fiftieth year, after seven cycles of sabbatical years totaling 49 years), and the right of redemption. The sabbatical year meant that the land was to lie fallow, a practice with both religious and agronomic functions. In the ancient Near East, there are parallels to periodic fallowing, but Israel's system was unique in its theological basis – the land belongs to the Lord (v. 23). The Year of Jubilee, which began with the blowing of the shofar on the tenth day of the seventh month – the Day of Atonement (v. 9) – involved the release of slaves and the return of land to original families. Similar "edicts of justice" (mīšarum) are known from Mesopotamia, where kings could cancel debts upon ascending the throne, but these were sporadic royal initiatives rather than cyclical institutions as in Israel.

## Key Terms

**Yovel (יוֹבֵל)** – The word is often translated "Year of Jubilee" and likely originally referred to the sound of a ram's horn. This fiftieth year meant a complete economic reset (vv. 10-13). **Deror (דְּרוֹר)** – "freedom" or "release" (v. 10) – is a technical term for the manumission of slaves and cancellation of debts, also known from Akkadian texts (andurārum). **Go'el (גֹּאֵל)** – "the redeemer" – designates the nearest relative who had the duty to buy back land or redeem a poor relative from slavery (vv. 25, 48-49). **Aḥuzzah (אֲחֻזָּה)** – "heritage" or "possession" – the central term for family property that could not be sold permanently, since all land belongs to God (vv. 23-24). **Neshekh (נֶשֶׁךְ)** and **tarbit (תַּרְבִּית)** (vv. 36-37) – respectively "interest" and "increase/addition" – the prohibition against charging interest to poor Israelites was radical in a world where interest rates of 20-33% were common.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Mesopotamian cuneiform texts document royal "mīšarum" edicts in which debts were canceled and slaves released, showing that the concept of economic reset was known in the region. Inscriptions from the second millennium B.C. from Babylon describe such measures. The difference is that these Mesopotamian decrees were irregular royal initiatives, often occurring at a new king's accession, whereas the Israelite Year of Jubilee was institutionalized as a fixed cycle with God as the true "king" who ordered the release. Ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from ancient Israel and archaeological finds of property boundary markers can shed light on the property conditions regulated by these laws, although direct archaeological evidence for Jubilee practices is lacking.
