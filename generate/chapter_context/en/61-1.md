# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Peter 1

## People

The chapter opens with the Hebrew form of the name "Symeon" (Συμεὼν), which is more archaic than the common Greek form "Simon". This form appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Acts 15:14, where James uses it in a Jewish-Christian context. The use of this name may indicate an intention to emphasize Peter’s Jewish identity and apostolic authority. Peter refers to his impending death as "the putting off of my tent" (v. 14), noting that the Lord Jesus himself had revealed this to him—a possible reference to the prophecy in John 21:18-19.

## Places

The chapter mentions "the holy mountain" (ἐν τῷ ἁγίῳ ὄρει, v. 18), referring to the Mount of Transfiguration where Peter was an eyewitness to Jesus’ transformation. The Gospels do not identify the mountain by name, but tradition has pointed either to Mount Tabor in Galilee or Mount Hermon further north. The designation "holy" reflects Jewish tradition, in which places of divine revelation were regarded as sacred—just as Sinai was called "the mountain of God" after the theophany there. Peter’s use of the term "holy" underscores the event’s significance as a divine revelation.

## Events and Customs

The Transfiguration on the mountain (vv. 16-18) was a theophany in which three disciples—Peter, James, and John—witnessed Jesus being transformed and receiving divine confirmation. In Jewish tradition, mountaintops held special significance as places of divine revelation (Moses at Sinai, Elijah at Carmel and Horeb). The voice from heaven ("This is my beloved Son") echoes the language of Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1, texts understood messianically. Peter’s self-designation as "eyewitness" (ἐπόπται) was a technical term used in mystery religions to describe initiates who had seen the sacred rituals.

## Key Terms

The chapter contains several key terms requiring cultural explanation. The word **ἐπίγνωσις** (epignosis, "full knowledge") appears three times in the chapter (vv. 2, 3, 8) and denotes a deeper, more personal knowledge than ordinary knowledge—especially in relation to God. The expression **θείας κοινωνοὶ φύσεως** ("partakers of divine nature," v. 4) uses philosophical language familiar from Hellenistic culture, but Peter fills it with biblical content concerning renewal and salvation. The list of virtues in verses 5-7 (faith, virtue, knowledge, self-control, perseverance, godliness, brotherly love, love) is structurally similar to Hellenistic lists of virtues, yet its content is clearly shaped by Christian ethics, with **ἀγάπη** (agape, self-sacrificing love) as the climax.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The expression **σεσοφισμένοις μύθοις** ("cleverly devised myths/fables," v. 16) was a polemical term used by Hellenistic authors to dismiss religious narratives they considered fabricated. Peter uses this language to contrast the apostolic proclamation with false teachers. The term **παρουσία** (parousia, "coming/arrival") in the same verse was in Hellenistic times a technical expression for an official visit by a king or emperor to a city. Christians adopted this term for Christ’s return, giving it both political and eschatological overtones in a culture where emperor worship was widespread.
