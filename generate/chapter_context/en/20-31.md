# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 31

## People

The chapter opens with the words of **King Lemuel** (לְמוּאֵל), a ruler otherwise not mentioned in the Bible. The name means "devoted to God" or "belonging to God." Tradition has suggested various identifications—some rabbinic sources link him to Solomon, while others view him as a non-Israelite king. The addition of "massa" (מַשָּׂא) can mean either "oracle/word of prophecy" or refer to a geographical region in northern Arabia, which may suggest a connection to Arabic wisdom traditions. Most striking is that the wisdom is conveyed by **his mother** (אִמּוֹ)—a rare voice in biblical literature where a queen mother appears as teacher and advisor to a king.

## Events and Customs

The mother's warning against wine and strong drink (vv. 4–7) reflects a widespread concern in the ancient Near East regarding rulers' self-control. Parallels exist in Egyptian wisdom texts such as "The Instruction of Amenemope" and Mesopotamian royal advice, where intoxication is seen as incompatible with just judicial authority. The custom of giving strong drink to the dying or grieving (vv. 6–7) is known from several cultures and served a comforting function. The mention of "city gates" (שְׁעָרִים, vv. 23, 31) refers to the central place for legal proceedings, commerce, and public recognition in Israelite cities—here her husband sits among "the elders of the land."

## Key Terms

The expression **"eshet chayil"** (אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, v. 10) is often translated as "capable wife" or "worthy woman," but the Hebrew *chayil* has a broader meaning encompassing strength, virtue, ability, and even military might. The same word is used for brave warriors. This gives the portrait of the woman significant weight—she is not merely skilled, but powerful and capable. The word **"kena'ani"** (כְּנַעֲנִי, v. 24) is translated here as "merchant" rather than "Canaanite," reflecting that Canaanites were known as traders—the term became synonymous with commercial activity. **"Shani"** (שָׁנִים, v. 21), scarlet fabric, was a costly material dyed with the kermes insect (kermes vermilio) and indicates the household's wealth.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The acrostic structure in vv. 10–31—where each verse begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet—is a known literary form in ancient Israel (cf. Psalm 119, Lamentations). Archaeological finds of weaving weights, spindles (פֶּלֶךְ, v. 19), and textile tools from Iron Age Israel confirm the extensive textile production described. Excavations at Tel Rehov and other sites have uncovered households where women played a central economic role through textile work, which aligns with the image of the woman trading with merchants and clothing her family in quality fabrics such as linen (שֵׁשׁ) and purple (אַרְגָּמָן).
