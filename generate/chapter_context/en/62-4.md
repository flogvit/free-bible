## Key Concepts

**Πνεῦμα (pneuma) – "spirit"**: In the first part of the chapter (vv. 1–6), this term is used in a distinctive way that reflects the spiritual atmosphere of late antiquity. John urges believers to "test the spirits" (δοκιμάζετε τὰ πνεύματα), an expression that mirrors the widespread belief in both Jewish and Greco-Roman circles that spiritual powers could inspire prophets and teachers. The verb δοκιμάζω was used for testing metals for authenticity, suggesting a thorough, critical evaluation of spiritual claims.

**Ἐν σαρκὶ ἐληλυθότα – "come in the flesh"** (v. 2): This expression constitutes the very test criterion for distinguishing true from false teaching. The formulation directly addresses proto-Gnostic or docetic tendencies that claimed Christ only appeared to have a physical body. The Greek perfect participle (ἐληλυθότα) emphasizes that the incarnation is an enduring reality—Jesus came and remains in the flesh.

**Ἀγάπη (agape)**: The second main section of the chapter (vv. 7–21) provides the most concentrated treatment of love in the New Testament. The famous declaration "ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν" ("God is love," vv. 8, 16) is unique in biblical literature. While Greek philosophy often defined the divine through abstract attributes such as immutability or omnipotence, John defines God's nature through his relational actions.

**Ἱλασμός (hilasmos) – "atonement sacrifice"** (v. 10): This term, which also appears in 2:2, has its background both in the Greek translation of the Old Testament (Septuaginta) and in Greco-Roman religious practice. In a Jewish context, it points to the rituals of the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), while in the broader Hellenistic world it was well known from temple worship. John uses the term to explain how God's love was concretely expressed.

**Παρρησία (parresia) – "boldness"** (v. 17): In a Greek political context, this denoted a citizen's right to free speech in the assembly. John transfers the concept to believers' relationship with God on the day of judgment—those who live in love will have bold access to God, without fear or shame.

## Events and Customs

The chapter's warning against "false prophets" (ψευδοπροφῆται, v. 1) reflects a time of intense spiritual activity and competing religious voices. Traveling prophets and teachers claiming divine inspiration were common in this period, and congregations had to develop criteria for evaluating their legitimacy.

The contrast between the "spirit of truth" and the "spirit of error" (τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς ἀληθείας καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς πλάνης, v. 6) has parallels in Jewish apocalyptic thought. This dualistic pattern helped readers understand the spiritual struggle between truth and falsehood described in the chapter.
