## People

### Mordecai
Mordecai's position and influence reach their peak in this chapter. He is described as "great in the king's house," with a reputation spreading to all provinces, and he "continues to grow greater" (9:3-4). His power is so significant that all the officials of the empire—satraps, governors, and royal servants—support the Jews out of fear for him (9:3). Mordecai takes the initiative to establish Purim as an annual festival by sending letters to all Jews throughout the empire (9:20-21), and together with Queen Esther he writes a second letter to confirm the celebration (9:29-31).

### Haman's Ten Sons
The ten sons named in the text (9:7-10)—Parshandatha, Dalphon, Aspatha, Poratha, Adalia, Aridatha, Parmashta, Arisai, Aridai, and Vaizatha—all bear Persian names, confirming the family's integration into Persian culture. They were killed along with the other 500 in Susa on the first day (9:6-10), and at Esther's request their bodies were hanged on the gallows the following day (9:13-14). In traditional Hebrew manuscripts, these names are written in a distinctive vertical column, and certain letters appear in unusual sizes.

## Events and Customs

### The Jews' Victory and Self-Defense
The chapter describes the fulfillment of the decree granting the Jews the right to defend themselves. On the thirteenth day of Adar, the Jews gathered in their cities throughout the empire and struck down their enemies (9:1-2). No one could stand against them because fear of them had fallen upon all peoples (9:2). In Susa, 500 men were killed on the first day (9:6), and at Esther's request for an additional day, another 300 were killed on the second day (9:15). In the other provinces, the Jews killed 75,000 of their enemies (9:16). The text emphasizes three times that the Jews did not lay hands on the plunder (9:10, 15, 16), even though the decree gave them the right to it.

### Public Hanging of Haman's Sons
Esther's request to hang the bodies of Haman's ten sons on the gallows (9:13-14) reflects an ancient Near Eastern practice in which executed enemies' bodies were publicly displayed as a warning and disgrace. This involved hanging already dead bodies on wooden stakes. The practice is known from Assyrian and Persian sources and aimed to demonstrate complete victory and deter future rebellions.

### Origin and Practice of the Purim Festival
The chapter describes the historical background for the festival of Purim and establishes its rituals. The name "Purim" comes from the word "pur" (פּוּר), meaning "lot" (9:24: "הִפִּיל פּוּר הוּא הַגּוֹרָל") – a reference to the casting of lots that Haman used to determine the date for the destruction of the Jews. The Jews in the provinces rested and celebrated on the fourteenth day of Adar, while the Jews in Susa, who fought for two days, rested and celebrated on the fifteenth (9:17-19).

The chapter establishes four central elements of the celebration: a feast (מִשְׁתֶּה), joy (שִׂמְחָה), sending food gifts to one another (מִשְׁלוֹחַ מָנוֹת), and gifts to the poor (מַתָּנוֹת לָאֶבְיוֹנִים) (9:22). The festival marks "the month that was turned for them from sorrow into gladness and from mourning into a holiday" (9:22).

## Key Terms

### Perazim – the Unwalled Towns
The expression "הַפְּרָזִים" (ha-perazim, 9:19) refers to Jews living in unwalled villages without fortifications, as opposed to those living in cities like Susa. The word comes from the root פ-ר-ז, indicating openness or lack of protection. This distinction explains why the celebration dates varied.

### Kiyyemu ve-kibbelu – The Obligation
The phrase "קִיְּמוּ וְקִבְּלוּ" (9:27) – "they confirmed and undertook" – describes the Jews' commitment to celebrate Purim annually. This double formulation emphasizes both the binding and voluntary aspects of establishing the festival, and it includes their descendants as well as all who joined them.
