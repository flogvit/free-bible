# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 6

## People

**The priests and diviners of the Philistines (כֹּהֲנִים וְקֹסְמִים)** played a central role in Philistine religious life. The priests served at the temples of gods such as Dagon, while the diviners practiced various forms of divination to interpret divine will. The combination of these two groups shows that the Philistines sought both ritual and oracular guidance during their crisis. The use of diviners (קֹסְמִים) was forbidden in Israel (Deut 18:10), but it was common practice in surrounding cultures.

**The five lords (סַרְנֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים)** represented the leaders of the five Philistine city-states: Ashdod, Gaza, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron. The Hebrew word "seren" (סֶרֶן) is a non-Semitic loanword, possibly of Aegean origin, and is used exclusively for Philistine rulers. This reflects the Aegean origins of the Philistines.

## Places

**Beth-Shemesh** (בֵּית שֶׁמֶשׁ, "house of the sun") was located in the Shephelah region, on the border between Philistine and Israelite territory. The name suggests that the site may originally have been a Canaanite cult place dedicated to the sun god. The city was a Levitical town (Josh 21:16), which explains why Levites were present to handle the ark (v. 15). Archaeological excavations at Tel Beth-Shemesh have revealed settlement from the Bronze Age and Iron Age.

**Kiriath-Jearim** (קִרְיַת־יְעָרִים, "city of forests") was located higher up in the Judean hills, about 13 km from Beth-Shemesh. The city had a Canaanite background and belonged to the Gibeonites (Josh 9:17). Its location on the heights gave it a degree of isolation, which may have been considered safer for storing the ark.

## Events and Customs

**The guilt offering (אָשָׁם)** was a known category in Israelite sacrificial practice, but here the concept is used by the Philistines. The gold objects functioned as "compensation" or "atonement" for wrongdoing against the God of Israel. The practice of making votive gifts in the form of body parts or plagues that afflicted someone was widespread in the ancient world. At sanctuaries in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean region, similar votive offerings made of metal or terracotta representing healed body parts have been found.

**The test with the cows** was a form of divination. Cows that had recently given birth would naturally seek to return to their calves. By separating them and letting them pull the cart without guidance, the Philistines created a test: if the cows went against their natural instinct and pulled toward Israelite territory, this confirmed divine intervention. The use of animals that had never borne a yoke in religious contexts was common in antiquity and is also found in Israelite practice (Num 19:2; Deut 21:3).

**The reference to Egypt and Pharaoh** (v. 6) shows that the story of the exodus from Egypt was known even among neighboring peoples. The Philistine priests warned against the same kind of hardening that led to Egypt's disaster.

## Key Terms

**טְחֹרִים / עֹפָלִים (techorim/ofalim)** – The text has two readings: "boils" or "hemorrhoids." The ketiv (the written form) has עפלי, while the qere (the read form) has טְחֹרֵי. This may indicate both the nature of the disease and a certain modesty in the transmission. Together with the mice, this suggests a serious epidemic.

**עַכְבָּרִים (akbarim, "mice/rodents")** are mentioned as destroyers of the land (v. 5). The golden mice represented a prayer that the plague would cease. Some scholars have proposed a link to bubonic plague based on the combination of boils and rodents, but this interpretation is speculative.

**אַרְגַּז (argaz)** – the word for "chest" or "box" into which the gold objects were placed, occurs only here in the Old Testament. It may be a Philistine loanword, underscoring that this was a Philistine arrangement.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations have uncovered votive offerings from Philistine temples, including small metal and ceramic objects. At Ekron, a large temple complex from the Iron Age with numerous cultic items has been found. Although no gold objects as described in the text have been discovered, these findings confirm the practice of making votive offerings in Philistine religion. Furthermore, Philistine pottery has been found at Beth-Shemesh, confirming cultural contact between the Philistines and Israelites in this border region.
