# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 15

## People

**The Canaanite woman** is a central figure in this chapter. Matthew uses the term "Χαναναία" (Canaanite), an archaic word that points back to Israel's ancient enemies in the Old Testament. Mark calls her Syrophoenician (Mark 7:26), which was the common designation at that time. Matthew's choice of words emphasizes that she belongs to the Gentiles and historically to Israel's enemies, making her faith all the more remarkable. The fact that she addresses Jesus as "Son of David" shows that she was familiar with Jewish messianic expectations.

**The Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem** represent the religious elite who traveled from the capital city to examine Jesus' ministry. Their coming from Jerusalem suggests that Jesus' reputation had reached the highest religious circles, and that they felt a need to confront him directly.

## Places

**Tyre and Sidon** (v. 21) were Phoenician coastal cities northwest of Galilee, in Gentile territory. Tyre was located about 60–65 kilometers from Capernaum. These cities were well-known trading centers with a long history and often received negative mention in the Old Testament (Isa 23; Ezek 26–28). That Jesus traveled to this area is significant—it marks one of his few journeys outside Jewish territory.

**The Sea of Galilee and the mountain** (v. 29) form the setting for the second feeding miracle. The expression "God of Israel" (v. 31), which the crowd praises, may indicate that this region had a mixed population of both Jews and Gentiles. **Magadan** (v. 39) is likely identical with Magdala on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, known as the hometown of Mary Magdalene.

## Events and Customs

**Handwashing before meals** (v. 2) was not a hygienic but a ritual requirement developed in oral tradition. This practice was not commanded in the Mosaic Law but was part of "the tradition of the elders" (παράδοσις τῶν πρεσβυτέρων). The Pharisees had developed detailed rules for ritual purity, which were later written down in the Mishna tractate Yadayim ("Hands"). They believed that everyday objects and contact with other people could make hands ritually unclean, and that this must be washed away before eating.

**The Korban vow** (vv. 5–6) was a practice by which some could declare their property as "a gift" (δῶρον, Hebrew: קָרְבָּן)—that is, dedicated to the temple. This could be misused to avoid financial responsibility toward one's parents, since the property was then formally pledged away. Jesus criticizes this as an example of how human traditions could be used to bypass God's command to honor father and mother.

## Key Terms

**Κοινοῖ** (koinoi, "makes unclean/profanes") appears several times in the chapter (vv. 11, 18, 20). This verb deals with ritual uncleanness—that which makes a person unfit to participate in worship or fellowship. Jesus' radical teaching is that uncleanness does not come from external factors such as food or unwashed hands, but from the evil thoughts and deeds of the heart.

**Κυνάριον** (kynarion, "little dog," vv. 26–27) is the diminutive form of "dog." While "dog" was often a derogatory term for Gentiles, the diminutive may suggest a house pet rather than a street dog. The woman's reply shows quick-witted humility: she accepts the image but points out that even the dogs under the table get crumbs.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in ancient Tyre and Sidon have revealed prosperous Phoenician cities with extensive trade and religious pluralism. Inscriptions and temples show that the area was characterized by Canaanite religion, including worship of Baal and Astarte. This provides context for why the encounter with the Canaanite woman was so culturally charged—she came from an environment with a completely different religious background than the Jewish one.

At the Sea of Galilee, archaeologists have found remains of fishing villages and boats from the first century, including a first-century fishing boat discovered in 1986. These findings confirm the maritime culture that forms the backdrop for Jesus' travels around the lake, including the conclusion of this chapter where he takes a boat to Magadan.
