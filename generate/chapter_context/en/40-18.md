# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 18

## Events and Customs

### Children in the Ancient World
When Jesus uses a child (παιδίον, *paidion*) as an example in verses 1–5, he overturns contemporary social values. In Greco-Roman and Jewish culture, children had low social status and no rights until they reached adulthood. They were dependent, vulnerable, and without influence. Rabbinic tradition could use children as a picture of those who had not yet learned the Torah. Jesus’ point is not about the "innocence" of children (a modern romantic notion), but rather their dependence and lack of status—precisely the opposite of what the disciples were seeking.

### Church Discipline
The procedure for conflict resolution in verses 15–17 reflects Jewish legal practice. The requirement of "two or three witnesses" (v. 16) quotes directly from Deuteronomy 19:15, which was foundational in Jewish legal thinking. The term "church" (ἐκκλησία, *ekklēsia*) is used here, and this is one of only two places in the Gospels where Jesus uses this word (cf. Matt 16:18). To treat someone as "a Gentile and a tax collector" (v. 17) meant regarding that person as outside the covenant community—not as an expression of contempt, but as an acknowledgment that the individual had placed themselves outside it.

## Key Terms

### Μύλος ὀνικός (Millstone)
The expression in verse 6 refers to a large millstone (μύλος ὀνικός, *mylos onikos*, "donkey millstone") so heavy that it had to be turned by a donkey, unlike hand mills. Such stones weighed several hundred kilograms. Drowning was not a Jewish method of execution but was known from Greek and Roman practice. The image underscores the seriousness of leading "little" believers astray—such a fate would be preferable to the judgment awaiting them.

### Γέεννα (Gehenna)
"Hell fire" in verse 9 (γέενναν τοῦ πυρός, *geennan tou pyros*) comes from the Hebrew *Ge-Hinnom* (Valley of Hinnom), a valley south of Jerusalem. In the Old Testament, this place was associated with child sacrifice to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). In intertestamental times, "Gehenna" developed into the term for the final place of punishment for the wicked, distinct from *Sheol/Hades*, which was the general abode of the dead.

### Binding and Loosing
The terminology of "binding" (δέω, *deō*) and "loosing" (λύω, *lyō*) in verse 18 were technical terms in rabbinic tradition. They primarily referred to the authority to interpret the Torah—to declare something forbidden (*assur*) or permitted (*muttar*). In the context of the church, this concerns the community’s authority to make decisions about what aligns with God’s will, including matters of membership and discipline.

### Forgiveness: Seventy Times Seven
Peter’s suggestion to forgive "up to seven times" (v. 21) was generous by rabbinic standards; some traditions suggested three times as sufficient. Jesus’ answer "seventy-seven times" or "seventy times seven" (ἑβδομηκοντάκις ἑπτά, v. 22)—either 77 or 490—likely alludes to Genesis 4:24, where Lamek demands seventy-seven-fold vengeance. Jesus turns the logic of vengeance into limitless forgiveness.

### Talents and Denarii
The parable of the unforgiving servant (vv. 23–35) uses concrete economic figures that would have been striking to the listeners. Ten thousand talents (μυρίων ταλάντων, v. 24) was an astronomical sum—equivalent to about 60 million daily wages, more than the annual tax revenue from entire provinces. The historian Josephus reports that all of Galilee and Perea together paid only 200 talents in annual taxes to the Romans. By comparison, one hundred denarii (v. 28) was roughly three to four months’ wages for a laborer. The contrast illustrates the grotesque nature of refusing forgiveness after having received incomparable mercy.
