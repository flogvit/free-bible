# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 15

## People

Paul lists several important eyewitnesses to Christ’s resurrection. **Cephas** (v. 5) is the Aramaic name for Peter, and its use suggests an early, Palestinian confession tradition. **James** (v. 7) refers to Jesus’ brother, who according to the Jewish historian Josephus was executed in Jerusalem around AD 62. James’s inclusion is remarkable since he was not among Jesus’ disciples during his earthly ministry. Paul also mentions **the twelve** as a technical term for the circle of apostles, as well as over **five hundred brothers and sisters** who saw the risen Lord at once—a claim that was verifiable since "most of them are still alive" (v. 6).

## Places

**Ephesus** is mentioned in verse 32, where Paul describes that he "fought with wild beasts." Ephesus was the capital of the Roman province of Asia. The expression about wild beasts may refer to literal gladiatorial combat, or more likely it is used metaphorically for the violent opposition Paul encountered there. Acts 19 describes riots in Ephesus connected with Paul’s ministry.

## Events and Customs

Verse 29 mentions the puzzling practice of **baptism for the dead** (βαπτιζόμενοι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν). This custom is not described elsewhere in the New Testament, and it is unclear whether Paul approved of the practice or merely used it as an argument ad hominem against those who denied the resurrection. Some scholars suggest it may have been a practice where living believers were baptized on behalf of deceased relatives who died before being baptized. Paul neither condemns nor recommends the custom, but uses it only to show the inconsistency of practicing this while denying the resurrection.

## Key Terms

**Ἀπαρχή** (aparchē, "firstfruits," vv. 20, 23) is a term drawn from the Jewish sacrificial system. The firstfruits were the first portion of the harvest offered to God, representing and consecrating the entire crop. By calling Christ the "firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep," Paul signals that Christ’s resurrection guarantees and inaugurates the future resurrection of believers.

**Σῶμα ψυχικόν** versus **σῶμα πνευματικόν** (v. 44) – "natural body" and "spiritual body" – is an important conceptual pair. The Greek *psychikos* does not denote anything inferior, but that which belongs to the present created order driven by *psychē* (the life principle). *Pneumatikos* refers to a body completely permeated and governed by God’s Spirit (*pneuma*), not an immaterial or "spiritual" body in the modern sense.

**Μυστήριον** (mystērion, "mystery," v. 51) is used here not in the sense of mystery cults, but concerning a divine truth now revealed: that not all will die, but all will be transformed at Christ’s coming.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verses 3–5 contain what scholars regard as one of the earliest Christian confessional formulas. The technical terms **παρέδωκα** ("I delivered") and **παρέλαβον** ("I received") are rabbinic technical terms for the transmission of tradition. The formula has a poetic, rhythmic structure with four "that" clauses (ὅτι), suggesting it was used in teaching and worship before Paul wrote this letter. This makes the text one of the earliest datable witnesses to belief in Christ’s death and resurrection, likely formulated within a few years after the crucifixion.
