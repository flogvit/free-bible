# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 9

## People

**Jehu, son of Jehoshaphat, son of Nimshi** was a commander in the Israelite army who served under King Joram. His anointing as king marked the beginning of the Jehu dynasty, which would rule over the Northern Kingdom for five generations (Jehu, Jehoahaz, Joash, Jeroboam II, and Zechariah). The existence of Jehu is confirmed by the Assyrian "Black Obelisk" of Shalmaneser III (circa 841–824 B.C.), where he is referred to as "Jehu, son of Omri"—a designation used by the Assyrians to identify kings of Israel. The obelisk depicts Jehu (or his envoy) in submission before the Assyrian king, providing a rare contemporary portrait of a biblical figure.

**Jezebel** was the daughter of Ethbaal, king of Sidon, and became queen through her marriage to Ahab. She represented Phoenician religious influence in Israel, particularly the worship of Baal. Her application of "puk" (a black eye cosmetic) and arrangement of her hair before meeting Jehu reflect both her royal dignity and possibly Phoenician ideals of beauty. Her mocking reference to "Zimri" (זִמְרִי, v. 31) demonstrates her knowledge of Israel's history—Zimri was an officer who killed his master King Elah but reigned for only seven days himself.

**Ahaziah, king of Judah**, was the son of Joram of Judah and Athaliah (daughter of Ahab and Jezebel). His visit to his wounded relative Joram unfortunately placed him in Jezreel when Jehu's coup took place. His death is indirectly confirmed by the Tel Dan inscription.

## Places

**Ramoth-gilead** (Hebrew: רָמֹת גִּלְעָד) was located east of the Jordan River in the Gilead region, likely identified with Tell er-Rumeith in modern-day Jordan. The site was strategically important and contested between Israel and Aram (Syria). It was here that Ahab had previously fallen in battle (1 Kings 22), and it remained a frontline in the conflict with King Hazael of Aram.

**Jezreel** (Hebrew: יִזְרְעֶאל) was one of the royal residences of the Omride dynasty, located in the fertile Jezreel Valley. Archaeological excavations at Tel Jezreel have uncovered remains of a fortified complex from the 9th century B.C., featuring casemate walls and a corner tower, matching the description of a royal compound with watchtowers and gates. **Naboth's vineyard** (vv. 21, 25–26), where Joram was killed, directly links this event to Ahab's earlier crime (1 Kings 21).

**The ascent at Gur, near Ibleam** (v. 27) marks the place where Ahaziah was mortally wounded. Ibleam is identified with Khirbet Bel'ameh, south of the Jezreel Valley, along the escape route toward Megiddo.

## Events and Customs

**Secret anointing** illustrates an important practice in ancient Israel. The fact that the prophet's disciple was to anoint Jehu "in a private room" and then flee immediately shows this was a politically dangerous mission—effectively a declaration of rebellion against the reigning king. Pouring oil over the head was the ritual act that legitimized royal authority through divine selection.

**The throwing of garments and blowing of the shofar** (v. 13) represent two key elements in proclaiming a new king. The officers laying their cloaks under Jehu's feet on the steps was an act of submission, while the sound of the shofar was the traditional signal announcing a new king's accession to the throne. This spontaneous ceremony indicates that the coup had military support.

**The role of the court officials (סָרִיסִים)** in Jezebel's death is remarkable. These court officials—often eunuchs in Eastern courts—chose to obey Jehu rather than their own queen, indicating how quickly power shifted.

## Key Terms

The expression **"meshugga"** (הַמְשֻׁגָּע, "the madman," v. 11), used by the officers to describe the prophet's disciple, reflects how prophetic ecstasy could be perceived by outsiders. The word suggests intense, unconventional behavior associated with prophetic activity.

**"Zenuyim"** (זְנוּנִים, "whoredoms," v. 22) is used here metaphorically for religious apostasy—the worship of foreign gods was consistently portrayed as unfaithfulness to Israel's God. The connection with **"keshefim"** (כְּשָׁפִים, "sorceries") points to the religious practices Jezebel had introduced from Phoenicia.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Black Obelisk from Nimrud** (circa 841–824 B.C.) is the most direct archaeological witness to this chapter. The relief shows a kneeling figure identified in cuneiform as "Jehu, son of Omri," bringing tribute to Shalmaneser III. This is the only known contemporary depiction of an Israelite king and confirms both Jehu's historical existence and his later political situation as a vassal under Assyria.

**The Tel Dan inscription** (circa 840–830 B.C.), discovered in 1993–94, is an Aramaic victory stele in which King Hazael of Damascus boasts of having killed "the king of Israel" and "the king of the house of David." Although Hazael claims credit for himself, the names correspond to Joram and Ahaziah, the two kings whom 2 Kings 9 states were killed by Jehu. This raises interesting questions about the relationship between the two accounts.
