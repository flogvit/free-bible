# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 20

## Characters

Zophar from Na'amah speaks for the second time in this chapter (his final speech in the book). His name has an uncertain meaning, possibly related to the root "to see" or "to spy." Na'amah was likely a city in northwestern Arabia, Edom, or possibly in Judah (cf. Josh 15:41), which places Zophar in the same geographical and cultural sphere as the other friends. His speech is marked by emotional intensity – he admits that he is disturbed (vv. 2-3) and feels personally offended by Job's words.

## Key Terms

**שְׂעִפַּי (se'ippay)** – "my troubled thoughts" (v. 2). This rare Hebrew word describes divided, disturbing thoughts. It suggests an inner mental unrest that drives Zophar to respond.

**פְּתָנִים (petanim)** – "poisonous snakes/cockatrices" (vv. 14, 16). This term refers to a particularly venomous snake species, likely the Egyptian cobra or asp. In ancient Near Eastern thought, the serpent's poison was an image of deadly evil that ultimately turns against the one who practices it.

**אֶפְעֶה (ef'eh)** – "viper/adder" (v. 16). Another word for a poisonous snake, which reinforces the imagery of venom. The combination of these serpent terms points to a widespread belief in the Middle East that immoral wealth poisons from within.

## Events and Customs

The chapter's central image of "swallowing riches only to vomit them up again" (v. 15) reflects an ancient notion that ill-gotten gains cannot be kept. In several ancient cultures of the Middle East, there was a belief that the body physically reacted to moral wrongdoing – dishonesty would literally make one sick. The metaphor of honey and cream (v. 17) – "streams of honey and cream" – represented the ultimate picture of prosperity and blessing in the ancient world, especially in a semi-arid landscape. That the wicked will not see these streams means they are excluded from life's richest blessings.

The description that heaven "reveals his guilt" and the earth "rises up against him" (v. 27) reflects a cosmic understanding of justice where all creation testifies against the wicked – a concept found in several Old Testament texts and Mesopotamian literature.
