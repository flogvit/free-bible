# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 37

## Places

**The Valley (הַבִּקְעָה, ha-biq'ah)** in verses 1–2 is not identified with a specific geographical location, but the word is also used in Ezekiel 3:22–23 and 8:4 to describe the place where Ezekiel previously encountered God's glory. The term denotes a wide, flat plain or valley floor. Some scholars have suggested that this may be a visionary depiction of a battlefield or mass grave, which was a well-known reality in the ancient Near East following major military confrontations. Unburied bodies on the battlefield were considered a great shame and calamity in ancient Near Eastern culture.

## Key Terms

**Ruach (רוּחַ)** is a central word in this chapter and appears with several meanings: "wind," "breath," "spirit," and "Spirit." In verse 9, the word is used four times with different nuances: Ezekiel is to prophesy to the "ruach" (the wind/spirit), which will come from the four "ruchot" (directions of the wind) and breathe upon the dead so that they receive "ruach" (the breath of life). This ambiguity is intentional and connects to the creation account in Genesis 2:7, where God breathes the breath of life into humanity.

**The Dry Bones (עֲצָמוֹת יְבֵשׁוֹת)** represent a state of complete hopelessness. In verse 11, the people's lament is quoted: "Our bones are dried up, our hope is lost; we are cut off." The expression "our bones are dried up" was likely an idiom describing total despair and physical exhaustion among the exiles.

**Tree/Stick (עֵץ, 'ets)** in verses 16–17 can be translated as either "tree" or "stick." This probably refers to a writing stylus or a piece of wood used for writing, possibly a precursor to a scroll or a marker indicating tribal affiliation.

## Events and Customs

**The Symbolic Action with the Two Sticks** (verses 15–28) was a typical prophetic sign-act. Ezekiel was to write on two pieces of wood—one for Judah and one for Joseph/Ephraim—and join them together into one. This illustrated the reunification of the southern kingdom (Judah) and the northern kingdom (Israel/Ephraim), which had been divided since the time of Rehoboam (around 930 BC, see 1 Kings 12). For the exiles, this was a powerful promise: not only would they return, but the historical division that had weakened the nation for over 300 years would also be healed.

**Opening the Graves** (verses 12–13) was a radical image. In Israelite thought, the grave was the final resting place, and Sheol was the realm where one was cut off from God. That God would open graves and bring the people up was an unprecedented promise that shatters ordinary notions of death and demonstrates God's power over even the most extreme boundaries.

## People

**My Servant David** (verses 24–25) does not refer to the historical King David, who had been dead for more than 400 years, but to a future Davidic ruler. In verses 22 and 24, the title "king" (מֶלֶךְ, melech) is used, while verse 25 uses "prince" (נָשִׂיא, nasi). This alternation may reflect Ezekiel's nuanced view of future leadership, where נָשִׂיא possibly points to a different kind of leadership in the restored community than the failed monarchy.
