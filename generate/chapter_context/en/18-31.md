## Events and Customs

Job 31 presents a detailed "purity code" or negative confession, in which Job declares his innocence by listing sins he has not committed. This form has parallels in Egyptian religious practice, particularly chapter 125 of the "Book of the Dead," where the deceased makes a similar negative confession before the gods. Job's oath covers sexual purity (vv. 1, 9–12), honesty in business dealings (vv. 5–6), fair treatment of servants (vv. 13–15), care for the poor and vulnerable (vv. 16–23), rejection of idolatry (vv. 24–28), and hospitality (vv. 31–32). The structure with "if I have done this… let this happen to me" follows a well-known oath pattern from ancient Near Eastern legal practice.

## Key Terms

The word **תָּו** (*tav*, v. 35) is the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet and in antiquity was written as a cross or an X mark. Job sets his "tav"—his signature or mark confirming the truth of the oath—and asks that the Almighty answer him. This reflects a practice where illiterate people made a mark instead of signing legal documents. The phrase **עָוֺן פְּלִילִים** (*avon pelilim*, vv. 11, 28) means "a sin before judges"—that is, an offense requiring legal action, not merely moral condemnation. The expression **יִשְׁקְלֵנִי בְמֹאזְנֵי־צֶדֶק** ("weigh me on scales of justice," v. 6) alludes to the widespread ancient Near Eastern belief in a divine weighing of human deeds.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verses 26–28, concerning sun and moon worship, reflect a common practice in the ancient Middle East. The gesture where "the hand kisses the mouth" (v. 27: וַתִּשַּׁק יָדִי לְפִי) likely describes an act of worship directed toward heavenly bodies—an expression of reverence and devotion. Archaeological finds from Mesopotamia and Canaan show extensive astral cults. Job's explicit rejection of this places his faith in contrast to surrounding religions and underscores monotheistic piety. The verses on agricultural ethics (vv. 38–40)—that the land itself "cries out" if it has been unjustly acquired—reflect ancient Near Eastern ideas that land and property had a sacred dimension tied to family and justice.
