# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 21

## Places

**The Temple and the Treasury (γαζοφυλάκιον):** The treasury was located in the "Court of Women" within the temple complex, where thirteen trumpet-shaped offering chests were placed along the walls. These chests were marked for various purposes—temple tax, offerings, wood, incense, etc. Both men and women had access to this area. Herod the Great's temple construction, which began around 20 B.C.E., made it one of the most impressive structures of antiquity. The "beautiful stones" (λίθοις καλοῖς) admired by Jesus' contemporaries were massive white limestone blocks, some up to 12 meters long and weighing hundreds of tons. Votive offerings (ἀναθήμασιν) included the golden vine above the temple entrance and other costly gifts from wealthy Jews and pagan rulers.

**The Mount of Olives (τὸ ὄρος τὸ καλούμενον Ἐλαιῶν):** This ridge east of Jerusalem, separated from the city by the Kidron Valley, was covered with olive trees. It lay about a Sabbath day's journey (approximately 1 km) from the city and served as a natural resting place for pilgrims. Jesus' nighttime stays here in the days before Passover reflected both practical considerations (sleeping outside the overcrowded city) and possible security needs.

## Events and Customs

**The Widow's Temple Offering:** The widow's two "mites" (λεπτά) were the smallest bronze or copper coins in circulation—two lepta equaled one quadrans (the smallest Roman copper coin), which amounted to 1/64 of a denarius (a day's wage). Voluntary gifts to the temple were an important expression of piety, and givers could be observed by others. Jewish law protected widows as a vulnerable group, yet in practice many lived in extreme poverty. That she gave "all she had to live on" (πάντα τὸν βίον) underscores her radical trust in God.

**Persecution in Synagogues and Before Authorities:** Jesus describes a legal process where believers are "handed over to synagogues and prisons" (v. 12) and brought before "kings and governors" (βασιλεῖς καὶ ἡγεμόνας). Synagogues held local judicial authority and could impose flogging. The Roman authorities—the provincial governors and client kings such as Herod Antipas—represented the higher court of appeal.

## Key Terms

**"The Times of the Gentiles" (καιροὶ ἐθνῶν):** This expression, unique to Luke, denotes the period during which Jerusalem will be "trampled by the Gentiles" (πατουμένη ὑπὸ ἐθνῶν). The term καιρός suggests a limited, fixed period rather than indefinite time (χρόνος). The phrasing has parallels in Daniel 8:13-14 and Zechariah 12:3, implying that Gentile dominion has a divinely set limit.

**"Desolation" (ἐρήμωσις):** This word connects with prophetic language concerning the fall of Jerusalem, particularly the "abomination of desolation" in Daniel 9:27; 11:31; 12:11. In Luke's version, the sign is militarily concrete: Jerusalem surrounded by armies (στρατοπέδων). Matthew and Mark use the more symbolic expression "the abomination of desolation."

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The destruction of Jerusalem in 70 C.E. corresponds to Jesus' prophecy that "not one stone will be left upon another" (v. 6). Josephus describes how the Romans under Titus systematically tore down the temple, and how soldiers even dug between the stones to find melted gold. Archaeological excavations at the southwest corner of the Temple Mount have uncovered massive stone blocks thrown down from the temple wall, still lying where they fell—a vivid illustration of the historical fulfillment of this prophecy. The inscription "to the place of trumpeting" found among the ruins testifies to the former splendor of the temple.
