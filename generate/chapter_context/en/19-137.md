# Historical and Cultural Context for the Book of Psalms, Chapter 137

## Places

**Babylon and the Rivers** – The psalm opens with a reference to "the rivers of Babylon", which points to the Euphrates and Tigris rivers as well as the extensive canal system in Babylonia. The exiled Jews were settled in various areas, including by the Kebar Canal (Ezek 1:1–3). These riverbanks served as natural gathering places for the exile community, possibly for prayer and mourning since they were outside the city’s idolatrous worship.

**Zion and Jerusalem** – These names are used interchangeably in the psalm and represent both the physical city and the temple that was destroyed in 586 B.C. For the exiles, Jerusalem was not merely a geographical location but the spiritual center for the worship of YHWH.

## Events and Customs

**The Babylonian Exile** – The psalm directly reflects the experience of exile following Nebuchadnezzar’s destruction of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. The Babylonians practiced mass deportation of conquered peoples, a strategy aimed at breaking down national identity. The exiles were forced to entertain their oppressors—a humiliating practice known from other ancient sources.

**Edom’s Betrayal** – Verse 7 mentions "the sons of Edom" who called for the destruction of Jerusalem. The Edomites, Israel’s neighboring people and relatives (descendants of Esau), exploited Judah’s fall by plundering and hindering refugees. This betrayal was deeply felt because it came from a "brother nation" (cf. Obadiah; Ezek 25:12–14).

## Key Terms

**Kinnor (כִּנּוֹר)** – The Hebrew word is often translated as "harp" or "lyre", and was a stringed instrument central to temple worship. Hanging the kinnor on trees symbolized that the music of worship had fallen silent—an act of grief and protest.

**"A song of YHWH" (שִׁיר־יְהוָה)** – This expression in verse 4 refers to the sacred song belonging to temple worship. The question "How can we sing?" expresses a theological crisis: Could YHWH be worshipped on foreign, unclean soil, far from his chosen dwelling?

**"Blessed is he" (אַשְׁרֵי)** – This word, known from Psalm 1:1, is used here in verses 8–9 concerning the one who repays Babylon. This shocking image reflects ancient warfare brutality where victors crushed the children of the defeated (cf. Hos 10:14; 2 Kgs 8:12). The phrasing expresses raw despair and a cry for just retribution rather than a divine command to act.
