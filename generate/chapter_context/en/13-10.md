# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 10

## People

**Saul** was Israel's first king, anointed by the prophet Samuel. He belonged to the tribe of Benjamin. **Jonathan**, Saul's son, was known for his close friendship with David. **Abinadab** and **Malki-Shua** were two of Saul's other sons who also fell in battle. The Chronicler omits Ish-Bosheth (Eshbaal), who according to 2 Samuel survived.

The **armor-bearer** played an important role in ancient Israel as the king's closest protector in combat. His refusal to kill Saul may reflect a deep reverence for the anointed king, even in a desperate situation.

## Places

**Mount Gilboa** (הַר גִּלְבֹּעַ) is located in the northern part of the central highlands, southeast of the Jezreel Valley. The mountain rises to about 500 meters above sea level and controlled important trade routes. The site was strategically significant because it overlooked the valley where the Philistines had gathered their army.

**Jabesh-gilead** lay east of the Jordan River in the Transjordan region. Its inhabitants had a special connection with Saul because he had previously rescued them from the Ammonites (1 Samuel 11). This explains their courageous effort to retrieve and bury his remains.

**The temple of Dagon** (בֵּית דָּגוֹן) was the sanctuary of the Philistines' chief god. Dagon was a fertility deity worshipped throughout the Canaanite region. Archaeological finds from Ugarit show that the cult of Dagon was widespread among Semitic peoples long before the arrival of the Philistines.

## Events and Customs

**Plundering the dead** (לְפַשֵּׁט אֶת־הֽ͏ַחֲלָלִים) was common practice in ancient warfare. The Philistines' treatment of Saul's body—taking his head and weapons as trophies—was a way to demonstrate victory and humiliate the enemy. Sending messengers with news of their triumph to the temples was a religious act that gave honor to their gods.

**A seven-day fast** was a traditional mourning period in Israel. This marked the most intense phase of grief and showed respect for the dead. The fact that the men of Jabesh fasted for seven days underscores the depth of their gratitude and sorrow.

**Suicide in war** was culturally accepted as an alternative to being captured and dishonored by the enemy. The term "uncircumcised" (הָעֲרֵלִים) was a derogatory expression used by Israelites for the Philistines, emphasizing their status outside the covenant community.

## Key Terms

**Maʿal** (מַעַל) – "treachery" or "unfaithfulness" – is a central concept in verse 13. This word is used to describe serious breaches of the covenant relationship with God. Saul's sin is described as twofold: he was disobedient to the Lord's word, and he sought counsel from a medium (אוֹב) instead of seeking the Lord.

**Seeking the Lord** (דָּרַשׁ בַּיהוָה) is an important theological theme. The contrast between seeking God and seeking other sources of guidance (such as spiritists) is crucial for evaluating Saul. His fundamental error was that "he did not seek the Lord" (verse 14).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Tel Qasile near modern-day Tel Aviv have uncovered a Philistine temple complex from the Iron Age, providing insight into what Philistine temples looked like, including where war spoils and trophies might have been displayed. Discoveries of weapons and other objects in temple areas confirm the custom of dedicating war booty to the gods.
