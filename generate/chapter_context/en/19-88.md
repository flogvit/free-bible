# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 88

## People

The psalm's superscription mentions two attributions: "the sons of Korah" and "Heman the Ezrahite". Heman is one of the few named psalmists outside David and Asaph. According to 1 Chronicles 6:18–23, Heman was a Levitical singer who served in the temple, and 1 Kings 5:11 (4:31 in some translations) refers to him as one of the wise men whose wisdom Solomon surpassed. The designation "Ezrahite" (הָאֶזְרָחִי) may indicate either affiliation with the Zerah clan from the tribe of Judah, or it could be an honorific title meaning "the native". That the psalm is attributed both to the sons of Korah (a Levitical singing family) and to Heman individually suggests that it held a special place in the temple's liturgical tradition.

## Key Terms

The psalm uses several Hebrew terms for the realm of the dead that reveal Israelite cosmology: **שְׁאוֹל** (she'ol) – the dwelling place of the dead; **בּוֹר** (bor) – the pit or abyss; **אֲבַדּוֹן** (avaddon) – "the place of destruction"; and **אֶרֶץ נְשִׁיָּה** (erets neshijah) – "the land of forgetfulness". These terms do not describe a place of punishment, but rather a shadowy existence cut off from God and community. The expression **רְפָאִים** (refa'im) in verse 11 refers to "shades" or "the powerless dead", a concept Israel shared with neighboring peoples, though without the same cultic worship of the dead.

The rare word **אָפוּנָה** (afunah) in verse 16 is a hapax legomenon – it occurs only here in the entire Old Testament. It is usually translated something like "I am bewildered" or "paralyzed with fear", but its meaning is uncertain, which has led to many different interpretations throughout history.

## Events and Customs

The superscription contains the enigmatic term **עַל־מָחֲלַת לְעַנּוֹת** (al-machalat le'annot), which is likely a musical or liturgical direction. The word may mean "sickness" or "affliction" and suggests that the psalm was used in connection with serious illness or ritual lament. In Israelite culture, sickness was often associated with social isolation – the sick person was regarded as ritually unclean and cut off from the community (verses 9 and 19). The psalm's description of being "counted among those who go down to the pit" (verse 5) reflects the cultural view that serious illness was a form of provisional death, where the person already found themselves in a liminal state between life and death.

The rhetorical questions in verses 11–13 about whether God works wonders for the dead reflect the Old Testament understanding that the realm of the dead was outside God's active sphere – not because God lacked power there, but because the dead could no longer participate in covenant fellowship and praise.
