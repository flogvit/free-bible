# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 46

## Places

**Beersheba** (בְּאֵר שָׁבַע) was the southernmost point of inhabited Canaan, often expressed as "from Dan to Beersheba," and marked the boundary toward the wilderness. The place held special significance for Jacob's family: Here Abraham had planted a tamarisk tree and called upon the name of the Lord (Gen 21:33), and here Isaac had built an altar after God revealed Himself to him (Gen 26:23-25). That Jacob stops precisely here to offer sacrifices to "the God of his father Isaac" shows that he consciously connects with the family's holy tradition before leaving the Promised Land.

**Goshen** (גֹּשֶׁן) was a fertile area in the eastern Nile Delta, well-suited for livestock raising. Egyptian texts refer to this region as a place where Asian nomads were permitted to settle with their flocks, especially during drought periods. The area was strategically located—close enough to the Egyptian center of power for protection, yet sufficiently separated so that the Hebrews could preserve their identity.

## People

**Asenath** (אָסְנַת), daughter of Potiphera, priest of On (v. 20), represents Joseph's integration into Egyptian society. On (also known as Heliopolis) was the center of sun worship in Egypt and one of the kingdom's most important religious centers. That Joseph married the daughter of a priest there shows what high status Pharaoh had given him. The names Manasseh ("the one who causes to forget") and Ephraim ("double fruitfulness") are nevertheless Hebrew names with theological significance (cf. Gen 41:51-52), showing that Joseph maintained his Israelite identity despite integration into Egyptian culture.

**The genealogy** (vv. 8-27) mentions a total of 70 persons in Jacob's household. The text specifies that 66 persons came directly from Canaan, "who came out of his loins" (v. 26). With Joseph and his two sons already in Egypt (v. 27), as well as Jacob himself, this makes a total of 70 persons. The number 70 has symbolic meaning in the Bible and represents completeness. The Septuagint (the Greek translation) lists 75 persons; this number, which is also cited in Acts 7:14, may be due to the Septuagint including more of Joseph's descendants.

## Events and Customs

**The night vision at Beersheba** (מַרְאֹת הַלַּיְלָה, v. 2) follows a pattern known from other divine revelations in the Bible: God calls the person by name twice (cf. Gen 22:11; Exod 3:4), and the person answers "Here I am" (הִנֵּנִי). God's promise to "bring you up again" (וְאָנֹכִי אַעַלְךָ גַם־עָלֹה, v. 4) points both to Jacob's burial in Canaan (Gen 50:13) and prophetically to the future exodus from Egypt. The promise that "Joseph shall put his hand upon your eyes" (v. 4) refers to the custom of closing the eyes of the deceased, suggesting that Jacob will die peacefully with his beloved son by his side.

**The reunion scene** (v. 29) describes Joseph as having "harnessed his chariot"—an expression of high Egyptian status, since chariots were a luxury symbol reserved for the elite. That he "fell on his neck" and "wept long" reflects Eastern expressions of strong emotional devotion at reunions.

## Key Terms

**"An abomination to the Egyptians are all shepherds"** (תוֹעֲבַת מִצְרַיִם כָּל־רֹעֵה צֹאן, v. 34) reflects a real cultural tension. The Egyptians, who primarily practiced agriculture along the Nile, had an strained relationship with nomadic shepherds. This "abomination" was likely both religious (some livestock were sacred in Egypt) and social (nomads were considered uncivilized). Joseph strategically used this to ensure that his family would settle separately in Goshen, where they could preserve their lifestyle and identity.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian administrative texts from the New Kingdom (ca. 1550-1070 BC) document that Semitic nomadic groups regularly received permission to enter Egypt with their flocks, especially during drought periods. A well-known text from a border guard (Papyrus Anastasi VI) reports that "Shasu Bedouins" from Edom were allowed to pass through the fortresses at Wadi Tumilat in order to "keep themselves and their flocks alive." This confirms that the story of Jacob's family reflects authentic conditions at the Egyptian border during the Bronze Age.
