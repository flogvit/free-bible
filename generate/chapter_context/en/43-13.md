# Historical and Cultural Context for John 13

## People

**Judas Iscariot** is specifically mentioned as "Judas, son of Simon Iscariot" (Ἰούδας Σίμωνος Ἰσκαριώτου, v. 2, 26). The nickname "Iscariot" has been interpreted in various ways: it may derive from the Hebrew *ish Kerioth* ("man from Kerioth"), a town in Judea, which would make him the only non-Galilean disciple. Others have linked the name to *sicarii* (dagger-men), a group of Jewish rebels, but this interpretation is more uncertain. The fact that Judas was responsible for the money bag (γλωσσόκομον, v. 29) shows he held a trusted position within the group.

**The disciple whom Jesus loved** (v. 23) is introduced here for the first time in the Gospel with this designation. This anonymous figure, traditionally identified as John, was "reclining at Jesus’ side" (ἐν τῷ κόλπῳ)—an honored position at the meal that indicated special closeness to the host.

## Events and Customs

**Foot washing** was a necessary practice in antiquity due to dusty roads and open sandals. Normally, this task was performed by the lowest-ranking slave in the household, often a non-Jewish servant. Jewish slaves were usually exempt from this duty because it was considered especially degrading. When Jesus performs this act, he radically breaks with social conventions—a rabbi and master doing the work of a slave. Peter’s reaction (v. 6–8) reflects the shock at this overturning of hierarchy.

**The meal and reclining posture** are described with the verb ἀνακείμενος (v. 23, 28), indicating that participants were lying down at the table in Greco-Roman style, supported on their left elbow with feet stretched out behind them. This explains how "the disciple whom Jesus loved" could lean against Jesus’ chest and how Jesus could reach the disciples’ feet. This dining custom was adopted from Greek-Roman culture and used on festive occasions.

**Dipping a piece of bread** (τὸ ψωμίον, v. 26) and handing it to a guest was a sign of honor and hospitality at Jewish meals. By giving this to Judas, Jesus highlights the irony and tragedy of the betrayal—he receives an honor token at the very moment his treachery is revealed.

## Key Terms

**"Before the Feast of Passover"** (Πρὸ τῆς ἑορτῆς τοῦ πάσχα, v. 1) places the meal in relation to Passover. John’s chronology appears to differ from that of the Synoptics, where this is the actual Passover meal. In John’s account, Jesus is crucified at the same time the Passover lambs are being slaughtered in the temple, underscoring his role as "the Lamb of God."

**"He who eats my bread has lifted his heel against me"** (v. 18) quotes Psalm 41:10 and uses the image of someone lifting a heel against another—an image of treacherous attack by one whom you trusted, someone who shared your meal.

**"Little children"** (τεκνία, v. 33) is an affectionate term of address used only here by Jesus in the Gospels. The word otherwise appears frequently in 1 John. It marks an intimate tone that characterizes the farewell discourse beginning in this chapter.

**"A new commandment"** (ἐντολὴν καινήν, v. 34)—the command to love one another was not new in itself (cf. Lev 19:18), but Jesus gives it new content by making his own love the standard: "as I have loved you."
