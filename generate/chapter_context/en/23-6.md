# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 6

## People

**King Uzziah (Azariah)** is mentioned as a chronological marker in the opening of the chapter. Uzziah reigned over Judah for about 52 years (c. 790–739 B.C.). His death marked the end of a long period of stability and ushered in a more uncertain political time, with the growing threat of the Assyrian Empire under Tiglath-Pileser III. Dating Isaiah’s call vision to the year of his death underscores this transition.

## Places

**The Temple (הֵיכָל)** in Jerusalem forms the setting for the vision. The temple was the center of Israel’s worship and symbolized God’s presence among His people. The vision may be understood as describing the holy space where the incense altar stood, since a seraph takes a glowing coal from the altar. God’s robe "filling the temple" (שׁוּלָיו מְלֵאִים אֶת־הַהֵיכָל) alludes to how God’s glory filled the tabernacle and the temple at their dedication (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:10–11).

## Events and Customs

**The purification ritual with a glowing coal** reflects well-known cleansing practices in Old Testament worship. Fire was associated with purification and God’s holiness. The altar from which the coal is taken was the place of atonement through sacrifices. When the seraph touches Isaiah’s lips with the coal, it corresponds to his confession that he is "a man with unclean lips"—lips being the instrument of prophetic speech—and this cleansing qualified him for his mission. The action combines elements of priestly purification with a prophetic call.

## Key Terms

**Seraphim (שְׂרָפִים)** appear only in Isaiah 6 as the designation for these specific heavenly beings at God’s throne. The word is derived from the root שׂרף, meaning "to burn." In Numbers 21:6–8, the same word refers to poisonous snakes. Here, the seraphim are described as six-winged beings who serve at God’s throne, cover their faces and feet in reverence, and proclaim God’s holiness.

**Trisagion (קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ)**—the triple proclamation of holiness—is a superlative form in Hebrew expressing absolute holiness. This later became central to both Jewish and Christian liturgy. The phrase "of the Lord of hosts" (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת) portrays God as the cosmic king over heavenly armies.

**"Unclean lips" (טְמֵא־שְׂפָתַיִם)** links ritual impurity to the organ of speech. In a prophetic context, the lips are the instrument for God’s word, and the impurity may refer to the people’s sinful speech, false oaths, or idolatry in which Isaiah shares as a member of the community.
