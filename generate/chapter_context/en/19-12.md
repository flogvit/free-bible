## Key Concepts

### "Sheminit" (הַשְּׁמִינִית)
The heading's instruction "al-hassjeminit" (verse 1) literally means "upon the eighth" and likely refers to a musical instrument tuned to a low register, or to a specific melodic form. The same term appears in Psalm 6 and 1 Chronicles 15:21, where it is linked with lyre playing. This suggests that the psalm was intended for liturgical performance with specific instrumental accompaniment.

### "Flattering Lips" and "Double Heart" (שְׂפַת חֲלָקוֹת / בְּלֵב וָלֵב)
The expression "flattering lips" (verses 3–4) describes speech that is smooth and ingratiating, yet devoid of truth. "Heart and heart" (lev va-lev) is a Hebrew idiom for duplicity—having two hearts, meaning to say one thing while intending another. In wisdom literature and the prophets, such speech was regarded as especially detestable because it undermines the foundation of trust in society.

### Silver Refined Seven Times (כֶּסֶף צָרוּף ... מְזֻקָּק שִׁבְעָתָיִם)
The image in verse 7 of silver refined "seven times" reflects ancient metallurgical practice. Archaeological finds from the Levant show that silver was melted and purified repeatedly in clay crucibles to remove impurities. The number seven symbolizes completeness in Hebrew thought, and the phrase emphasizes that the Lord's words are absolutely pure and reliable—in sharp contrast to human deceitful speech.

## Events and Customs

### Social Oppression and Legal Protection
The psalm's mention of "the ruin of the poor" and "the groaning of the needy" (verse 6) reflects a concrete social reality in ancient Israel. In a culture where oral agreements and testimony were crucial to the legal system, powerful people with "flattering lips" could manipulate court cases and drive the poor from their land and property. Prophets such as Amos and Isaiah condemned this same practice. The psalm gives voice to the powerless and assures that the Lord Himself will intervene as defender for those who have no one to plead their cause.
