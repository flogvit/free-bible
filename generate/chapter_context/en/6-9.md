# Historical and Cultural Context for Joshua 9

## People

The Gibeonites were the inhabitants of Gibeon and three associated cities. They are identified as **Hivvites** (v. 7), one of the seven Canaanite peoples. The Hivvites are mentioned in several places in the Old Testament as a distinct ethnic group in Canaan. The Gibeonites proved to be skilled diplomats who chose survival over military confrontation. Their list of Israelite victories (vv. 9–10) demonstrates that the reputation of God's mighty deeds had spread widely throughout the region—they mention events from Egypt and victories over Sihon and Og, but strategically omit the recent victories at Jericho and Ai to maintain the impression that they came from a distant land.

## Places

**Gibeon** (Hebrew: גִּבְעוֹן) was located about 10 kilometers northwest of Jerusalem, identified with modern-day El-Jib. Archaeological excavations have revealed a significant Bronze Age settlement with an impressive water system. Gibeon was a large city, described in Joshua 10:2 as "one of the royal cities" and "larger than Ai". The four cities in the Gibeonite alliance—**Gibeon**, **Kephirah**, **Beeroth**, and **Kirjath-jearim** (v. 17)—formed a group in the central highlands and controlled important trade routes. **Gilgal** (v. 6), where the Israelite camp was located, lay in the Jordan Valley near Jericho and served as the starting point for the conquest.

## Events and Customs

The chapter illustrates the binding power of **oath-taking** (Hebrew: שָׁבַע) in the ancient Near East. When the leaders had sworn "by the Lord, the God of Israel" (vv. 18–19), the oath was unbreakable—even though it was made under false pretenses. Breaking such an oath would bring God's wrath upon the people (v. 20). This understanding of the sanctity of oaths was widespread throughout the ancient Near East.

The expression "hewers of wood and drawers of water" (חֹטְבֵי עֵצִים וְשֹׁאֲבֵי־מַיִם) describes the lowest servant tasks in society. Both duties were physically demanding and typically performed by slaves or members of the lowest social classes. That the Gibeonites were assigned these roles "for the altar of the Lord" (v. 27) meant they became temple servants—a position later formalized as "Nethinim" (temple slaves) in post-exilic times.

## Key Terms

The word **בְּרִית** (berit, "covenant/alliance") is central to the chapter and appears seven times. In an ancient Near Eastern context, a berit was a formal, binding agreement often made between different parties with unequal power positions. The Gibeonites' phrasing "we are your servants" (עֲבָדֶיךָ אֲנָחְנוּ, v. 8) signaled their willingness to submit in exchange for protection—a typical vassal covenant formulation.

The expression **"they did not inquire of the Lord"** (וְאֶת־פִּי יְהוָה לֹא שָׁאָלוּ, v. 14) likely refers to seeking an oracle through the high priest, either by means of the Urim and Thummim or some other form of divine guidance. This stands in contrast to practices described elsewhere in the book of Joshua and marks a leadership error.
