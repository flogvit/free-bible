# Historical and Cultural Context for Daniel 11

## People

Daniel 11 contains a detailed prophetic description of historical figures. **The three Persian kings after Cyrus, plus a fourth** (v. 2) are traditionally identified as Cambyses II, Darius I, and Xerxes I (Ahasuerus), the latter being the "fourth" who "stirs up all against the kingdom of Greece"—a reference to the Persian wars against Greece (490–479 B.C.).

**The mighty king** (v. 3) is Alexander the Great, whose empire was divided among four of his generals after his death in 323 B.C. (v. 4). **The king of the South** consistently refers to the Ptolemaic dynasty in Egypt, while **the king of the North** denotes the Seleucid dynasty in Syria. A significant portion of the chapter (vv. 21–35) describes Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164 B.C.), who is called "despicable" (נִבְזֶה, *nivzeh*) and who seized power through "flattering words" (חֲלַקְלַקּוֹת, *chalaklakkot*).

## Places

**"The beautiful land"** (אֶרֶץ־הַצְּבִי, *erets ha-tsevi*, vv. 16, 41) is a poetic designation for the land of Israel, which lay geographically between the two great powers and thus became a strategically vital area in their conflicts. The expression underscores the land's special status in God's eyes.

**"The sanctuary, the fortress"** (הַמִּקְדָּשׁ הַמָּעוֹז, v. 31) refers to the temple in Jerusalem, which was desecrated by Antiochus IV in 167 B.C. The geographical references to **Edom, Moab, and Ammon** (v. 41)—areas east of the Jordan—as well as **Egypt, Libya, and Cush** (vv. 42–43) show the scope of the military expansion described.

## Events and Customs

A central event described in the chapter is **the desecration of the temple** (v. 31): "They shall profane the sanctuary, the fortress; they shall take away the regular burnt offering and set up the abomination that makes desolate." This corresponds with 1 Maccabees 1:54, which describes how Antiochus IV erected a pagan altar over the burnt offering altar—identified with "the abomination that makes desolate" (הַשִּׁקּוּץ מְשׁוֹמֵם, *ha-shikkuts meshomem*). The expression likely involves a wordplay on "Ba'al Shamem" (lord of heaven), a Syrian deity.

**The regular burnt offering** (הַתָּמִיד, *ha-tamid*) was the continuous morning and evening sacrifice that formed the core of temple service. Its abolition represented a direct attack on Jewish worship. The restoration of this offering was later celebrated by the Jews through the festival of Hanukkah.

## Key Terms

The term **"the prince of the covenant"** (נְגִיד בְּרִית, *negid berit*, v. 22) has been subject to various interpretations—either as a reference to the deposed high priest Onias III, or as a messianic reference. The expression **"the holy covenant"** (בְּרִית קֹדֶשׁ, vv. 28, 30) denotes God's covenant with Israel, against which the hostility of the king of the North is directed.

**"The wise"** (מַשְׂכִּילִים, *maskilim*, vv. 33, 35) is an important designation for the faithful who instruct the people during persecution. The word can also be translated as "those who have insight" and becomes central to understanding those who chose martyrdom over apostasy.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The richness of detail in Daniel 11:5–35 corresponds remarkably with Hellenistic historiography, particularly Polybius's *Histories* and various Ptolemaic and Seleucid sources. **The Rosetta Stone** (196 B.C.) and other Ptolemaic inscriptions confirm several of the political alliances and marriages described, including the Berenice episode (v. 6).

**Babylonian astronomical diaries** from the Seleucid period provide independent dates for several of the military campaigns described. Archaeological finds from **Tel Dan** and other sites in Israel show traces of the military conflicts between Ptolemies and Seleucids over control of "the beautiful land."
