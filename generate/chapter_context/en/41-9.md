# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 9

## People

**Moses and Elijah** appear in the transfiguration on the mountain (v. 4) as representatives of, respectively, the Law and the Prophets—the two main divisions of the Hebrew Bible. Both had special theophanies (divine revelations) on mountains: Moses on Sinai and Elijah on Horeb. Both also had unusual endings to their earthly lives—Moses' burial place was unknown, and Elijah was taken up into heaven. In Jewish tradition, there were expectations concerning the return of both in the last days, especially Elijah (Mal 4:5).

**The father of the possessed boy** (vv. 17–29) represents a type common in antiquity: parents seeking healers and exorcists for their sick children. His cry, "I believe; help my unbelief!" (v. 24), has become a classic expression of the struggle of faith.

## Places

**The high mountain** (v. 2) is traditionally identified with Mount Tabor, although the text does not name it. Mount Hermon, located closer to Caesarea Philippi (the context from chapter 8), is geographically more plausible. Mountains held deep symbolic meaning in Jewish tradition as places of divine revelation—Sinai, Horeb, Carmel, and Zion were all linked with important events between God and his people.

**Capernaum** (v. 33) was Jesus' operational base in Galilee. Archaeological excavations have uncovered a synagogue from the fourth century built over an earlier structure, as well as what is traditionally identified as Peter's house. The city was a fishing village community on the Sea of Galilee with an estimated population of 1,500.

## Events and Customs

**The Transfiguration** (vv. 2–8) contains several elements that would have evoked strong associations for Jewish readers: the dazzling white clothing recalls heavenly beings in apocalyptic literature; the cloud represents God's presence (as with the tabernacle and temple); and the voice from heaven confirms Jesus' identity using language echoing Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1. Peter's suggestion to build three shelters (σκηνάς, *skēnas*) may be linked to the Feast of Tabernacles, where Israelites lived in temporary dwellings.

**The exorcism of the mute spirit** (vv. 17–29) describes symptoms that in antiquity were understood as demonic possession but which modern readers recognize as resembling epilepsy. In the ancient world, the boundary between illness and demonic activity was fluid. Exorcism was widespread in both Jewish and Greco-Roman culture, but Jesus' method—without incantations or magical formulas, solely by his word—was distinct.

**The discussion about who is greatest** (vv. 33–37) reflects the strong honor-and-shame culture of the Mediterranean region. Social ranking was fundamentally important, and even among disciples, questions about position were natural. Jesus' use of a child as an example was radical: children had minimal social status in antiquity and represented the opposite of honor and power.

## Key Terms

**Μετεμορφώθη** (*metemorphōthē*, "was transformed," v. 2) describes a visible change in Jesus' outward appearance. The word is also used in Romans 12:2 and 2 Corinthians 3:18 regarding believers being transformed. The Greek word has given rise to the term "metamorphosis."

**Γέεννα** (*geenna*, "Gehenna," vv. 43, 45, 47) refers to the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, a place with a dark history ranging from child sacrifice to Moloch (2 Kgs 23:10). In intertestamental times, this location developed into an image of final punishment. The expression "where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched" (vv. 44, 46, 48) is drawn from Isaiah 66:24.

**Μύλος ὀνικός** (*mylos onikos*, "a millstone turned by a donkey," v. 42) refers to the large type of millstone that required a donkey to turn it, as opposed to smaller hand mills. Such a stone around one's neck would guarantee drowning—an image illustrating the seriousness of leading others astray.

**Ἅλας** (*halas*, "salt," vv. 49–50) had many functions in antiquity: preservation, cleansing, flavoring, and sacrificial use. Salt was also associated with covenants ("covenant of salt," Num 18:19). Jesus' words about salt losing its strength play on the fact that salt from the Dead Sea region could become contaminated and thus useless.
