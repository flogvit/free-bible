## People

### Eliphaz the Temanite
Eliphaz is the first of Job’s friends to speak in this chapter, which likely indicates that he was the oldest or most respected among them. The name "Eliphaz" (אֱלִיפַז) means "my God is pure gold" or "God is victorious." Teman was a region in Edom (southeast of the Dead Sea), known in ancient times for its wise men (Jer 49:7; Obad 8–9). Eliphaz’s origin from Teman gives him authority as a representative of the Edomite wisdom tradition, which was highly regarded in the ancient Near East.

## Key Concepts

### Vision Terminology (vv. 12–16)
Eliphaz describes a nighttime revelation using several significant Hebrew terms. "Tardema" (תַּרְדֵּמָה) in verse 13 refers to a deep sleep, the same word used for Adam’s sleep in Gen 2:21 and for Abram’s sleep in Gen 15:12. "Shemets" (שֵׁמֶץ) in verse 12 means a "whisper" or "faint sound"—the revelation came not with power, but subtly. "Temuna" (תְּמוּנָה) in verse 16 refers to a form or shape that could not be clearly identified. This description follows a pattern known from Mesopotamian dream revelations, where divine messages often came in the stillness of night.

### Lion Imagery (vv. 10–11)
The chapter uses five different Hebrew words for lion: "aryeh" (אַרְיֵה), "shachal" (שָׁחַל), "kefir" (כְּפִיר), "layish" (לַיִשׁ) and "lavi" (לָבִיא). This rich terminology reflects the lion’s central place in Near Eastern imagery as a symbol of power and strength. The fact that even mighty lions can lose their teeth and die of hunger illustrates Eliphaz’s point that no one, regardless of strength, escapes the consequences of wickedness.

### "House of Clay" (v. 19)
The expression "botnei-chomer" (בָֽתֵּי־חֹמֶר)—"house of clay"—is a poetic description of the human body. This image has parallels in Mesopotamian literature, where humans were said to be created from clay. It underscores human frailty and mortality in comparison with the divine.

## Events and Customs

### Night Vision (vv. 12–16)
Eliphaz recounts a nighttime revelation in which a word was "stolen" to him (v. 12), an unusual way of describing divine communication. In his deep sleep (tardema), he sees a form he cannot identify and hears a quiet voice. This type of night vision was known in the ancient Near East as a means by which God could communicate, and Eliphaz uses this experience to lend authority to his speech.

### Wisdom Teaching and Exhortation
Eliphaz refers to Job’s former role as counselor and comforter for others (vv. 3–4). This reflects the ancient custom whereby wise men served as advisors in society, strengthening the weak and offering guidance to those who struggled. The fact that Job himself had fulfilled this role makes Eliphaz’s challenge all the more pointed: can Job now receive the same kind of exhortation he once gave to others?
