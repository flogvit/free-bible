# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 4

## People

**Annas and Caiaphas** are mentioned as high priests in verse 6, reflecting the complex political situation. Formally, Caiaphas was the high priest (AD 18–36), but his father-in-law Annas (high priest AD 6–15) retained significant influence after the Romans removed him from office. Josephus confirms that the family of Annas dominated the high priesthood for several decades. John and Alexander, both from a priestly lineage, are otherwise unknown in historical sources. Some scholars have identified John with Jonathan, son of Annas, who later became high priest. Alexander’s Greek name indicates he likely belonged to the Hellenized Jewish elite.

**Barnabas** is introduced in verses 36–37 by his given name, Joseph. He was a Levite from Cyprus, indicating that he belonged to the Diaspora Judaism. The nickname "Barnabas" is explained as "son of encouragement/comfort" (υἱὸς παρακλήσεως) – παράκλησις can mean both comfort, exhortation, and encouragement. Traditionally, Levites were not permitted to own land in Israel (Numbers 18:20), but this prohibition was not strictly enforced in the Diaspora or during the late Second Temple period.

## Places

**The Captain of the Temple’s jurisdiction** (ὁ στρατηγὸς τοῦ ἱεροῦ) covered the temple area where the arrest took place. This official was the second-highest religious authority after the high priest and was responsible for order and security within the temple precincts. He commanded the Levitical guards of the temple and had the authority to arrest anyone who disturbed the peace of the temple.

**The Sanhedrin/Council** (συνέδριον, verse 15) was the Jewish council that met in or near the temple area. Archaeological excavations have identified possible meeting places, including structures on the western side of the Temple Mount. The council consisted of high priests, elders, and scribes – all three groups are mentioned in verse 5.

## Events and Customs

**Overnight detention** (verse 3) was necessary because Jewish law forbade trials after sunset. The phrase "for it was already evening" (ἦν γὰρ ἑσπέρα ἤδη) shows that Luke is familiar with this practice. The Mishnah confirms that the Sanhedrin could not judge capital cases at night, and interrogations had to wait until the next day.

**Common ownership** (verses 32–35) among believers has parallels in several contemporary movements. The Qumran community practiced a similar economic fellowship, where new members surrendered their property to the community. The Greco-Roman world also knew the ideal of friendship in which "all things were common" (πάντα κοινά) – an expression used by both Plato and Pythagorean philosophers.

## Key Terms

**Parrhesia** (boldness/openness) appears several times in the chapter (verses 13, 29, 31) and is a central concept. In the Greek political context, it denoted a citizen’s right to free speech in the assembly. The council’s amazement that "uneducated and ordinary men" (ἀγράμματοί ... καὶ ἰδιῶται) spoke with such boldness reflects that this right traditionally belonged to educated citizens.

**The cornerstone** (κεφαλὴ γωνίας, verse 11), which Peter quotes from Psalm 118:22, was a technical architectural term. Archaeological finds of monumental stones from the temple wall in Jerusalem – some weighing over 500 tons – illustrate the importance of cornerstones in monumental architecture. The expression was used metaphorically in Jewish tradition to refer to a rejected person or thing that becomes decisive.
