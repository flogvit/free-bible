## Key Concepts

The chapter contains several Greek words and expressions with special significance. The word **ταπεινοφροσύνη** (*tapeinofrosyne*, "humility") in verse 2 was generally negative in Greco-Roman culture—it denoted a slave mentality or lack of self-respect. Paul radically redefines the term as a Christian virtue. Similarly, **πραΰτης** (*praytes*, "gentleness") was often despised as weakness in antiquity but is highlighted here as Christlike character.

The expression **ἓν βάπτισμα** ("one baptism") in verse 5 points to baptism's role as an initiation rite in the early congregations. In contrast to the mystery cults of the region, which had graded initiations, Paul emphasizes that there is only one baptism that unites all believers. The term **κυβεία** (*kybeia*, "dice-playing") in verse 14 comes from gambling language and denotes cheating and deception—a vivid picture of false teaching.

## Events and Customs

Verses 8–10 contain a quotation from Psalm 68:19, but Paul follows a Jewish interpretive method (midrash) by adapting the text christologically. The original psalm says that God "received gifts," but Paul writes "gave gifts." This change reflects an early Jewish synagogue tradition that linked the psalm to Moses' ascent on Sinai to receive and then give the Torah to the people. Paul transfers this to Christ.

The exhortations in verses 25–32 to put away lying, stealing, and corrupt speech mirror the so-called "vice lists" common in both Jewish and Greco-Roman moral instruction. These concrete exhortations were especially relevant in an urban context marked by trade and religious pluralism. The contrast with "working with one's own hands" (v. 28) marked a break with certain ancient attitudes that looked down on manual labor.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The list of leadership gifts in verse 11—apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds, and teachers—provides unique insight into the structure of the early congregation. Inscriptions from the first and second centuries in Asia Minor confirm that the terms **ποιμήν** ("shepherd") and **διδάσκαλος** ("teacher") were used for congregational leaders in the region. The Didache (an early Christian writing, often dated to the end of the first or beginning of the second century) mentions similar categories, confirming that these were established functions in congregations around the Mediterranean.
