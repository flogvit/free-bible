# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 7

## People

**Hiram of Tyre** (חִירָם מִצֹּר) was the craftsman whom Solomon brought to carry out the bronze work for the temple (v. 13-14). He must not be confused with King Hiram of Tyre, who supplied materials. This Hiram was the son of a widow from the tribe of Naphtali and a bronze worker from Tyre (אִישׁ־צֹרִי חֹרֵשׁ נְחֹשֶׁת). The description of him as filled with "wisdom, understanding, and knowledge" (חָכְמָה, תְּבוּנָה, דַּעַת) uses the same terminology as for Bezalel, the craftsman behind the tabernacle (Exod 31:3). The Phoenicians were renowned in antiquity for their metallurgical expertise, and the combination of Israelite and Phoenician heritage made Hiram especially qualified.

## Places

**The House of the Forest of Lebanon** (בֵּית יַעַר הַלְּבָנוֹן) was a large building measuring 100 x 50 x 30 cubits (about 45 x 23 x 14 meters), which served as an armory and treasury (v. 2-5). The name likely came from the many cedar columns that reminded people of a forest in Lebanon. **The Jordan Valley between Succoth and Zarethan** (v. 46) was the area where the king had the bronze work cast in clay-rich soil (בְּמַעֲבֵה הָאֲדָמָה). This region had soil suitable for casting molds, and archaeological surveys have confirmed metallurgical activity in this region during the Iron Age.

## Key Terms

**Jachin and Boaz** (יָכִין, בֹּעַז) were the names of the two bronze pillars at the temple entrance (v. 21). Jachin means "he will establish" and Boaz may mean "in him is strength". Such named pillars at temple entrances are known from other Phoenician and Canaanite temples. They likely had symbolic meaning connected to God’s eternal covenant with the Davidic dynasty.

**The Bronze Sea** (הַיָּם מוּצָק) was an enormous cast bronze basin about 4.5 meters in diameter that held 2,000 baths, approximately 44,000 liters of water (v. 23-26). The twelve oxen that carried it probably represented the twelve tribes of Israel. Large water basins are known from other Near Eastern temples and symbolized cosmic order and life-giving water.

**Mekhonot** (מְכֹנוֹת) – the ten mobile carts with bronze basins (v. 27-39) – were elaborate constructions with wheels, decorated with cherubim, lions, and palm motifs (v. 29, 36). Similar cultic carts have been found in archaeological excavations on Cyprus from the same period.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological finds from Megiddo, Hazor, and other places have revealed "prototypes" of cultic stands and basins from the Iron Age that resemble the descriptions in this chapter. A bronze basin stand from Cyprus (about 1200-1100 BCE) with wheels and decorations of lions and cherubim confirms that such objects were common in Eastern Mediterranean culture. The pomegranate motif (רִמֹּנִים) that adorned the pillars is also confirmed through numerous finds of pomegranate jewelry and decorations from Israelite contexts.
