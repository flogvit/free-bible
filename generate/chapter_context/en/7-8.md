# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 8

## People

**Zebah and Zalmunna** are the two Midianite kings whom Gideon pursues and ultimately kills. Their names carry symbolic meaning in Hebrew: Zebah (זֶבַח) means "sacrifice" or "slaughter offering," and Zalmunna (צַלְמֻנָּע) may mean "shadow is withheld." These kings represented the highest political and military leadership among the Midianites. The fact that they had killed Gideon's brothers at Tabor (vv. 18–19) gave Gideon a personal motive for vengeance in addition to his national mission.

**Jether**, Gideon's firstborn son, is mentioned only here in the Bible. His hesitation to kill the kings (v. 20) reflects both his young age and the contemporary belief that it was honorable to be killed by a worthy warrior—a sentiment expressed by the Midianite kings themselves when they asked Gideon to carry out the execution (v. 21).

## Places

**Succoth** and **Penuel** were Israelite towns east of the Jordan, in the territory of the tribe of Gad. Succoth was located in the Jordan Valley near the confluence of the Jabbok and Jordan rivers, while Penuel lay farther east along the Jabbok River—the place where Jacob wrestled with God (Gen 32). Both cities refused to provide Gideon with supplies, likely out of fear of Midianite retaliation if Gideon failed. The tower of Penuel (vv. 9, 17) was probably a fortified structure that served as a refuge and defensive stronghold.

**Karkor** (v. 10) was a location in the Transjordanian desert region, possibly in the Wadi Sirhan area. "The way of those who dwell in tents" (v. 11) refers to a trade route through the territory of nomads east of the settled regions.

## Events and Customs

**Vengeance for Blood and Brother-Killing**: Gideon's interrogation of Zebah and Zalmunna regarding the killings at Tabor (vv. 18–19) reveals that the conflict had a personal dimension. In ancient Near Eastern culture, blood vengeance was a sacred duty owed by the nearest male relative. The kings' response that the victims resembled "sons of kings" may have been both a compliment to Gideon's family and an acknowledgment of their high status.

**The Offer of Kingship** (vv. 22–23) represents a turning point in Israelite history. The people desired a hereditary dynasty modeled after Egypt or Canaan, but Gideon rejected this with the theologically sound reasoning that the Lord should rule over Israel. This episode anticipates the later debate about kingship in 1 Samuel 8.

**The Punishment of Succoth and Penuel**: Gideon punished the elders of Succoth by "teaching them" with thorns and briers from the wilderness (v. 16)—likely a form of flogging or torture. The tower at Penuel was demolished, and its inhabitants were killed (v. 17). These severe punishments reflect the brutal justice of wartime and the importance of tribal loyalty.

## Key Terms

**Ephod** (אֵפוֹד, v. 27): Normally a priestly garment used in worship, but Gideon's ephod appears to have been a cultic object made of gold—possibly some kind of oracle device. Its weight of 1,700 shekels of gold (about 20 kg) suggests it was a substantial object. The fact that Israel "whored after" the ephod indicates that it became an object of idolatrous worship.

**Crescents/Moon Ornaments** (שַׂהֲרֹנִים, vv. 21, 26): Ornamental jewelry shaped like crescent moons, worn by both the camels and the kings. These were associated with moon worship, which was widespread among Semitic nomadic peoples, including the Midianites and Ishmaelites. The use of such symbols underscores the pagan character of Israel's enemies.

**Ishmaelites** (v. 24): Here the Midianites are called Ishmaelites, possibly reflecting that these terms were used interchangeably for nomadic traders in the region. Both groups descended from Abraham—the Ishmaelites through Hagar and the Midianites through Keturah.

**Baal-Berith** (בַּעַל בְּרִית, v. 33): Means "Lord of the Covenant" or "Covenant Baal." This was a Canaanite deity especially worshiped in Shechem, where remains of a large temple have been found. The name suggests a god who guaranteed covenants and agreements—a distorted parallel to Israel's covenant relationship with the Lord.
