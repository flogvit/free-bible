# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 36

## People

The chapter presents Esau's genealogy and establishes him as the ancestor of the Edomites. His three wives represent various alliances: Ada was the daughter of Elon the Hittite, Oholibama was the daughter of Anah, son of Zibeon the Hivite, while Basemath was the daughter of Ishmael and sister of Nebaioth. These marriages show how Esau connected with Canaanite and Ishmaelite groups. Timna, who was a concubine (pilegesh) to Eliphaz, bore Amalek—the ancestor of the Amalekites, who later became bitter enemies of Israel (Exodus 17:8-16).

## Places

Mount Se'ir (הַר שֵׂעִיר) is the central geographical area in the chapter. This mountain region lies southeast of the Dead Sea and extends down toward the Gulf of Aqaba. The name "Se'ir" means "hairy" or "rugged," describing the uneven terrain. Before the Edomites took over the area, the Horites lived there (v. 20). Bozrah (בָּצְרָה, v. 33) was an important Edomite city and is mentioned by several prophets (Isaiah 34:6; Jeremiah 49:13). The city is identified with modern Buseira in Jordan.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes how Esau and Jacob separated because the land could not support both of their wealth (vv. 6-7), a pattern that repeats Abraham's and Lot's separation (Genesis 13:5-12). The list of kings in verses 31-39 is remarkable because it shows a non-dynastic kingdom—each king comes from a different city and family, suggesting an elective monarchy or power shifts based on conquest, unlike the later dynasties in Israel and Judah.

## Key Terms

The title **"alluf"** (אַלּוּף) is often translated as "chieftain" or "prince" and denotes a clan or tribal leader. The term is used consistently throughout the chapter to describe Edomite leadership. **The Horites** (הַחֹרִי) were the original inhabitants of Se'ir before the Edomites. Earlier they were identified with "cave dwellers" (from Hebrew "hor" = cave), but modern research often links them to the Hurrians known from Mesopotamian sources, although this identification is debated. The rare word **"hajemim"** (הַיֵּמִם) in verse 24, which describes Anah's discovery in the wilderness, is uncertain—it may mean hot springs, mules, or water.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The list of kings in Genesis 36 represents an early tradition about the Edomite kingdom "before any king reigned over the Israelites" (v. 31), a formulation indicating that the text was written after Israel had gained kings. Archaeological findings from copper mining operations in the Timna Valley and Feinan show significant activity in the region during this period.
