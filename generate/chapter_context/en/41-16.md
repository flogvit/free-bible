# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 16

## People

The chapter highlights three women as witnesses to the empty tomb: Mary Magdalene, Mary (the mother of James), and Salome. That women were the first witnesses is remarkable in a culture where women's testimony was generally given less weight. Mary Magdalene is identified in verse 9 as the one from whom Jesus had cast out seven demons, showing her special connection to Jesus. Salome is mentioned by name only in the Gospel of Mark (here and in 15:40). Tradition often identifies her with "the mother of the sons of Zebedee" (Matt 27:56), that is, the mother of the disciples James and John, but this is not certain.

The "young man" (νεανίσκον) dressed in a white robe (v. 5) is not explicitly described as an angel, but his white garment and the message he brings signal a heavenly messenger. White clothing was associated with heavenly purity and divine presence in Jewish tradition.

## Places

The tomb (μνημεῖον) described was typical of wealthy Jewish burials in the first century: a rock-cut tomb with a large rolling stone in front of the entrance. Such tombs have been found in great numbers around Jerusalem. The stone (λίθος) rolled in front of the entrance could weigh several hundred kilograms and required considerable strength to move.

Galilee is mentioned as the place where they are to meet the risen Jesus (v. 7). This points back to Jesus' ministry in Galilee and forward to a new meeting with the disciples in their home region, away from Jerusalem where the crucifixion took place.

## Events and Customs

Anointing the dead body with fragrant spices and ointments (ἀρώματα) was an important Jewish burial custom. The purpose was partly to show respect for the deceased and partly to reduce the odor of death. That the women came "when the Sabbath was over" (διαγενομένου τοῦ σαββάτου) shows that they waited until the Sabbath rest had ended at sunset on Saturday evening before buying the spices. They arrived at the tomb at sunrise on Sunday morning—the first day of the Jewish week.

The expression "the first day of the week" (τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων) later became the basis for Christian Sunday celebration as the day of resurrection.

## Key Terms

The verb "he has risen" (ἠγέρθη) is in the passive form, which theologically suggests that God is the actor—God raised Jesus from the dead. This is the core message of early Christian preaching.

The women's reaction is described with two strong words: "trembling" (τρόμος) and "astonishment" (ἔκστασις). This combination expresses the overwhelming experience of standing before something divine and unexpected. Mark's original ending (v. 8) with the words "for they were afraid" (ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ) is unusually abrupt and has occupied biblical scholars for centuries.

## Chapter-Specific Textual Critical Issues

Mark 16 contains one of the most well-known textual critical problems in the New Testament. The oldest and most reliable manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus from the fourth century) end the gospel at verse 8. Verses 9–20, known as "the longer ending," are not found in these manuscripts but were added later and appear in younger manuscripts. There is also a "shorter ending" preserved in some manuscripts. This shows that early copyists regarded the ending at verse 8 as problematic and sought to supplement the text with resurrection accounts that harmonized with the other gospels.
