# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 57

## Events and Customs

Chapter 57 contains a detailed description of Canaanite-inspired idolatry that had infiltrated Israel's religious practice. The prophet condemns several specific rituals: worship "under every green tree" (v. 5) was a widespread Canaanite practice where sacred groves served as cultic gathering places for fertility rites. The shocking reference to "slaying children in the valleys, under the clefts of the rocks" (v. 5, שֹׁחֲטֵי הַיְלָדִים בַּנְּחָלִים) points to child sacrifice, likely to the god Molech, a practice confirmed by archaeological finds from Phoenicia and Carthage (tophet sanctuaries) as widespread in the region.

The verses concerning "smooth stones of the brook" (v. 6) contain a Hebrew wordplay on "cheleq" (חֵלֶק, portion/share) and "chalaq" (חָלָק, smooth). The smooth stones in the valley are ironically called the people's "portion"—in contrast to the Lord, who should have been their true inheritance. Drink offerings and grain offerings made to these stones reveal a syncretistic religion. The "high places" mentioned in verse 7 were bamot—elevated cult sites on mountain tops where Canaanite worship traditionally took place, often involving sexual-religious rituals that the prophet describes using metaphors of adultery.

Verse 9 describes how the people "went to the king with oil" (וַתָּשֻׁרִי לַמֶּלֶךְ בַּשֶּׁמֶן) and sent messengers "far away." This may refer to Molech worship (Hebrew "melek" = king) or to political alliances with foreign superpowers—both forms of unfaithfulness toward the Lord.

## Key Terms

The word "tsaddiq" (צַדִּיק, "the righteous") in verses 1–2 refers to the godly who die and thus escape experiencing coming disasters—a concept reflecting a view of death as protection rather than punishment. The expression "shalom shalom" (שָׁלוֹם שָׁלוֹם, "peace, peace") in verse 19 is an emphatic doubling denoting complete, comprehensive peace—for both "the one far off" (Gentile nations or exiles) and "the one near" (those who remained in the land).

The Hebrew expression "dakka" (דַּכָּא, "the crushed") and "shefal-ruach" (שְׁפַל־רוּחַ, "the lowly in spirit") in verse 15 describes people humbled through suffering. The fact that God "dwells" with such people alongside His transcendent majesty represents a remarkable theological tension between God's unapproachable holiness and His closeness to the suffering.

## Places

References to "valleys" (נְחָלִים, nachalim) and "clefts of the rocks" (vv. 5–6) as cultic sites may point toward the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, known for child sacrifice, but the text uses a general term that could encompass multiple locations where such practices occurred. The Valley of Hinnom later became so associated with evil that the Hebrew "Ge-Hinnom" became the Greek "Gehenna"—hell. "My holy mountain" (הַר־קָדְשִׁי, v. 13) refers to Zion/the temple hill as the legitimate place of worship in contrast to the illegitimate high places.
