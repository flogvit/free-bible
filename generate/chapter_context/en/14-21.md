# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 21

## People

**Jehoram (Joram, יְהוֹרָם)** became king of Judah after his father Jehoshaphat at the age of thirty-two and reigned for eight years (vv. 5, 20). His marriage to a daughter of Ahab from the northern kingdom (v. 6) led to the introduction of Baal worship in Judah. The murder of all his brothers upon taking power (v. 4)—named Azariah, Jehiel, Zechariah, Azariahu, Michael, and Shephatiah (v. 2)—was a brutal practice known from several ancient cultures to eliminate potential rivals for the throne, but stood in sharp contrast to his father Jehoshaphat’s peaceful distribution of inheritance (v. 3).

**Elijah (אֵלִיָּהוּ הַנָּבִיא)** is mentioned as the sender of a written message (מִכְתָּב) to Jehoram (vv. 12–15). This is the only place in the Bible where Elijah delivers a prophetic message in writing. The letter contains judgment against Jehoram for his idolatry and fratricide.

**Jehoahaz (יְהוֹאָחָז)** is mentioned in v. 17 as Jehoram’s youngest son and the only one who survived the Philistine and Arab attack. He is identical with Ahaziah, who later became king (chapter 22).

## Places

**Edom** was an area southeast of the Dead Sea that had been under Judah’s control since David’s time. Edom’s rebellion (vv. 8–10) marked a significant weakening of Judah’s power and had economic consequences as trade routes toward the Red Sea were threatened.

**Libnah (לִבְנָה)** was a Levitical city in the lowlands of Judah near the Philistine border. The fact that this city also rebelled at the same time (v. 10) shows that Jehoram’s religious apostasy created internal division. The text links the rebellion directly to Jehoram having "forsaken YHWH, the God of his fathers."

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the establishment of "high places" (בָּמוֹת, *bamot*) in the mountains of Judah (v. 11). These high places were cult sites often associated with Canaanite idolatry. The expressions וַיֶּזֶן (v. 11, "caused to commit adultery") and וַתַּזְנֶה (v. 13) use marital unfaithfulness as a metaphor for spiritual infidelity toward YHWH—a common prophetic imagery.

**The Philistine and Arab attack** (vv. 16–17) is described as God’s judgment in that He "stirred up" (וַיָּעַר) the spirit of the enemy against Jehoram. The attackers took everything found in the royal house, including Jehoram’s sons and wives, except for his youngest son Jehoahaz.

The fact that the people did not light a "fire" (שְׂרֵפָה) for Jehoram at his death (v. 19) refers to the honor custom of burning incense and fragrant herbs at royal funerals, not cremation. This refusal, together with the fact that he was not buried in the kings’ tombs (v. 20), was a public condemnation of his reign.

## Key Terms

The word **נִיר** (*nir*, "lamp/light," v. 7) is used figuratively regarding the continuation of the Davidic dynasty. A burning lamp symbolized a house’s existence and hope. God’s promise to preserve "a light" for David meant that the dynasty would not be completely wiped out, despite the sins of individual kings—this explains why God did not destroy Judah during Jehoram’s time.

The expression **בְּלֹא חֶמְדָּה** (v. 20, "without anyone longing for him" or "without honor") summarizes Jehoram’s legacy as a despised king.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Edom’s secession from Judah during this period corresponds with archaeological findings showing increased Edomite activity and urbanization in the 8th century BCE. Inscriptions and pottery finds indicate that Edom developed into an independent kingdom precisely at this time, confirming the biblical account of the rebellion.
