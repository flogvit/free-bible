## People

**Gideon (גִּדְעוֹן)** is introduced as the son of Joash from the Abiezrite clan in the tribe of Manasseh. His name likely means "hewer" (from the root גדע), which becomes prophetic since he later cuts down the Asherah pole. He is first presented as a fearful man threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it from the Midianites. His alternative name **Jerubbaal (יְרֻבַּעַל)**, meaning "let Baal contend against him," is given after he tears down the altar of Baal. This double name reflects the transition from a timid farmer to a judge who challenges Canaanite religion.

**Joash (יוֹאָשׁ)** was Gideon's father and owned an altar of Baal with an Asherah pole. Despite his connection to the cult of Baal, he defended his son with a theological argument: if Baal truly is god, he can defend himself. This suggests that syncretism in Israel was pragmatic rather than deeply convinced belief.

## Places

**Ophrah (עָפְרָה)** is identified as the home of the Abiezrite clan and is likely located in the western region of Manasseh. The place must not be confused with Ophrah in Benjamin (Josh 18:23). The sacred oak (אֵלָה) where the angel of the Lord appeared was typical of Canaanite shrines, but here the site is transformed into a legitimate place of worship for Yahweh through the altar Gideon builds.

**The Valley of Jezreel (עֵמֶק יִזְרְעֶאל)** is mentioned as the place where the enemy army gathered (v. 33). This fertile plain between Mount Gilboa and the hill country of Carmel was strategically important and a natural invasion route.

## Events and Customs

**Midianite raid tactics** are described in detail in the chapter. The Midianites, together with the Amalekites and "the people of the East," used camels for mobility – an early example of large-scale nomadic warfare with camels. The Israelites had to hide in mountain caves and strongholds (מִנְהָרוֹת, מְעָרוֹת, מְצָדוֹת), which testifies to a systematic pattern of plundering where attackers destroyed crops and livestock.

**The offering under the oak** (vv. 18-21) follows a pattern known from theophany accounts: food is prepared, placed on a rock, and consumed by divine fire. This resembles Manoah's experience (Judg 13:19-20) and serves as confirmation of divine presence. The meal with a young goat, unleavened bread, and meat broth represented significant resources during a time of famine.

**The destruction of the altar of Baal** was a provocative act that could have resulted in the death penalty according to local custom. That Gideon carried this out at night with ten servants shows both obedience and fear. Using the wood from the Asherah pole as fuel for the burnt offering was a symbolic humiliation of the Canaanite cult.

## Key Terms

**Asherah (אֲשֵׁרָה)** here refers to a wooden pillar or carved cult object representing the fertility goddess Asherah, Baal's consort in Canaanite religion. Such pillars were often placed beside altars and were forbidden in Israelite law (Deut 16:21).

**"The Spirit of the Lord clothed Gideon" (וְר֣וּחַ יְהוָ֔ה לָבְשָׁ֖ה אֶת־גִּדְע֑וֹן)** uses the verb "lāḇaš" (to clothe) in an unusual way – the Spirit "clothed himself with" Gideon as a garment. This strong expression indicates complete divine empowerment and occurs only here, in 1 Chron 12:18, and 2 Chron 24:20.

**The fleece test (גִּזַּ֥ת הַצֶּ֖מֶר)** with dew represents Gideon's need for confirmation. Dew was normally abundant in the region, so the first test (wet fleece, dry ground) was natural. The reversed test (dry fleece, wet ground) was the real miracle, since wool normally absorbs moisture from its surroundings.
