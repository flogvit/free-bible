# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 118

## Events and Customs

Psalm 118 is a processional liturgy associated with thanksgiving festivals at the temple, likely the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot). The structure reveals a liturgical movement: a procession approaches the temple (vv. 1–18), requests entry through the gates (vv. 19–20), and performs rituals within the temple courts (vv. 21–29). Verse 27 describes a specific festival custom: "Bind the festal offering with cords to the horns of the altar." The Hebrew text (אִסְרוּ־חַג בַּעֲבֹתִים עַד־קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ) uses עַד (until/to), indicating a procession toward the altar. This refers to the practice of leading sacrificial animals in procession, possibly bound with branches or cords, up to the burnt offering altar. The horns of the altar were the four raised projections at the corners of the altar, which held special sanctity.

The cry in verse 25 (Hebrew *hoshi'a na* – "save now!") became a central part of the Feast of Tabernacles liturgy and later developed into the Greek "hosanna." During this festival, the people carried *lulav* (bundles of palm, myrtle, and willow branches) and marched around the altar while shouting this verse.

## Key Terms

The word *chesed* (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love" or "faithful love") is repeated as a refrain throughout the psalm and denotes God's covenantal faithfulness—a love that remains true to its commitments. The expression *sha'arei-tsedeq* (שַׁעֲרֵי־צֶדֶק, "gates of righteousness") in verse 19 may refer to a specific temple gate, possibly the eastern gate used on solemn occasions.

The image of "the stone that the builders rejected" (v. 22) uses architectural terminology. *Rosh pinnah* ("cornerstone" or "capstone") was either the foundation stone that determined the angle of the entire building or the capstone that crowned and completed the structure. The metaphor describes a dramatic reversal in which what was rejected becomes the most important.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The liturgical structure of Psalm 118 is reflected in findings from the temple and contemporary sources. The Mishnah tractate Sukkah describes in detail how this psalm was used during the Feast of Tabernacles in the Second Temple period. The people responded with "Hallelujah" or repeated half-verses while the Levites sang, and all waved their *lulav* bundles at verses 1, 25, and 29. This confirms the antiphonal character of the psalm, where different groups (Israel, the house of Aaron, those who fear the LORD) respond to one another.
