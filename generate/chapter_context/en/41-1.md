# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 1

## People

**John the Baptist** is depicted wearing clothing made of camel hair and a leather belt, a deliberate parallel to the prophet Elijah (2 Kings 1:8). This signaled to Jewish listeners that John stood in the prophetic tradition.

**Simon, Andrew, James, and John** are introduced as fishermen by the Sea of Galilee. The fact that Zebedee had hired workers (v. 20) indicates that his family belonged to the middle class. The fishing industry around the lake was extensive and regulated, with fishing rights controlled by the authorities.

## Places

**The Jordan River and the wilderness** were charged with symbolic meaning. The Israelites crossed the Jordan to enter the Promised Land, and John's baptism in the same river symbolized a new beginning for the people. The wilderness was where Israel wandered for 40 years, and Jesus' 40 days there mirror this.

**Capernaum** was a bustling town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, strategically located along the trade route known as the Via Maris. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains that may date from a first-century synagogue beneath the ruins of a later synagogue from the fourth century.

**Nazareth** was an insignificant village in Galilee with an estimated population of 200 to 400 people. The fact that the town is not mentioned in the Old Testament underscores Jesus' humble origins.

## Events and Customs

**Baptism for repentance** was known from Jewish purification rituals, but John's practice was unique in that it was a one-time act connected with confession of sin and repentance. That John baptized Jews—not only Gentiles who converted—implied that even the people of Israel needed cleansing and repentance. This was a radical message.

**The Sabbath service in the synagogue** (v. 21) followed a set pattern of scripture reading, translation into Aramaic, and exposition. It was common to invite visiting teachers to speak. That Jesus taught "with authority" (ἐξουσία) stood in contrast to the teaching of the scribes, who typically cited earlier rabbis as their authority.

**Leprosy and purification rites** (vv. 40–44) were regulated in Leviticus 13–14. Lepers were ritually unclean and had to live isolated outside the community. Healing required inspection by a priest and specific sacrifices. That Jesus touched the leper broke with purity norms and demonstrated his radical approach.

## Key Terms

**Gospel** (εὐαγγέλιον) originally meant "good news" and was used in Greco-Roman culture for imperial proclamations, such as the announcement of a new emperor's accession to the throne. Mark's use of this word concerning Jesus was therefore a bold claim with political overtones.

**The kingdom of God** (ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ) was a central concept in Jewish expectation. It referred not primarily to a geographical area, but to God's rule and kingship breaking into the world. This was tied to the hope for Israel's restoration and God's final victory.

**Unclean spirit** (πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον) in Jewish understanding was an evil spiritual power that tormented people and opposed God. That Jesus had authority over these spirits demonstrated that the kingdom of God was breaking forth and Satan's power was being defeated.
