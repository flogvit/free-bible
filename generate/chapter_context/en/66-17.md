# Historical and Cultural Context for Revelation 17

## Places

The chapter explicitly identifies "the great prostitute" with a city that sits on "seven hills" (verse 9) and has "dominion over the kings of the earth" (verse 18). For John's contemporary readers, this was an unmistakable reference to Rome, which was universally known as "the city on seven hills" (Septimontium). The seven hills—Palatine, Aventine, Caelian, Esquiline, Viminal, Quirinal, and Capitoline—were central to Rome's founding myths and religious identity. Roman coins from the imperial period often depicted the goddess Roma seated upon seven hills.

The description of the woman sitting "on many waters" (verse 1) alludes to ancient Babylon by the Euphrates, but it also fits Rome, which controlled the Mediterranean Sea and its trade routes. "Babylon" (Βαβυλών, verse 5) functions as a code name for Rome, just as it does in Jewish apocalyptic literature from that period (4 Ezra, 2 Baruch)—both empires had destroyed Jerusalem and the temple.

## Events and Customs

The woman's clothing of "purple and scarlet" (πορφυροῦν καὶ κόκκινον) and her adornment with "gold, precious stones, and pearls" (verse 4) precisely reflect the luxury of the Roman elite. Purple fabric was extremely costly, extracted from murex snails, and reserved for emperors and senators. Scarlet was the color of Roman military cloaks and official garments. This description would immediately evoke images of imperial power and decadent wealth for its readers.

The cup in the woman's hand (verse 4) may allude to the Roman custom of libatio—drink offerings to the gods—but here the cup is filled with "abominations." This stands in sharp contrast to the cup of the Lord's Supper and suggests that Rome forces the nations to drink the poison of idolatry. Roman religious ceremonies often required subject peoples to participate in emperor worship and sacrifices to Roman gods.

## Key Terms

The Greek word πόρνη (pórnē, "prostitute") and related forms (πόρνης, πορνῶν, πορνείας) are used repeatedly throughout this chapter. In prophetic tradition, "whoredom" is a standard metaphor for idolatry and unfaithfulness to God (cf. Hosea, Ezekiel 16 and 23). When John applies this term to Rome, he accuses the empire of leading the nations into religious apostasy, especially through emperor worship, which demanded that people worship the emperor as divine.

The expression "the beast who was and is not and will come" (τὸ θηρίον ὃ ἦν καὶ οὐκ ἔστιν καὶ παρέσται, verse 8) parodies God's name "who is and who was and who is to come" (1:4, 8; 4:8). This likely reflects the Nero redivivus myth—a widespread belief in the first century that Emperor Nero, who died in AD 68, had not truly died but would return, often thought to come from the East with Parthian armies. Several false Neros actually appeared in the decades following his death.

The title "King of kings and Lord of lords" (κύριος κυρίων ... καὶ βασιλεὺς βασιλέων, verse 14; cf. Deut 10:17; Dan 2:47) was used as a title for Persian and Parthian kings and was also applied to Roman emperors. John attributes this title to the Lamb, constituting a direct challenge to the emperor's claimed sovereignty.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries confirm the extreme wealth described in the chapter. Excavations in Rome have uncovered enormous quantities of imported luxury goods—Indian pearls, Chinese silk, Egyptian ivory, and precious stones from across the empire. The shipwreck at Mahdia (found off the coast of Tunisia) and other Roman-era shipwrecks demonstrate the scale of the luxury trade that made Rome "the great city."

Inscriptions from Asia Minor, especially from Ephesus and Pergamum, document the widespread nature of emperor worship and its demands for loyalty. These inscriptions show how local elites competed to display devotion to Rome and the emperor, creating the social and economic pressure Christians faced when they refused to participate. One inscription from Ephesus hails the emperor as "god and savior," language that directly challenged the Christian confession of Jesus as Lord.
