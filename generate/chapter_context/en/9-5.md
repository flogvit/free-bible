# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 5

## Places

The chapter follows the ark on its journey through three of the five major Philistine city-states: **Ashdod**, **Gath**, and **Ekron**. Ashdod was located by the coast, about 35 km south of modern-day Tel Aviv, and served as a significant port city and religious center for the Philistines. Gath lay further inland, east of Ashdod, while Ekron was the northernmost of the Philistine cities. Archaeological excavations at Tell Ashdod have uncovered temple structures from the Iron Age. The cities were organized as independent city-states, each ruled by its own *seren* (lord), yet they cooperated in matters of common concern, as seen in the council meetings described in verses 8 and 11.

## People

The god **Dagon** (דָּגוֹן) was a Semitic deity with roots in Mesopotamia. The name is linked to *dagan* (grain), and he was worshipped as a god of fertility and grain. The Philistines, who originally came from the Aegean region, adopted the Dagon cult from local Semitic peoples, and his temple in Ashdod was likely the city's most important sanctuary. The term *sarnei pelishtim* (סַרְנֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים, "Philistine lords") in verses 8 and 11 is consistently used to refer to the five Philistine rulers and is a non-Semitic loanword probably originating from an Aegean language.

## Events and Customs

The Philistines' placement of the ark of the covenant in Dagon's temple (v. 2) followed a widespread practice in antiquity where captured divine images were brought to the victor's temple. This symbolized that the victor's god had defeated the conquered deity. The irony of the narrative lies in the reversal of roles: Israel's invisible God humbles Dagon in his own temple. The custom of not stepping on the temple threshold (v. 5) shows how the story explains a religious practice (etiological function). The sanctity of thresholds was widespread in the ancient Near East—it marked the boundary between profane and sacred space.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word *ofalim/tehorim* (עֲפָלִים/טְחֹרִים) in verses 6, 9, and 12 is disputed. The *ketiv* form (the written text) suggests "tumors" or "swellings," while the *qere* form (the read text) points to "hemorrhoids." The Septuagint adds that mice ravaged the land. This addition, together with the description of swellings, has led some scholars to propose bubonic plague as a possible background, although this remains speculative. The expression "the hand of the Lord was heavy" (*yad-YHWH*) in verses 6 and 11 is a powerful metaphor for divine intervention as punishment—an expression also used in Exodus regarding the plagues on Egypt. The word *mehumah* (מְהוּמָה, "confusion/panic") in verses 9 and 11 describes the chaotic state that arises when God intervenes directly—the same word is used for God-sent panic in military contexts.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Tell Ashdod have uncovered several layered temple structures from the Philistine period (1100–900 BCE). A particularly interesting find consists of small clay figurines combining human and animal features. In verse 4, it is mentioned that "only Dagon" (*raq Dagon*) remained after his head and hands had fallen off—the expression is disputed and may mean that only the body or torso was left. Discoveries of Philistine pottery with Aegean characteristics confirm the Philistines' origins and their distinct cultural identity in the region.
