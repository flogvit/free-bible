# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 29

## People

The chapter mentions the kings **Sihon of Heshbon** and **Og of Bashan** (v. 6–7), two Amorite rulers whom Israel defeated before crossing the Jordan. Heshbon was the capital of Sihon’s kingdom in Transjordan, and archaeological excavations at Tell Hesban have revealed settlement dating back to the Bronze Age. Bashan, Og’s kingdom, included the fertile highlands east of the Sea of Galilee. These victories were of great significance because they provided land for the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half the tribe of Manasseh, and served as concrete evidence of the Lord’s help.

## Places

The reference to **Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim** (v. 22) points to the destroyed cities near the Dead Sea (Genesis 19). The southern part of the Dead Sea region is known for its sulfate and salt deposits, and the landscape still bears signs of volcanic and geological activity. The description of "sulfur and salt" rendering the land barren corresponds with the character of this area. These cities functioned as a cultural symbol of total destruction throughout the Old Testament period.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes a **covenant-making ceremony** in which all Israel gathers—leaders, elders, children, women, and foreigners, down to woodcutters and water carriers (v. 9–10). The expression "to enter into the covenant" (לְעָבְרְךָ בִּבְרִית, v. 11) can literally be translated as "to pass through the covenant," possibly alluding to the ancient custom where covenant participants walked between split animal carcasses (cf. Genesis 15:17; Jeremiah 34:18). The covenant ceremony also included curses (אָלָה), which were standard in ancient Near Eastern treaties.

## Key Terms

The expression **"a root that bears bitter and poisonous fruit"** (שֹׁרֶשׁ פֹּרֶה רֹאשׁ וְלַעֲנָה, v. 17) uses plant imagery to describe the spread of idolatry. The Hebrew *ro'sh* denotes a poisonous plant, while *la'anah* (wormwood) was known for its bitterness. The image warns that one person’s apostasy can poison the entire community.

The concluding verse (v. 28) contains the well-known statement about **"the hidden things"** (הַנִּסְתָּרֹת) versus **"the revealed things"** (הַנִּגְלֹת). In the Hebrew Masoretic text, the words "us and our children" have dots above certain letters—a rare textual critical marking used by ancient scribes to signal uncertainty or special significance. This verse establishes an important principle: the revealed commands of the Torah are Israel’s responsibility, while God’s hidden plans remain with Him.
