# Context for Leviticus 26

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains what is called a "covenant ratification formula" or "conditional covenant," with blessings and curses. This literary form was widespread in the ancient Near East, especially in Hittite vassal treaties from the second millennium B.C.E. In such treaties, the overlord promised blessings if the vassal kept the covenant, while breach would bring specific curses. The structure of Leviticus 26 follows this pattern: first, blessings for obedience (vv. 3–13), then escalating curses for disobedience in several stages (vv. 14–17, 18–20, 21–22, 23–26, 27–39), and finally hope for restoration (vv. 40–45).

The curses describe a gradual intensification—sickness, famine, wild animals, war, plague, and exile—reflecting the actual reality of covenant breach in ancient societies. The sevenfold escalation is clearly marked in the text (שֶׁבַע, "seven [times]," mentioned in vv. 18, 21, 24, 28). Particularly striking is the description that ten women will bake bread in one oven (v. 26), indicating extreme food shortage during a siege. The reference to eating "the flesh of your own sons and daughters" (v. 29) has parallels in Assyrian and Babylonian curse texts and describes siege conditions that actually occurred in antiquity.

## Key Terms

**Berit** (בְּרִית, "covenant," vv. 9, 15, 25, 42, 44, 45): The central term of the chapter that ties together the blessings and curses. The covenant with Israel is the foundation for the entire structure—blessings follow from covenant faithfulness, curses from covenant breach. In v. 42, the covenants with the patriarchs are mentioned in reverse chronological order: Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham—a literary structure that emphasizes God remembering back through the generations.

**Bamot** (בָּמוֹת, "high places," v. 30): These were cultic high places often located on mountain tops or artificial mounds. They were widespread in Canaanite religion and later became a problem in Israel when they were used for syncretistic worship.

**Chammanim** (חַמָּנִים, "sun pillars" or "incense altars," v. 30): These cult objects were likely connected to sun worship or incense offerings. Archaeological finds from Palmyra and other places have uncovered such stone altars.

**Gillulim** (גִּלּוּלִים, "idols," v. 30): A derogatory Hebrew word for idol images, possibly derived from a word meaning "dung" or "round stones." The term is often used by the prophet Ezekiel and emphasizes the worthlessness of idols.

**Qeri** (קֶרִי, vv. 21, 23–24, 27–28, 40–41): This rare word, often translated "hostility" or "opposition," describes an attitude of indifference or opposition toward God. Its meaning is debated—it may mean regarding God's actions as random rather than purposeful.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Vassal treaties from Hatti (the Hittite Empire) and later Assyrian treaties provide important background for understanding the chapter's structure and literary form. Especially relevant is Esarhaddon's vassal treaty (672 B.C.E.), which contains similar escalating curses including famine, cannibalism, and exile. Although this document is later than the traditional dating of the Pentateuch, it shows that such formulations were part of a long tradition in the region.

Archaeological excavations in Israel have uncovered numerous *bamot* (high places) with associated *matstsevot* (מַצֵּבֹת, stone pillars, cf. v. 1) and incense altars that match the descriptions in this chapter. Finds from Tel Dan, Megiddo, and Arad show that such cult sites were common in the Iron Age, confirming the text's relevance to actual religious practice in ancient Israel.
