# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 23

## People

**The scribes and Pharisees** were two partially overlapping groups in Jewish society. The scribes (γραμματεῖς) were professional legal experts and copyists of Scripture, while the Pharisees were a religious movement known for their precise observance of both written and oral law. Not all scribes were Pharisees, but many belonged to this faction. Josephus estimates that there were about 6,000 Pharisees at this time. They had significant influence over the religious practices of ordinary Jews, even though they did not hold the same formal political power as the Sadducees in the Sanhedrin.

**Zechariah, son of Barachiah** (v. 35) is a debated identification. Some scholars connect him to the priest Zechariah who was killed in the temple court according to 2 Chronicles 24:20-21 (where he is called the son of Jehoiada). Others think of the prophet Zechariah (Zech 1:1) or a Zechariah whom Josephus records as being killed by the zealots in the temple in AD 67-68. The most likely interpretation is that Jesus refers to Zechariah in Chronicles, who stands last in the order of the Hebrew Bible—so that from Abel to Zechariah covers "from first to last" martyrdom in Scripture.

## Places

**Moses' seat** (v. 2, Μωϋσέως καθέδρας) was a specific place of honor in the synagogue where the teacher sat when instructing. Archaeologists have found stone chairs from this period in several synagogues, including a famous example from the Chorazin synagogue in Galilee (dated to the 3rd-4th centuries but built on an older tradition) and from Delos. The chair symbolized the teacher's authority to interpret the Law of Moses and represented continuity in the transmission from Moses.

**The temple and the altar** (vv. 16-22, 35) refer to the Herodian temple in Jerusalem. The gold in the temple included gilded walls, the golden vine above the entrance to the Holy Place, and gifts of gold. The altar of burnt offering stood in the courtyard before the temple building itself. The area "between the temple and the altar" (v. 35) was the priests' court—a most holy place where the murder of Zechariah constituted a particularly severe desecration.

## Events and Customs

**Phylacteries** (φυλακτήρια, v. 5), called *tefillin* in Hebrew, were small leather boxes containing Scripture passages that were bound on the forehead and left arm during prayer. The box contained parchment scrolls with texts from Exodus 13:1-16 and Deuteronomy 6:4-9; 11:13-21. Archaeological finds from Qumran have provided preserved examples of such prayer straps from the first century. The criticism was that some made them extra wide (πλατύνουσι) to appear more pious before others.

**Tassels** (κράσπεδα), or *tsitsit* in Hebrew, were fringes attached to the corners of garments according to Numbers 15:38-40. They were meant to remind the wearer of God's commands. To "enlarge" the tassels was a sign of demonstrative piety.

**Tithing mint, dill and cumin** (v. 23) shows how the Pharisees expanded the laws on tithing. The books of Moses required tithes from grain, wine and oil, but the Pharisees extended this to include even the smallest garden herbs to ensure they fulfilled the law completely. This practice is confirmed in the Mishnah (Ma'aserot 4:5).

**Whitewashed tombs** (v. 27) refer to the custom of whitewashing graves before Passover. This was done to make them visible so that pilgrims could avoid unintentionally touching them and becoming ritually unclean before the festival (cf. Num 19:16). The lime gave the tombs a neat, white appearance on the outside, while they contained decay within—a powerful image of outward piety hiding inner corruption.

## Key Terms

**"Rabbi"** (Ῥαββί, vv. 7-8) literally means "my great one" or "my master" and was an honorific title for teachers. In Jesus' time it was becoming a formal title for recognized legal experts. Jesus warns against using such titles as status symbols within the community of disciples, where equality as brothers and sisters should prevail.

**"Woe to you"** (Οὐαί, vv. 13-29) is repeated seven or eight times depending on whether verse 14 is included (this verse is textually disputed and missing in several important manuscripts). The expression forms a structuring element in the chapter. This term (Hebrew *hoy*) comes from prophetic language in the Old Testament where it introduces words of judgment (cf. Isaiah 5:8-23). It expresses not only warning but also grief over the conditions described.

**"Hypocrites"** (ὑποκριταί) comes from Greek theater and originally denoted an actor who wore a mask. In this context, it describes people whose outward religious appearance does not match their inner attitude and motivation. The word is used six times in this chapter alone.

**"Gehenna"** (γεέννης, vv. 15, 33) is the Greek form of Hebrew *Ge-Hinnom* (Valley of Hinnom), a valley south of Jerusalem. In the Old Testament, this place was associated with idolatry and child sacrifice (Jer 7:31), and in intertestamental times it became a symbol of the final place of punishment for the wicked.

**Proselytes** (προσήλυτον, v. 15) were Gentiles who had fully converted to Judaism through circumcision (for men), baptism and offering. The Pharisees were active in missionary work, which is also confirmed by Josephus and Roman sources. The criticism is not of the mission itself, but that new converts were shaped according to wrong ideals.
