# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 51

## People

Abraham and Sarah are highlighted in verses 1–2 as the prime examples of God’s faithfulness and blessing. The reference to Abraham being called as "one" (אֶחָד, *echad*) yet multiplied serves as comfort for the exiles who now feel few in number. This is one of the few direct references to Abraham and Sarah in Isaiah, emphasizing that God can repeat His miracle: creating a great nation from a single person. For the exile community, which felt reduced and humiliated, this was a powerful reminder of their identity and origin.

## Places

**Zion/Jerusalem** stands at the center of the chapter (vv. 3, 11, 16–17). The city is personified as a woman who has drunk the cup of the Lord’s wrath and lies in ruins. This reflects the actual condition following Babylon’s destruction in 586 B.C. The prophecy that Zion’s wilderness will become like Eden (v. 3) draws on familiar images of the garden of paradise and promises a radical transformation of the devastated landscape.

**Tehom** (תְּהוֹם, "the deep") in verse 10 refers to the primeval sea and the great abyss. The term connects the creation account with the Exodus from Egypt, where God dried up the depths of the sea so that the redeemed could cross over.

## Events and Customs

**The cup of wrath** (כּוֹס חֲמָתוֹ) in verses 17–23 is a powerful image drawn from legal and ritual practices in the ancient Near East. The cup symbolizes the judgment that God assigns, which must be drunk to the dregs. This metaphor also appears in Jeremiah and other prophets. That the cup will now be transferred to Jerusalem’s oppressors represents a turning point in destiny.

The image of enemies forcing the defeated to lie flat on the ground while they walk over them (v. 23) reflects known practices in the ancient Near East, where victors literally trampled their foes as a sign of total dominance.

## Key Terms

**Rahab** (רַהַב) in verse 9 is a mythological sea monster representing chaotic forces. In Israelite poetry, Rahab was often used as a symbol for Egypt (cf. Isa 30:7). Here the monster is linked with **tannin** (תַּנִּין, "sea serpent/dragon"), and both refer to God’s victory over chaos at creation and at the Red Sea. The prophet thus unites creation theology and Exodus theology to affirm that God can bring about a new deliverance.

**Tzedek** (צֶדֶק, "righteousness") is used throughout the chapter and denotes both God’s saving action and the right order He establishes. For Isaiah, righteousness is not abstract morality but God’s concrete intervention on behalf of His people.

**Torah** (תּוֹרָה) in verse 4 here refers to God’s instruction and guidance that will go out from Him to the nations. The word carries a broader meaning than the specific Mosaic Law, pointing toward a universal dimension of God’s salvation.
