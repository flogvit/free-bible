# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 42

## People

**The Lord’s Servant (עַבְדִּי, 'avdi)**: Isaiah 42:1–9 contains the first "Servant Song" in the book of Isaiah. The identity of the servant has been the subject of extensive debate. In its immediate context, the servant may refer to the prophet himself, to Israel as a nation (cf. 41:8–9), or to a future ideal figure. The Hebrew word *meshullam* (מְשֻׁלָּם, "the devoted" or "the complete") in verse 19 is otherwise used as a proper name, but here it appears to describe the servant with an ironic double meaning.

**Kedar (קֵדָר)**: An Arab Bedouin tribe descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13). They were known as nomadic people in the Syro-Arabian desert and are mentioned in Assyrian annals as *Qidri*. Here, Kedar represents the desert peoples who will praise the Lord.

## Places

**Sela (סֶלַע)**: The city mentioned in verse 11 is likely the Nabatean Petra in southern Jordan, or another mountain settlement in the Edom region. The name means "rock" in Hebrew. The place had a long history of habitation in biblical times. Its location in the mountains explains the phrasing "from the mountaintops they will cry out."

**The Islands/Coastlands (אִיִּים, 'iyyim)**: This expression, which appears in the chapter (vv. 4, 10, 12), refers to the Mediterranean islands and coastal regions, including Cyprus, Crete, and the Greek islands. In the ancient Near East, these areas represented the farthest edges of the world from an Israelite perspective.

## Events and Customs

**The Motif of Captivity**: Verses 7 and 22 describe people in captivity and dark prisons. This reflects the Babylonian practice of deporting conquered peoples and holding prisoners in underground cells or "houses of darkness" (בֵּית כֶּלֶא). Assyrian and Babylonian reliefs often show captives in chains, and texts mention dungeons where opponents were kept in darkness.

**"A New Song" (שִׁיר חָדָשׁ)**: The call in verse 10 to sing a new song to the Lord is a well-known psalm motif (cf. Ps 33:3; 96:1; 98:1). In an Old Testament context, new songs marked God’s new acts of salvation. The expression signals that God is about to do something radically new that requires fresh worship.

## Key Terms

**Mishpat (מִשְׁפָּט)**: This word appears four times in verses 1–4 and is usually translated as "justice" or "judgment." However, it encompasses more than legal righteousness; it includes divine order, just conditions, and God’s plan of salvation for the nations. That the servant will "bring forth justice to the nations" suggests a universal dimension in God’s plan.

**A Reed That Is Crushed / A Dimly Burning Wick (קָנֶה רָצוּץ / פִּשְׁתָּה כֵהָה)**: The images in verse 3 are drawn from daily life. Crushed reeds were used to make simple flutes, and dimly burning linen wicks were the light source for the poor. The servant will not break what is already weak or extinguish what is nearly burned out—a radical contrast to the brutal behavior of kings in that era.

**God’s Struggle as Birth Pangs**: In verse 14, the image of a woman giving birth (כַּיּוֹלֵדָה אֶפְעֶה) is used to describe God’s intervention. This is an unusual and powerful image that combines pain with creative power, suggesting that God’s "silence" is now broken by a dramatic act that brings something new into the world.
