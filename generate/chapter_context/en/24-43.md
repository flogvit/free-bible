# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 43

## People

**Azariah son of Hoshaiah** and **Johanan son of Kareah** appear here as leaders of the remaining Judean population after Gedaliah’s murder. Azariah (Hebrew עֲזַרְיָה, "The Lord has helped") is called "Jaazaniah" (יַאֲזַנְיָה) in 42:1, which may indicate that he had two names or that this represents a textual variant. These men are described as "arrogant" (הַזֵּדִים), an expression that in Hebrew connotes arrogant rebellion against God. Johanan had previously played a positive role by warning Gedaliah about Ishmael (40:13–16), but here he shows disobedience to the prophet’s word.

**Baruch son of Neriah** is accused of inciting Jeremiah against the people. Baruch was Jeremiah’s secretary and scribe (chap. 36). The accusation against him may reflect that he held higher social status and influence than an ordinary scribe—his brother Seraiah was a court official under King Zedekiah (51:59). A seal impression (bulla) bearing the inscription "Belonging to Berechiah son of Neriah, the scribe" has been found archaeologically and supports his historical existence.

## Places

**Tahpanhes** (תַּחְפַּנְחֵס) was an Egyptian border city in the eastern Nile Delta, known in Greek as Daphne (Δάφναι). The city was strategically located at the border with the Sinai Desert and served as a military garrison and trading post. For Judean refugees, this was a natural first stop upon entering Egypt. The city likely already had a significant Jewish colony at that time.

**Beth Shemesh** (בֵּית שֶׁמֶשׁ, "House of the Sun") in verse 13 does not refer to the Israelite city but to the Egyptian city Heliopolis (On), the most important center for sun worship in Egypt. The city was known for its massive obelisks and temple complex dedicated to the sun god Ra. The name is a Hebrew translation of the city’s function as a place of sun worship.

## Events and Customs

Jeremiah’s symbolic act of burying large stones in the masonry at Pharaoh’s residence (verse 9) follows a pattern of prophetic sign-acts. Burying stones as a foundation for Nebuchadnezzar’s future throne was a visual prophecy that the Judean refugees were to witness. Such actions made the prophetic message concrete and memorable. The Hebrew word מַלְבֵּן can mean either "brick terrace" or "brick kiln," and likely refers to a public square or entrance area at the Egyptian royal building in Tahpanhes.

## Key Terms

The word **שַׁפְרִיר** (saphrir) in verse 10, translated as "throne carpet" or "canopy," is a rare Hebrew word that occurs only here in the Old Testament. Its meaning is uncertain, but it probably refers to a royal tent or magnificent canopy that Nebuchadnezzar would set up as a sign of his victory and dominion over Egypt. The image of the shepherd who will "wrap himself" (יַעְטֶה) around Egypt like a cloak (verse 12) is a powerful expression of total conquest—Nebuchadnezzar would plunder the land as easily as a shepherd puts on his clothes.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

At Tell Defenneh (ancient Tahpanhes), archaeologist Sir Flinders Petrie conducted excavations in 1886 and discovered the remains of a large brick platform in front of a fortress-like building from the 26th Dynasty (Saite period). Petrie identified this as the place mentioned in Jeremiah 43:9, called "Pharaoh’s house at Tahpanhes." Although the identification is not certain, the findings show that there was a significant Egyptian royal or administrative building on the site during Jeremiah’s time, which aligns with the biblical description.
