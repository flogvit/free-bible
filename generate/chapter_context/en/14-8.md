# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 8

## People

**Hiram (Huram) of Tyre** is mentioned in verse 2 as the one who gave cities to Solomon. This is interesting because the parallel text in 1 Kings 9:10-13 describes the opposite—that Solomon gave cities to Hiram, who was dissatisfied with them. The Chronicler’s account may reflect that Hiram returned the cities after being dissatisfied (cf. 1 Kgs 9:12-13), or it may represent the author’s theological emphasis that Israelite land was not permanently surrendered.

**Pharaoh’s daughter** (verse 11) was a politically significant marriage alliance. Marriages with the Egyptian royal family were highly prestigious in antiquity, since Egypt rarely gave princesses in marriage to foreign rulers. The fact that Solomon moved her out of the City of David for reasons of holiness shows the tension between political alliances and religious purity.

## Places

**Tadmor in the wilderness** (verse 4) is traditionally identified with Palmyra, an important oasis city in the Syrian desert. The city lay strategically along trade routes between Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean. Archaeological excavations at Palmyra have revealed monumental ruins, although most date from later periods. Solomon’s fortification of the site secured control over lucrative caravan trade.

**Hamath-Zobah** (verse 3) was an Aramean kingdom in northern Syria, near the Orontes River. Solomon’s military control over this area represented Israel’s greatest geographical extent and control over important northward trade routes.

**Upper and Lower Beth-Horon** (verse 5) were two cities that controlled the main passage from the coastal plain up to the Judean highlands. These fortified towns, with walls, gates, and barriers, were critical for the defense of Jerusalem.

**Baalath** (verse 6) was a city that Solomon built up, probably located in the region of Dan. The name means "female form of Baal" and may indicate that the place was previously associated with Canaanite worship.

**Ezion-Geber and Elath** (verse 17) were situated on the Gulf of Aqaba (the Red Sea) in Edom. Archaeological investigations at Tell el-Kheleifeh have uncovered remains that may have been an industrial facility or storage area from the Iron Age. These ports gave Solomon access to sea trade with Arabia, East Africa, and India.

## Events and Customs

**Forced labor of non-Israelites** (verses 7-9) followed a pattern known throughout the ancient Near East. The Chronicler emphasizes that Israelites were not made slaves but served as soldiers and officers. This differs from the account in 1 Kings, which suggests that Israelites also were required to perform forced labor. The system of *mas* (forced labor) was common in Egyptian and Mesopotamian practice.

**The three annual festivals** (verse 13)—the Festival of Unleavened Bread (Passover), the Festival of Weeks (Shavuot), and the Festival of Tabernacles (Sukkot)—were pilgrimage feasts during which Israelite men were to appear before the Lord in Jerusalem. This arrangement is rooted in the Mosaic Law (Exod 23:14-17) and became central to Jerusalem’s religious and economic significance.

**The overseers** (verse 10) numbered 250 chief officers who supervised the workers. This number differs from 1 Kgs 9:23, which mentions 550 overseers, a discrepancy that may be explained by different categorizations of officials.

## Key Terms

**"Ish ha-Elohim"** (אִישׁ־הָאֱלֹהִים) – "man of God" – is used for David in verse 14. This is an honorific title usually applied to prophets (such as Moses and Elijah), emphasizing David’s special authority as the one who organized temple worship.

**The concept of holiness** in verse 11 illustrates the idea that places where the Lord’s ark had been became holy and unsuitable for a foreign woman. This reflects priestly theology regarding degrees of holiness and purity.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Trade with Ophir** (verse 18) mentions 450 talents of gold from Ophir. An ostracon (inscribed pottery shard) found at Tell Qasile near Tel Aviv, dating to the eighth century B.C., bears an inscription traditionally read as a reference to "Ophir gold." This may be the only extra-biblical evidence for trade with Ophir, although the reading is debated among scholars. The location of Ophir is also disputed, with suggestions including southern Arabia, Somalia/Eritrea, or western India.
