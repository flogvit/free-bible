# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 10

## Places

The chapter takes place in the temple complex in Jerusalem, specifically mentioning "the inner court" (v. 3), "the threshold of the house" (v. 4, 18) and "the east gate of the Lord's house" (v. 19). These architectural elements correspond to Solomon's temple as described in 1 Kings 6–7. The inner court was the priests' area, inaccessible to ordinary Israelites. The threshold held special sacred significance in ancient Near Eastern thought as the transition between the holy and the profane. That God's glory moves from the cherubim to the threshold and then to the east gate shows a gradual withdrawal from the temple—a dramatic reversal of when the glory filled the temple at its dedication (1 Kings 8:10–11).

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **כְּרוּב/כְּרוּבִים** (*keruv/keruvim*, cherub/cherubim) is central to this chapter and appears more than twenty times in various forms. Ezekiel here explicitly identifies the living creatures from chapter 1 as cherubim (v. 15, 20). In Israelite religion, cherubim were guardians of holy places—two golden cherubim stood above the ark of the covenant in the Most Holy Place (Exodus 25:18–22), and cherub motifs were woven into the curtain and carved into the walls. In broader ancient Near Eastern iconography, composite creatures with human, lion, ox, and eagle elements served as throne guardians for the gods.

The word **גַּלְגַּל** (*galgal*, v. 2, 6, 13) means "wheel" or "whirling thing" and is used here as a special name for the wheels in the vision. That the wheels are called by this name "in my hearing" (v. 13) emphasizes their cosmic and mysterious character. The term evokes associations with whirlwinds and heavenly movement.

## Events and Customs

The action of taking glowing coals from the altar and scattering them over the city (v. 2, 6–7) has its background in sacrificial worship. In the temple, fire from the burnt offering altar was considered holy, and coals from the altar were used for incense offerings (Leviticus 16:12). Here, the holy fire is turned into judgment—the same fire that represented God's presence now becomes an instrument of destruction. The man clothed in linen recalls priestly garments (Exodus 28:42; Leviticus 6:10), but his role here is to execute judgment, not atonement. The parallel with Isaiah 6:6–7 is clear, but there the coals brought cleansing; here they bring destruction.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

A remarkable difference from 1:10 is that in verse 14 the first face is described as "the face of a cherub" (פְּנֵי הַכְּרוּב) instead of the face of an ox. This raises questions about the relationship between the cherub and the ox in the prophet's vision and has been the subject of much discussion among interpreters.

Archaeological excavations have uncovered numerous depictions of composite creatures from the ancient Near East that illuminate Ezekiel's description of the cherubim. From Nineveh, Nimrud and other Assyrian cities, colossal sphinxes and *lamassu* figures have been found—composite creatures with human heads, lion or ox bodies and wings. Ivory carvings from Samaria and Nimrud show similar composite beings as throne figures. These findings confirm that such creatures represented a well-known iconographic tradition that Ezekiel's audience would immediately recognize as expressions of divine majesty and royal power.
