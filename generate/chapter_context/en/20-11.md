# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 11

## Events and Customs

Chapter 11 opens with a strong condemnation of false weights (v. 1), reflecting a widespread practice in the ancient Near East. Merchants used stone weights to measure goods, and it was tempting to have two sets—one for buying and one for selling. This practice was so common that it is condemned multiple times in the Mosaic Law (Leviticus 19:35–36; Deuteronomy 25:13–16). Archaeological finds from Israelite cities have uncovered standardized weights marked with royal symbols, showing that authorities attempted to regulate trade.

The verses about hoarding grain (v. 26) reflect the economic reality of an agricultural society. During times of scarcity, wealthy grain merchants could withhold supplies to drive up prices. This severely harmed the poor, and society cursed such speculators while blessing those who sold at fair prices. The practice of standing surety for others (v. 15) was risky and is warned against in several places in Proverbs, since it could lead to financial ruin.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word *tsedaqah* (צְדָקָה, "righteousness") appears repeatedly throughout the chapter (vv. 4, 5, 6, 18, 19) and denotes more than legal correctness—it involves living in right relationship with God and with others. The word *tachbulot* (תַּחְבֻּלוֹת, v. 14), translated as "wise guidance" or "strategic planning", comes from nautical language and describes the art of steering a ship—a metaphor for leading people through dangerous waters.

The expression "a gold ring in a pig’s snout" (v. 22) uses an image that was especially offensive in Israelite culture. The pig was unclean and despised, while gold rings were valuable ornaments. The contrast between outward beauty and inner lack of judgment could hardly be expressed more strongly. The term *nephesh berakah* (נֶפֶשׁ־בְּרָכָה, "a generous soul", v. 25) describes a person who is a source of blessing to others—an ideal in Israelite communal thinking.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Israel and Judah have uncovered numerous stone weights from the Iron Age, many with inscriptions such as *pim*, *shekel*, and *beka*. Interestingly, the weight of these stones varies significantly, even within the same city, confirming the text’s concern about dishonest weights. At Lachish and other cities, sets of weights that clearly deviate from the standard have been found, possibly used for fraud. This provides concrete background for the strong condemnation in verse 1.
