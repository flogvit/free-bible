## Events and Customs

The psalm’s call to the "redeemed" gathered "from lands, from east and west, from north and from sea" (vv. 2–3) points to a context after the Babylonian exile. This language reflects prophetic promises of return, especially from Isaiah 43:5–6 and 49:12. The psalm may have been used as a liturgical song of thanksgiving at the temple following its rebuilding, where returning Israelites together confessed the Lord’s faithfulness.

The four scenarios described—wanderers in the desert (vv. 4–9), prisoners in darkness (vv. 10–16), the sick because of sin (vv. 17–22), and sailors in a storm (vv. 23–32)—represent archetypal crises. The structure, with repeated cries to the Lord followed by thanksgiving (refrains in vv. 8, 15, 21, 31), suggests responsive use in worship. The offering of a "thank offering" and giving testimony "with joy" (v. 22) refers to the concrete practice of thank offerings described in Leviticus 7:12–15.

## Places

"The sea" (יָם, *yam*) in verse 3 refers to the Mediterranean Sea, which lay west of Israel and therefore denoted the western direction in Hebrew thought. The section on seafaring (vv. 23–32) describes "the great waters" and "the depths" (מְצוּלָה, *metsulah*), expressions that point to the Mediterranean Sea and its dangers. The description of waves rising "to heaven" and sinking "into the depths" (v. 26) reflects the real danger of storms on the open sea in ancient times.

## Key Terms

The word "חֶסֶד" (*khesed*)—translated as "steadfast love" or "mercy"—appears as a key term in the psalm’s refrains (vv. 8, 15, 21, 31) and conclusion (v. 43). The term expresses God’s covenantal faithfulness and compassion, a love that goes beyond what is expected and remains even when people fail.

The expression "יֹשְׁבֵי חֹשֶׁךְ וְצַלְמָוֶת"—"those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death" (vv. 10, 14)—combines two words to create an intense picture of despair. "צַלְמָוֶת" (*tsalmavét*) can mean either "shadow of death" or "deep darkness" and is also used in Psalm 23:4. The term describes both physical imprisonment and spiritual hopelessness.

"שַׁעֲרֵי מָוֶת"—"the gates of death" (v. 18)—is an image drawn from the ancient Near Eastern worldview, where the realm of the dead was thought to be a fortified city with gates.
