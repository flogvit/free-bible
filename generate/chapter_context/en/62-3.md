# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 John 3

## People

**Cain** (v. 12) is used as a negative example from Genesis 4. In late Jewish tradition during John's time, Cain had developed into an archetypal figure of evil. Texts such as the *Book of Jubilees* and Philo’s writings portrayed him not merely as a murderer, but as one who belonged to the evil one. John’s phrasing "ἐκ τοῦ πονηροῦ ἦν" (was of the evil one) reflects this developed tradition in which Cain was seen as the devil's spiritual son—an interpretation also found in later rabbinic literature.

## Events and Customs

The expression "ἁγνίζει ἑαυτὸν" (cleanses himself) in verse 3 uses terminology from Jewish purification practices. In the temple context, the law required ritual cleansings before one could approach the holy place. John transfers this concept to ethical cleansing in anticipation of Christ’s appearing. For readers with a Jewish background, this would evoke associations with priests preparing for service.

Verses 16–17 address the early church's practice of economic sharing and care for poor fellow believers. The expression "τὸν βίον τοῦ κόσμου" (the world’s goods/livelihood) and the exhortation not to "close one’s heart" (κλείσῃ τὰ σπλάγχνα) point to concrete situations in the congregations where wealthy members were expected to help needy brothers and sisters.

## Key Terms

**Τέκνα θεοῦ** (children of God) in verses 1–2, 10 stands central to the chapter. In a Greco-Roman context, divine descent was reserved for emperors and mythical heroes. John radically expands the concept: all believers are born of God and will become "like him" (ὅμοιοι αὐτῷ). The contrast with "τέκνα τοῦ διαβόλου" (children of the devil) in verse 10 establishes a dualistic worldview that has parallels to the distinction between "sons of light" and "sons of darkness" found in the Qumran texts.

**Ἀνομία** (lawlessness) in verse 4 is more than breaking the law—it denotes rebellion against God’s order. In Jewish apocalyptic literature, anomia was linked to apostasy in the end times. When John defines sin as anomia, he connects the congregation's situation to an eschatological framework.

**Σπέρμα αὐτοῦ** (his seed/offspring) in verse 9 is an unusual expression. It describes a divine life-force that remains in the believer. The concept may have had polemical force against early proto-Gnostic ideas, but John anchors it in ethical transformation rather than cosmic speculation.

**Παρρησία** (boldness/freedom of speech) in verse 21 was an important democratic ideal in Greek culture—the right to speak freely in civic assemblies. In a religious context, it denotes confident access to God, which in pagan temples was reserved for priests and the chosen few.
