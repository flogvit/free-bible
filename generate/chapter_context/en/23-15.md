# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 15

## Places

Isaiah 15 contains a remarkable geographical mapping of Moab, making it possible to trace the spread of destruction throughout the land. **Ar-Moab** and **Kir-Moab** (verse 1) were major cities; Kir-Moab (also known as Kir-Hareseth) is identified with modern-day Kerak in Jordan, a fortified city on the high plateau. **Bajit** ("the house/temple") **and Dibon** (verse 2) are mentioned together—Dibon was Moab's administrative center, known from the Mesha Stele; the city lay in northern Moab and had "high places" for worship. **Nebo** and **Medeba** (verse 2) were cities on the northern plateau, where Nebo also gave its name to the mountain where Moses died. **Heshbon** and **Elealeh** (verse 4) lay in the far north of Moab, near the border with Ammonite and Israelite territory. **Jahaz** was the place where Israel defeated the Amorite king Sihon. **Horonaim** (verse 5) lay on the road southward and is also mentioned on the Mesha Stele. **Zoar** (verse 5) marks the southern border, at the southern tip of the Dead Sea, showing that the flight route extends through the entire land. **The waters of Nimrim** (verse 6) likely refer to Wadi Nimrim east of Jordan, known for its springs. **The Brook of Willows** (נַחַל הָעֲרָבִים, verse 7) probably designates Wadi el-Hesa, the boundary river toward Edom in the south. **Eglaim** and **Beer-elim** (verse 8) mark the extremes where cries of lamentation are heard, revealing the full extent of the catastrophe. **Dimon** (verse 9) is possibly a variant of Dibon or a separate place; the name is used in a wordplay with "blood" (דָם).

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes intense expressions of mourning that were typical for the ancient Near East. **Shaved heads and cut-off beards** (verse 2) were extreme acts of grief, since the beard was a sign of male honor and dignity. Voluntarily removing it signaled total despair. The law in Leviticus 19:27 forbade Israelites from such practices, but they were widespread among the Moabites. **Sackcloth on the streets and rooftops** (verse 3) shows collective mourning—rooftops were flat and used for social and religious activities. That the entire population dressed in sackcloth and wept in these public places indicates a national catastrophe.

## Key Terms

The expression **עֶגְלַת שְׁלִשִׁיָּה** ("eglat shelishijja", verse 5) is disputed. It may mean "three-year-old heifer" as a description of Zoar (young, strong, unyoked), or it could be a place name. Some translations interpret it to mean that Zoar has never been subjugated—like a young heifer that has not borne the yoke. The verb **נִדְמָה** (verse 1) means "to be destroyed/annihilated" (from the root ד-מ-ה), but likely creates a wordplay with silence/stillness (ד-מ-מ): the cities are both physically annihilated and silenced in the stillness of death.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Mesha Stele** (also called the Moabite Stone), discovered at Dibon in 1868, is directly relevant to this chapter. The stele, from about 840 B.C., was written by King Mesha of Moab and mentions several of the same cities as Isaiah 15: Dibon, Medeba, Nebo, Jahaz, and Horonaim. The stele describes how Mesha rebuilt cities and fortifications in Moab, confirming the historical existence and significance of these places. The site of Kir-Moab (Kerak) has been archaeologically investigated and shows continuous settlement from the Bronze Age, with substantial fortifications that confirm the city's role as a refuge in times of crisis.
