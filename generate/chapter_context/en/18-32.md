# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 32

## Characters

Chapter 32 introduces a new character: Elihu, son of Barakel the Buzite, from the family of Ram. This is the most detailed genealogical introduction in the book of Job. Buz is mentioned in Genesis 22:21 as a son of Nahor, Abraham’s brother, which places Elihu within the broader Abrahamic family circle but outside the patriarchs of Israel. The identity of Ram is uncertain; some scholars link the name to Aram or the Arameans, but this remains speculative. Elihu’s fourfold identification (name, father’s name, clan, and family) follows a pattern typical for important figures in the ancient Near East and signals that he is a significant new voice in the debate.

## Events and Customs

Elihu’s lengthy apology before speaking (vv. 6–22) reflects the deep respect for age that characterized cultures in the ancient Near East. Younger men were not to speak until elders had exhausted their arguments. The expression "I was afraid" (זָחַלְתִּי, *zachalti*) shows physical restraint—literally "I shrank back." This custom is well documented in Egyptian and Mesopotamian wisdom texts, where old age was automatically associated with experience and insight. Elihu’s breaking of this convention therefore requires extensive justification.

## Key Terms

The expression "the breath of Shaddai" (נִשְׁמַת שַׁדַּי, *nishmat Shaddai*) in verse 8 connects to the creation account in Genesis 2:7, where God breathes the breath of life into humanity. Elihu argues here that divine inspiration, not age, grants true insight. The image in verse 19 of a belly like wine without an outlet and new wineskins that will burst refers to the fermentation process, where gas builds up pressure. New wineskins (*ovot chadashim*) were more elastic, but even these would burst under enough pressure—a powerful metaphor for Elihu’s inner compulsion to speak.
