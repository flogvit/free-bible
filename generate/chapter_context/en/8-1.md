# Historical and Cultural Context for Ruth 1

## Characters

**Elimelech** (אֱלִימֶלֶךְ, "My God is king") was a man from Bethlehem in Judah, described as an "Ephrathite" – a designation for inhabitants of the Ephratah area around Bethlehem (cf. Gen 35:19; Mic 5:2). **Naomi** (נָעֳמִי, "my pleasantness" or "the agreeable one") is his wife. Their sons **Mahlon** (מַחְלוֹן, possibly "sickness") and **Chilion** (כִלְיוֹן, possibly "weakness" or "wasting away") have names that can later be read symbolically in light of their early deaths.

**Ruth** (רוּת) is a Moabite woman. The etymology of her name is uncertain but may be related to רְעוּת ("friendship") or the verb רָוָה ("to satisfy" or "to water"). **Orpah** (עָרְפָּה) is the other Moabite daughter-in-law; her name may be connected with עֹרֶף ("nape of the neck"), which takes on symbolic meaning when she turns her back to Naomi.

## Places

**Bethlehem in Judah** (בֵּית לֶחֶם יְהוּדָה) is located about 8 km south of Jerusalem in the Judean highlands. The name means "house of bread" – an ironic contrast to the famine that drives the family away. The addition "in Judah" distinguishes this place from Bethlehem in Zebulun (Josh 19:15).

**The fields of Moab** (שְׂדֵי מוֹאָב) refer to the high plateau east of the Dead Sea, in present-day Jordan. The journey from Bethlehem to Moab involved descending to the Dead Sea (about 400 m below sea level) and then ascending to the Moabite plateau (about 900 m above sea level) – a demanding route estimated at 50–80 km. Traditionally, Moab had an ambivalent relationship with Israel; the Moabites were related through Lot (Gen 19:37), yet also historical enemies.

## Events and Customs

**Famine as a driver of migration** was a known phenomenon in ancient Israel, where agriculture depended on annual rainfall. The high plateau of Moab often received more stable precipitation and could have better harvests when Judah suffered from drought.

**Marriage between Israelites and Moabites** was controversial. Deut 23:3–6 forbids Moabites from entering the assembly of the Lord, yet this chapter presents these marriages without direct condemnation. Later Jewish tradition debated whether the prohibition applied only to men or also to women.

**Naomi’s release of her daughters-in-law** (vv. 8–9) reflects that widows without sons normally returned to their father’s house ("your mother’s house" is an unusual expression, possibly hinting at the female sphere in marriage arrangements). Naomi’s blessing and kiss mark a formal dissolution of family obligations.

## Key Terms

**Hesed** (חֶסֶד, v. 8) is a central biblical term denoting faithful love, loyal kindness, or covenant loyalty. Naomi acknowledges that her daughters-in-law have shown hesed both toward their deceased husbands and toward herself. This concept becomes key to the entire book’s theology.

**Yevamah** (יְבִמְתֵּךְ, v. 15) is translated "your sister-in-law" and is related to levirate marriage (yibbum), where a brother’s widow was to be taken as wife by the surviving brother to raise offspring for the deceased (Deut 25:5–10). Naomi’s argument in vv. 11–13 plays on this custom – she has no more sons to offer.

**Shaddai** (שַׁדַּי, vv. 20–21), often translated "the Almighty," is an ancient divine name associated with the patriarchal narratives. Naomi’s use of this name in her lament underscores her experience of God’s sovereign yet painful intervention in her life.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

**The time reference** "at the beginning of barley harvest" (בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים, v. 22) places the arrival in March–April, a period of renewed hope and food after winter. This creates a literary contrast to the famine that opened the chapter.

**Naomi’s name change** from נָעֳמִי ("pleasantness") to מָרָא ("bitter," v. 20) is a wordplay summarizing the chapter’s theme of loss and suffering. Her use of both יהוה and שַׁדַּי in her lament (vv. 20–21) emphasizes that she holds God responsible for her fate.

**Ruth’s declaration** (vv. 16–17) contains a poetic structure with four parallel statements culminating in an oath by the Lord’s name – remarkable for a Moabite woman.
