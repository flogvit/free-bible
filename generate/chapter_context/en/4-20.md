# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 20

## People

**Miriam** dies and is buried in Kadesh in this chapter (v. 1). She was the sister of Moses and Aaron and played a significant role in Israel's history—she led the women in song after the exodus from Egypt (Exod 15:20-21) and is called a prophetess. Her death marks the end of an era. The text places the water shortage immediately following her passing (vv. 1-2), which has given rise to traditional interpretations suggesting a connection between the two events.

**Eleazar** (אֶלְעָזָר), Aaron's son, takes over as high priest in this chapter (vv. 25-28). The transfer of the priestly garments from father to son represents the first orderly succession in the Israelite priesthood. Eleazar would later serve under Joshua and play an important role in the division of the land.

## Places

**Kadesh** (קָדֵשׁ) in the Wilderness of Zin was a major oasis in the northern Negev region, likely identified with today's Ein el-Qudeirat or the nearby Ein Qadis. The name means "holy," and the place served as Israel's main base for much of the wilderness wanderings. Archaeological excavations at Ein el-Qudeirat have uncovered remains from several periods, including a fortress from the Iron Age, although no direct connection to Moses' time has been established.

**Mount Hor** (הֹר הָהָר, "mountain of the mountain"), where Aaron died, is described as being located at the border of Edom (v. 23). The traditional identification with Jebel Harun near Petra dates from Byzantine times, but modern scholars also suggest Tell el-Kheleifeh or other sites closer to Kadesh. The Hebrew expression may indicate a prominent or holy mountain.

**The King's Highway** (דֶּרֶךְ הַמֶּלֶךְ, v. 17) was an important trade route running north-south through Transjordan, from Damascus to the Gulf of Aqaba. This road is known from several ancient sources and was crucial for commerce and military movements in the region. Edom's control over this route explains their reluctance to allow a large crowd to pass through.

## Events and Customs

**The Water Miracle at Meribah** parallels an earlier event at Rephidim (Exod 17:1-7), but with significant differences. Here, Moses was to *speak* to the rock, not strike it. The Hebrew word for "rock" is also different: סֶלַע (sela – a large, steep cliff) here, versus צוּר (tsur – a solid bedrock) in Exodus. Moses' action of striking the rock twice instead of speaking to it resulted in him and Aaron being denied entry into the Promised Land.

**Priestly Succession** is described in detail in verses 25-28. The removal of Aaron's priestly garments and their clothing upon Eleazar before the entire congregation ensured a public and orderly transfer of the high priesthood. This ceremony established precedent for how priestly authority was to be passed on in Israel.

**Thirty Days of Mourning** for Aaron (v. 29) corresponds to the same period later observed for Moses (Deut 34:8). This was the longest mourning period in Israelite tradition, reserved for the most significant leaders. The phrase "all the house of Israel" emphasizes that the grief was universal.

## Key Terms

**Merrim Meribah** (מֵי מְרִיבָה) means "waters of contention" or "waters of strife" (v. 13). The root ר-י-ב means to contend or bring a legal case, and the place name recalls the people's dispute with the Lord. The verb is used in a legal sense and implies that the people "brought a lawsuit" against Moses and God.

**"To be gathered to his people"** (יֵאָסֵף אֶל־עַמָּיו, v. 24) is an idiomatic expression for death that suggests reunion with one's ancestors. The phrase presupposes a belief in continued existence after death and is used concerning the patriarchs and other significant figures.

**Rebels** (הַמֹּרִים, v. 10), which Moses uses to describe the people, can be translated as "rebels" or "stubborn ones." This strong expression reveals Moses' frustration and may be part of his sin—he spoke in anger rather than honoring God before the people.
