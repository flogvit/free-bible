# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 11

## People

**Barnabas** (Βαρναβᾶς) emerges as a key figure in this chapter. He was a Levite from Cyprus (Acts 4:36). His Cypriot background made him especially suited to evaluate what happened in Antioch, where Cypriots were among the pioneers (v. 20). The name Barnabas means "son of encouragement" (υἱὸς παρακλήσεως), and his role as an encourager is clearly seen when he "urged them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast purpose" (v. 23).

**Saul** (Σαῦλος) was brought by Barnabas from Tarsus (v. 25) to Antioch, where the two worked together for a whole year teaching the church (v. 26). Together they later brought relief to the elders in Jerusalem (v. 30).

**Agabus** (Ἅγαβος) is introduced as a prophet from Jerusalem (vv. 27–28). He also appears later in Acts 21:10–11. His prophecy about a famine "over all the world" (ἐφʼ ὅλην τὴν οἰκουμένην) was fulfilled during the reign of Emperor Claudius (AD 41–54). The Roman historian Suetonius mentions repeated famines under Claudius's rule, and Josephus describes a severe famine in Judea around AD 46–47.

**The six brothers** (οἱ ἓξ ἀδελφοὶ οὗτοι, v. 12) were Jewish believers from Joppa who accompanied Peter to Cornelius's house. They served as witnesses in Peter's defense before the church in Jerusalem and confirmed that the Holy Spirit had fallen upon Gentiles.

## Places

**Antioch** in Syria was the third largest city in the Roman Empire after Rome and Alexandria, with an estimated population of 250,000–500,000. The city lay on the Orontes River, about 25 kilometers from the Mediterranean Sea. Antioch was known for its cosmopolitan character, with a large Jewish population that had lived there since the city's founding by Seleucus I around 300 BC. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of magnificent colonnaded streets and a large Jewish synagogue. The city's mixed population of Greeks, Syrians, Jews, and Romans created a natural environment for the spread of the gospel to non-Jews.

**Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Cyrene** are mentioned as places from which those who spread the gospel originated. Phoenicia was the coastal strip north of Israel (modern-day Lebanon), Cyprus was an island in the eastern Mediterranean with a significant Jewish diaspora, and Cyrene was a Greek colony in North Africa (modern-day Libya) with a large Jewish population. Simon of Cyrene, who carried Jesus's cross, and Lucius of Cyrene, mentioned in Acts 13:1, testify to the connection between Cyrene and the early church.

**Tarsus**, where Barnabas brought Saul (v. 25), was the capital of the province of Cilicia and an important cultural and philosophical center. The city was known for its Stoic school of philosophy and served as a crossroads for trade between East and West.

## Events and Customs

**Table fellowship and circumcision** stand at the heart of the conflict in vv. 2–3. The expression οἱ ἐκ περιτομῆς ("those of the circumcision") refers to Jewish believers who believed that the ritual boundaries from the Mosaic Law still applied. The accusation "you went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them" (v. 3) shows that table fellowship was considered the most compromising issue. In Jewish tradition, sharing a meal expressed fellowship and acceptance. Later rabbinic literature contains detailed rules about eating together with Gentiles, mainly due to concerns about ritual impurity and non-kosher food.

**The first use of the name "Christians"** (Χριστιανούς) occurred in Antioch (v. 26). The word is formed according to a Latin pattern with the suffix -ianus, meaning "follower of." Similar formations such as "Herodians" and "Caesarians" were common. This suggests that the term came from the Greco-Roman population in Antioch who identified these believers as a distinct group connected to "Christos" (Messiah). It marks an important turning point where believers were no longer seen merely as a Jewish sect.

## Key Terms

**Τὰ ἔθνη** ("the Gentiles" or "the nations") in vv. 1 and 18 is the Greek word corresponding to the Hebrew *goyim*. In a Jewish context, this marked a fundamental distinction between Israel and all other peoples. The recognition in v. 18 that "God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance unto life" represents a theological breakthrough, where salvation is now explicitly acknowledged as universal.

**Ἑλληνιστάς** (v. 20) is usually translated "Hellenists" or "Greek-speaking Jews." In this context, it likely refers to Gentiles with Greek culture and language, in contrast to Greek-speaking Jews (as in Acts 6:1). Some textual witnesses read "Ἕλληνας" ("Greeks"), which would make the beginning of the mission to the Gentiles even clearer.

**Χρηματίσαι** (v. 26), translated "were called," actually means "to receive an official name" or "to be publicly designated as." This suggests that the name "Christians" became a public, recognized designation, not merely a nickname.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The famine under Claudius** is confirmed by several ancient sources. Josephus (Antiquitates 20.51–53, 101) describes how Queen Helena of Adiabene bought grain from Egypt and obtained figs from Cyprus to help those in distress in Jerusalem during this famine. Suetonius (Claudius 18) and Dio Cassius (60.11) also mention food shortages during this period. An inscription from Antioch mentions a certain Tiberius Claudius Cogidubnus as "curator of the grain supply" under Claudius, confirming that grain shortage was a widespread problem.

This external confirmation provides a reliable chronological marker for the events in the chapter and shows that Luke's historical notes align with contemporary sources. The collection for the poor in Judea (vv. 29–30) also demonstrates the early church's practice of economic solidarity across geographical and ethnic boundaries.
