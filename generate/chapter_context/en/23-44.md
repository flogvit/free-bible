# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 44

## People

**Cyrus (כוֹרֶשׁ)** is explicitly mentioned in verse 28. Cyrus II (Cyrus the Great) founded the Persian Empire and conquered Babylon in 539 B.C.E. It is remarkable that the prophecy names him as the Lord's "shepherd" who will carry out God's will by rebuilding Jerusalem and the temple. The Cyrus Cylinder, a clay cylinder document from around 539 B.C.E., confirms Cyrus's policy of allowing deported peoples to return home and rebuild their sanctuaries—something that aligns with the biblical account.

**Jeshurun (יְשֻׁרוּן)** in verse 2 is a poetic honorific name for Israel, derived from the root *jšr* ("upright" or "straight"). This name appears elsewhere only in Deuteronomy (32:15; 33:5, 26), making its occurrence here in Isaiah 44:2 especially significant. It expresses Israel's ideal character as God's chosen people.

## Places

**Jerusalem and the cities of Judah** (v. 26) are mentioned prophetically as places that will be rebuilt after destruction. This points to the situation following Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 B.C.E. Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered extensive layers of destruction from this period, as well as evidence of rebuilding during the Persian era, confirming the historical context for the fulfillment of the prophecy.

## Events and Customs

**Idol production** (vv. 9–20) is described in detail as a satirical portrayal of craft-based idol making. Isaiah depicts the entire process: the blacksmith works at the coals, the woodcarver uses a measuring line, chalk, plane, compasses, and other tools. The description reflects actual craftsmanship practices in the ancient Near East. From Mesopotamia and Egypt we know of rituals called "mouth opening" (*mīs pî*) and "mouth washing" (*pīt pî*), in which newly made images were "awakened to life" through ceremonies. Isaiah's polemic completely rejects such notions by emphasizing that idols remain lifeless wood—the same material used for fuel and cooking.

**Writing on the hand** (v. 5)—the expression that someone "writes on their hand: 'For the Lord'" (יִכְתֹּב יָדוֹ לַיהוָה) may refer to a practice of tattooing or marking belonging, possibly parallel to slave branding or cultic consecration known from surrounding cultures. Here it symbolizes a voluntary and public confession of allegiance to the God of Israel.

## Key Terms

**Goel (גֹּאֵל)**—"redeemer" or "deliverer" (vv. 6, 22, 24)—is a central legal term from Israelite kinship law. A *goel* was the nearest relative who had the duty to buy back property, redeem a kinsman from debt slavery, or avenge blood guilt. When God is called Israel's *goel*, it expresses his commitment as Israel's closest "kinsman" to free the people from exile—a redemption from Babylonian captivity.

**Tsur (צוּר)**—"rock" (v. 8)—is used as a metaphor for God as an unchanging refuge and foundation, in contrast to the unstable idols. The image was especially powerful in Israel's mountainous landscape where rocks provided protection and stability.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Cyrus Cylinder** (British Museum) is directly relevant to verse 28. This clay document from 539 B.C.E. describes Cyrus's conquest of Babylon and his policy of religious tolerance. The text states that Cyrus allowed deported peoples to return home and rebuild their temples—the same policy that enabled the Jews' return and the rebuilding of the temple. Although the cylinder does not mention the Jews specifically, it confirms the historical background for the prophecy and shows that Cyrus's approach was part of a deliberate political strategy.
