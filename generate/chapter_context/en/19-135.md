# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 135

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical locations with historical significance. **Zion** and **Jerusalem** (v. 21) are highlighted as the Lord’s dwelling place, reflecting the theology that God chose this site for His temple. **Bashan** (v. 11) was a fertile highland region east of the Jordan, ruled by King Og who was defeated during the conquest. The area was conquered during the wilderness wanderings and assigned to half the tribe of Manasseh. **Egypt** (vv. 8–9) represents the land of slavery and the place of God’s mighty acts, a central theme in Israel’s salvation history.

## Events and Customs

The chapter refers to **temple service** by mentioning those who "stand in the house of the Lord" and in its "courts" (v. 2). This describes the Levites’ role as servants in the sanctuary. Praise was organized among various groups—the house of Israel, the house of Aaron, the house of Levi, and all who fear God (vv. 19–20)—representing the entire people within a liturgical structure. The chapter’s form, alternating between calls to praise and reasons for praise, suggests use in public worship, possibly during festival celebrations.

## Key Terms

**Segullah** (סְגֻלָּה, v. 4) is often translated as "treasured possession" or "special property." The word denotes a king’s private wealth or special possession, distinct from the general resources of the kingdom. When Israel is called God’s "segullah," it expresses an intimate relationship of ownership. **Otot u-moftim** (אֹתוֹת וּמֹפְתִים, v. 9), "signs and wonders," is a fixed phrase describing God’s miraculous interventions in Egypt, especially the plagues.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The chapter’s polemic against idols (vv. 15–18) reflects similar texts from the ancient Near East. Babylonian texts describe how divine images were "born" through rituals in which their mouths were opened to give them life. Biblical satire turns this upside down: the images have mouths, but cannot speak. This type of critique of religion also appears in Isaiah 44 and Jeremiah 10, which may place the final form of the chapter in the exilic or early post-exilic period.
