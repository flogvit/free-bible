# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 21

## People

**The King of Arad** was a Canaanite ruler in the Negev region. Arad is well documented archaeologically, with a significant Canaanite city from the Early Bronze Age. His attack on Israel represents the first confrontation with the inhabitants of Canaan after the spies were sent out a generation earlier.

**Sihon, king of the Amorites**, ruled from Heshbon and had previously conquered substantial Moabite territories north of the Arnon River. He was a powerful regional ruler whose kingdom extended from the Arnon to the Jabbok. His refusal of passage and subsequent defeat became a paradigmatic example in Israelite tradition (referred to repeatedly in Psalms 135–136, Deuteronomy).

**Og, king of Bashan**, was known as one of the last "Rephaim" – a people associated with giants in Canaanite tradition. His capital, Edrei (modern Der'a in Syria), was strategically important. Bashan was famous for its fertile oak trees and fat cattle.

## Places

**Arad** is located in the eastern Negev, about 30 km northeast of Be'er-Sheva. Archaeological excavations have uncovered both a Canaanite city from the Early Bronze Age and a later Israelite fortress. Tell Arad shows clear destruction layers that may correspond to biblical accounts.

**Mount Hor**, where Aaron died (Numbers 20:22–29), was the starting point for the detour around Edom described in this chapter (v. 4). Traditionally identified with Jebel Harun near Petra, although some scholars suggest Jebel Madeirah further north.

**The Arnon Valley** (modern Wadi Mujib in Jordan) is a dramatic gorge that cuts down to the Dead Sea. It served as a natural boundary between Moab in the south and Amorite/Israelite territory in the north. The valley is approximately 3 km wide and up to 500 meters deep – an formidable geographical barrier.

**Heshbon** (modern Hesban in Jordan) was Sihon's capital. Archaeological excavations have found settlement traces from the Bronze Age, although the most significant finds are from the Iron Age and later periods.

## Events and Customs

**The Herem Vow** (vv. 2–3): Israel made a vow to "devote [cities] to destruction" (הַחֲרַמְתִּי, *haḥaramti*) the Canaanite cities. This meant total destruction as a religious act – no plunder or survivors were to be taken; instead, everything was dedicated to the Lord through annihilation. The place name Horma (חָרְמָה) preserves the memory of this vow.

**The Bronze Serpent** (vv. 8–9): The making of a serpent on a pole has parallels in surrounding cultures. In Egypt, the uraeus serpent was a protective symbol, and bronze serpents have been found in Egyptian temples in the Timna area (see "Chapter-Specific Finds"). The Hebrew wordplay between נָחָשׁ (*naḥash*, serpent) and נְחֹשֶׁת (*neḥoshet*, copper/bronze) is literarily significant. This serpent later became an object of idolatry and was destroyed by King Hezekiah (2 Kings 18:4).

**The Song of the Well** (vv. 17–18) is one of the oldest poetic fragments in the Bible. It celebrates a well dug by "princes" and "nobles" with their staffs – possibly a ritual act where leaders symbolically dedicated the water source. Such well songs are known from Bedouin culture in the Middle East up to modern times.

## Key Terms

**שָׂרָף** (*saraf*, vv. 6, 8): The word means "burning" and describes the serpents that caused burning pain through their bites. The same root is used for seraphim in Isaiah 6. The Negev desert has several venomous snake species, including sand vipers and horned vipers, whose venom causes intense burning pain and tissue necrosis.

**נֵס** (*nes*, vv. 8–9): Translated as "pole" or "banner," it refers to a high pole visible from a distance. The same word is used for military standards and signal poles.

**הַמֹּשְׁלִים** (*hammoshlim*, v. 27): "Those who utter proverbs/songs." This refers to professional poets or singers who preserved and performed poetic compositions about historical events.

**דֶּרֶךְ הַמֶּלֶךְ** (*derekh hammelekh*, v. 22): The "King's Highway" was an established trade route running north-south through Transjordan. It was a major international thoroughfare from the Gulf of Aqaba to Damascus.

## Chapter-Specific Finds

**Bronze Serpents from Timna**: In the Egyptian Hathor temple at the Timna mines (northern Negev), a gilded bronze serpent from the Late Bronze Age has been found, precisely from the period when the Israelites wandered in this area. This shows that serpent symbolism was widespread in the region and provides archaeological context for the account of the bronze serpent.

**The Reference to "The Book of the Wars of the Lord"** (v. 14): This quotation from an otherwise unknown collection called "The Book of the Wars of the Lord" (סֵפֶר מִלְחֲמֹת יְהוָה) testifies that early Israelite literary collections existed which have not been preserved. The fragmentary quote suggests epic war poetry celebrating the Lord's intervention in Israel's battles.

**The Heshbon Poem** (vv. 27–30): This victory poem about the conquest of Heshbon from Moab is likely an ancient Amorite victory song that the Israelites adopted. Ironically, the poem that originally celebrated Sihon's victory over Moab now serves as an introduction to the story of Sihon's own defeat by Israel.
