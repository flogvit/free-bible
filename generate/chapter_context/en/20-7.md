# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 7

## Events and Customs

Chapter 7 offers a rare glimpse into urban life in ancient Israel, particularly nighttime activity in the city streets. The scene unfolds at twilight and in the darkness of night (בְּנֶֽשֶׁף־בְּעֶ֥רֶב יוֹם, v. 9), a time that underscores the secretive nature of the encounter. The description of the young man wandering "at the corner" and taking the path toward her house (v. 8), and the woman who lurks "in the street", "in the squares" and "at every corner" (v. 12), reflects the typical layout of Israelite cities with narrow streets, open plazas, and street corners serving as natural meeting places. Windows with latticework (אֶשְׁנָב, *eshnav*, v. 6) were common in fine houses and allowed people to observe street life while remaining hidden.

The woman's reference to "peace offerings" (זִבְחֵי שְׁלָמִים, *zivhei shelamim*, v. 14) is culturally significant. The peace offering was one of the few sacrifices where the offerer could eat part of the meat, and this had to be consumed on the same day or the following day (Lev 7:15-16). She thus uses a religious obligation as a pretext for an extravagant feast—a perversion of something holy that would have been especially shocking to Israelite readers.

## Key Terms

The expression "foreign woman" (אִשָּׁה זָרָה, *ishah zarah*) and "the foreigner" (נָכְרִיָּה, *nokhriyyah*) in verse 5 have been the subject of much discussion. These terms can mean a foreigner by nationality, but in Proverbs they are used primarily to describe a woman who is "foreign" in a moral sense—one who stands outside the boundaries of the marriage covenant, whether she is unmarried, married to another man, or a prostitute.

The expression "dressed like a prostitute" (שִׁית זוֹנָה, *shit zonah*, v. 10) suggests a specific garment or appearance that signaled sexual availability. Although we do not know all the details, this indicates that ancient societies had visual codes to identify prostitutes.

The luxurious items described by the woman—Egyptian linen (אֵטוּן מִצְרָיִם, *etun mitsrayim*), myrrh, aloes and cinnamon (vv. 16-17)—reflect the international trade that characterized Israel, especially during the monarchy. Egyptian linen was particularly prized for its fineness. Myrrh and cinnamon came from Arabia and East Africa via trade routes passing through Israel, while aloe (אֲהָלִים, *ahalim*) likely refers to the aromatic aloewood tree from India or Southeast Asia. These exotic goods emphasize the woman's attempt to create an atmosphere of wealth and seduction.
