# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 4

## Events and Customs

Chapter 4 introduces the **sin offering** (חַטָּאת, *chattat*), a sacrifice specifically intended for unintentional sins. This differs from the burnt offering and the peace offering described in previous chapters. The ritual includes several distinctive elements: laying hands (סָמַךְ) on the animal’s head, sprinkling blood before the veil, and applying blood to the horns of the altar of incense. Blood played a central cleansing role—it was sprinkled seven times (a number symbolizing completeness) toward the veil that concealed the Most Holy Place.

A remarkable practice was that the remains of the sin offering for the priest and the congregation were to be burned **outside the camp** at a clean place near the ash heap (שֶׁפֶךְ הַדֶּשֶׁן). This contrasts with other offerings where the meat was eaten. Burning outside the camp signified that the sin offering carried away impurity from the sanctuary. The "clean place" outside the camp represented an area that was cultically neutral—neither holy nor unclean.

## Key Terms

The term **בִּשְׁגָגָה** (*bishgagah*) – "by mistake" or "unintentionally" – is fundamental to the entire chapter. This Hebrew expression describes sins committed without intent, as opposed to deliberate rebellion against God. This distinction was important in Israelite legal practice and theology: unintentional sins could be atoned for through sacrifice, while intentional sins ("with a raised hand") required stricter consequences.

The title **הַכֹּהֵן הַמָּשִׁיחַ** (*hakohen hamashiach*) – "the anointed priest" – refers to the high priest who was anointed with holy oil at his consecration. The word *mashiach* (anointed) is the origin of the term "Messiah." That the priest’s sin led to "guilt for the people" (לְאַשְׁמַת הָעָם) shows his representative role—his sin affected the entire community, and therefore required the most valuable sacrifice animal (a bull).

## People

The chapter mentions four categories of persons who could sin: **the anointed priest**, **the whole congregation of Israel**, **the leader** (נָשִׂיא, *nasi*), and **an ordinary person from the people of the land** (מֵעַם הָאָרֶץ). This hierarchical division reflects the structure of Israelite society. The leader (*nasi*) was a tribal chief or ruler with political authority, distinct from the religious leadership role held by the priest.

The value of the sacrifice animal corresponded to the person’s status: the high priest and the congregation had to offer a bull (the most expensive), the leader a male goat (שְׂעִיר עִזִּים זָכָר – a male animal), and ordinary people either a female goat (שְׂעִירַת עִזִּים) or a lamb (כֶּבֶשׂ), both female animals (נְקֵבָה תְמִימָה). The elders of the congregation (זִקְנֵי הָעֵדָה, verse 15) performed the laying on of hands on behalf of the people, showing their function as representatives in cultic actions.
