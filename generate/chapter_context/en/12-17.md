## People

**Hoshea son of Elah** (הוֹשֵׁעַ בֶּן־אֵלָה) was the last king of Israel and reigned for nine years (c. 732/731–722 B.C.). Unlike many of his predecessors, he is described as less evil, although he still "did what was evil in the Lord’s sight" (v. 2). He came to power through a coup against Pekah and was initially installed as an Assyrian vassal. His attempt to ally with Egypt and rebel against Assyria led directly to his imprisonment and the fall of the kingdom.

**Shalmaneser V** (שַׁלְמַנְאֶסֶר) was king of Assyria from 727–722 B.C. He began the siege of Samaria but likely died before it was completed. Assyrian sources confirm his campaign to the west. His successor, Sargon II, took credit for the fall of Samaria in his annals.

**So, king of Egypt** (סוֹא מֶלֶךְ־מִצְרַיִם) is debated among scholars. The name may refer to Pharaoh Osorkon IV of the 22nd dynasty or to a local ruler in the Nile Delta. Some believe "So" is a place name (Sais in the Nile Delta) or a title rather than a personal name.

## Places

**Samaria** (שֹׁמְרוֹן) was the capital of the northern kingdom, founded by Omri around 885–880 B.C. The city was strategically located on a hill and heavily fortified. The siege lasted three years (v. 5), underscoring the strength of its defenses.

**The places of deportation** mentioned in verse 6 include Halah, Habor by the river Gozan, and the cities of Media. Habor (the Khabur River) is located in present-day northeastern Syria, while Media was situated in northwestern Iran. The Assyrians placed deported peoples in outlying regions of their empire to break up national identities and prevent rebellion.

**The places of origin for the new inhabitants** (v. 24)—Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim—represent areas under Assyrian control. Cuthah lay south of Babylon and was known for the god Nergal. Hamath was an important city in Syria. This population exchange was typical Assyrian policy.

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** (vv. 17, 31) is mentioned as one of the sins that led to exile. The Israelites "made their sons and daughters pass through the fire," a practice associated with the cult of Molech. The people from Sepharvaim continued this practice with their gods Adrammelech and Anammelech. This custom was known in the region and is repeatedly condemned in the Old Testament.

**Syncretism** among the new inhabitants (vv. 29–33) describes how each ethnic group continued to worship their native gods while learning to "fear the Lord." This created a mixed religion that the text strongly criticizes. The phrase "they feared the Lord and served their own gods" (v. 33) summarizes this religious compromise.

## Key Terms

**Gillulim** (גִּלֻּלִים) in verse 12 is a derogatory term for idols. The word is likely derived from the root גלל, which can mean "roll" or be associated with excrement, expressing contempt for idolatry.

**Bamot** (בָּמוֹת), "high places," are repeatedly mentioned as central to Israel’s sin. These were cult sites on elevated ground where both legitimate Yahweh worship and syncretistic practices occurred.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Sargon II’s prism inscriptions and palace reliefs at Khorsabad mention the conquest of Samaria. He claims to have deported 27,290 people and reorganized the province. This confirms the biblical account, although it remains uncertain whether Sargon or Shalmaneser actually completed the conquest.

Assyrian administrative documents from this period mention Israelite names among the deportees, confirming that the population of the northern kingdom was scattered throughout the Assyrian Empire.
