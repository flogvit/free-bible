# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 22

## People

**David and Solomon** stand at the center of this chapter as representatives of two different eras in Israel's history. David is portrayed as the warrior king who secured the kingdom's borders through numerous military campaigns, while Solomon is introduced as the king of peace. The Hebrew name שְׁלֹמֹה (Shelomoh) is linked in verse 9 to the words שָׁלוֹם (shalom, "peace") and שֶׁקֶט (sheqet, "quietness"), describing the time when he will reign. David's description of Solomon as "נַעַר וָרָךְ" (na'ar varakh, "young and inexperienced/delicate") reflects a royal tradition in which an older king thoroughly prepares his successor.

**The Sidonians and Tyrians** (verse 4) are mentioned as suppliers of cedar wood. This reflects the established trade relations between Israel and the Phoenician city-states, which would become even more prominent during Solomon's temple construction.

## Events and Customs

**The use of foreigners as forced laborers** (verse 2) reflects a common practice in the ancient Near East. The word "גֵּרִים" (gerim) refers to immigrants or foreigners living in the land. These were organized as stonecutters to prepare dressed stones ("אַבְנֵי גָזִית" (avnei gazit)). Such forced labor was widespread in Egyptian and Mesopotamian building projects, and Israel adopted similar administrative structures for large state construction projects.

**The reason David was not allowed to build the temple** (verse 8) – that he had shed much blood – represents a theological principle that the sanctuary should be built during a time of peace. The book of Chronicles thus highlights an Israelite understanding of the relationship between war, cultic purity, and sacred architecture.

## Key Terms

**"Kikkar"** (כִּכָּר) – the talent – was the largest unit of weight in antiquity, approximately 30–35 kg. The amounts David gives in verse 14 (100,000 talents of gold and 1 million talents of silver) are astronomical and likely represent an idealized portrayal of the immense wealth required to build a house worthy of the Lord's name. Such exaggerated numbers were common in ancient royal inscriptions to emphasize the greatness of a project.

**"Mikdash"** – verse 19 uses "מִקְדָּשׁ" (mikdash, "sanctuary") as a designation for the temple. This word emphasizes the character of the place as a holy, set-apart space, in contrast to the movable tabernacle (mishkan) from the wilderness wanderings.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Phoenician areas have confirmed the extensive cedar trade described in verse 4. Cedar from Lebanon was the most sought-after building material throughout the ancient Near East, used in Egyptian, Assyrian, and Babylonian palaces. Inscriptions from several ancient kings mention the import of cedar as a sign of wealth and power. Phoenician city-states such as Sidon and Tyre controlled this trade and developed advanced techniques for transporting timber along the coast and into the interior of the land.
