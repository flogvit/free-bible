# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 16

## People

Romans 16 contains the longest list of greetings in Paul's letters, with around 26–28 named individuals. **Phoebe** (vv. 1–2) is described as a **διάκονος** (diakonos) of the church at Cenchreae and as a **προστάτις** (prostatis, "protector/patron"). This latter term was a legal term in Greco-Roman culture for a wealthy person who provided financial support and legal protection. Phoebe was likely the letter carrier who delivered Romans.

**Prisca (Priscilla) and Aquila** (vv. 3–5) are known from Acts 18. They were expelled from Rome under Emperor Claudius's edict (around 49 AD) and met Paul in Corinth. Now they had returned to Rome after Claudius's death (54 AD) and led a house church. **Andronicus and Junia** (v. 7) are described as "outstanding among the apostles" (ἐπίσημοι ἐν τοῖς ἀποστόλοις) and were Christians before Paul himself. Junia is most likely a woman's name, indicating that women held prominent roles in the early church.

**Tertius** (v. 22) identifies himself as the one who physically wrote down the letter (ὁ γράψας τὴν ἐπιστολὴν). He is the only one of Paul's secretaries to name himself and send his own personal greeting in the letters.

**Erastus** (v. 23) is called "the city treasurer" (οἰκονόμος τῆς πόλεως), an administrative position responsible for public funds. An inscription found in Corinth from the first century mentions an Erastus who funded a sidewalk as gratitude for his office, possibly the same person.

## Places

**Cenchreae** (v. 1) was the eastern port city of Corinth, about 11 kilometers from the center. The city lay on the Saronic Gulf and handled trade with the Aegean Sea and the eastern Mediterranean. Archaeological excavations have revealed harbor structures and sanctuaries. The fact that there was a Christian church there shows that the gospel spread early to trading centers around Corinth. According to Acts 18:18, Paul is said to have cut his hair there in connection with a vow.

## Events and Customs

**The holy kiss** (v. 16, φίλημα ἅγιον) was a liturgical practice in the early churches. In Greco-Roman culture, kissing was a common greeting among family members and close friends. The Christian practice marked believers as a spiritual family and was used in worship settings. Justin Martyr (around 150 AD) describes how the kiss of peace was exchanged before communion.

**House churches** (vv. 5, 14–15) were the norm for early Christianity. Without their own church buildings, believers gathered in private homes, often owned by wealthy members. Romans 16 suggests several such house churches in Rome, which explains the diversity and potential tensions within the Roman Christian community.

**The warning against those who cause divisions** (vv. 17–20) breaks with the warm tone of the greetings and warns against those who create "divisions" (διχοστασίας) and "stumbling blocks" (σκάνδαλα) contrary to the teaching the church has received. Paul describes these people with the powerful expression that they "serve their own appetites" (τῇ ἑαυτῶν κοιλίᾳ) and use "smooth talk and flattery" (χρηστολογίας καὶ εὐλογίας) to deceive the simple. The promise that "the God of peace will soon crush Satan under their feet" (v. 20) connects to the creation account (Genesis 3:15).

## Key Terms

The term **διάκονος** (diakonos, v. 1) used for Phoebe is the same word Paul uses for his own service and for other leaders like Apollos (1 Corinthians 3:5). It means "servant" or "helper," but in a church context it indicates an recognized role of service. That Paul uses this term for a woman shows that women held official functions in the early churches.

**Συγγενεῖς** (syngeneis, "relatives," vv. 7, 11, 21) can mean either biological relatives or fellow countrymen (Jews). In this context it likely refers to Jewish Christians, showing that Paul's network included both Jewish and Gentile Christian coworkers.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Erastus inscription** from Corinth, found in 1929 near the theater, reads: "Erastus laid this sidewalk at his own expense as thanks for his office as aedile." Although the title of office (aedil vs. oikonomos) is not identical, many scholars believe this may be the same person, since an oikonomos could advance to become an aedil. The inscription dates to the middle of the first century AD and would in that case be a rare archaeological witness to a named person in the New Testament.
