# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 12

## People

**Herod Agrippa I** (Ἡρῴδης ὁ βασιλεύς) is the central figure in this chapter. He was a grandson of Herod the Great and ruled over Judea and Samaria. Josephus describes him as a devout Jew who sought to win the people's favor through strict observance of the law—something that explains his persecution of Christian leaders "because he saw it pleased the Jews" (v. 3).

**James, brother of John** (Ἰάκωβος ὁ ἀδελφὸς Ἰωάννου), belonged to Jesus' innermost circle. He became the first of the twelve apostles to suffer martyrdom (v. 2). His execution by sword (μαχαίρῃ) was a form of capital punishment that, in Jewish tradition, was considered less shameful than stoning.

**James, the Lord's brother** – Another James is mentioned in verse 17, where Peter says: "Report these things to James and the brothers" (Ἀπαγγείλατε Ἰακώβῳ καὶ τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς). This is traditionally identified as James, the Lord's brother, who held a leading position in the Jerusalem church.

**Mary, mother of John Mark**, is mentioned as the owner of the house where the church was gathered (v. 12). The fact that she had a house large enough to accommodate "many" (ἱκανοί) and employed a servant girl (παιδίσκη) named Rhoda indicates considerable wealth. This home likely served as an important meeting place for the early church in Jerusalem.

**Rhoda** (Ῥόδη) was the servant girl who recognized Peter's voice at the gate but, in her joy, forgot to open it for him (vv. 13–14).

**Blastus** (Βλάστος) is described as "the king's chamberlain" (ὁ ἐπὶ τοῦ κοιτῶνος)—a trusted court position with direct access to the ruler. The fact that Tyre and Sidon won him over in order to gain an audience illustrates common practice at Hellenistic courts.

**Barnabas and Saul** are mentioned in verse 25, where they return after completing their mission (πληρώσαντες τὴν διακονίαν), taking John Mark with them.

## Places

**Jerusalem** is the main location of this chapter, where both Peter's imprisonment and the church gathering take place. The prison was likely connected to either the Antonia Fortress or Herod's palace.

**Caesarea** (Καισάρεια) was the Roman administrative city on the coast, built by Herod the Great. Agrippa often resided there, and it was here that he died (vv. 19–23). Josephus places his death in the theater at Caesarea during games held in honor of Emperor Claudius, probably in AD 44.

**Tyre and Sidon** were Phoenician trading cities northwest of Galilee. Their dependence on grain imports from the royal territory (διὰ τὸ τρέφεσθαι αὐτῶν τὴν χώραν ἀπὸ τῆς βασιλικῆς) forced them to seek reconciliation with Agrippa after a conflict (v. 20).

## Events and Customs

**The Days of Unleavened Bread** (ἡμέραι τῶν ἀζύμων) are mentioned in verse 3 as the time frame for Peter's imprisonment. This week-long festival immediately followed Passover and was one of the three major pilgrimage festivals. Herod waited until "after Passover" (μετὰ τὸ πάσχα) to carry out the trial, so as not to profane the feast with an execution—a move that shows his desire to appear devout.

**The Prison Guard System** is described in detail: four squads of four soldiers each (τέσσαρσιν τετραδίοις στρατιωτῶν) covered the watch shifts throughout the day and night. The fact that Peter slept bound between two soldiers with guards at the door (v. 6) represented maximum security according to Roman practice—where guards risked execution if the prisoner escaped (cf. v. 19).

**The King's Robe** (ἐσθὴς βασιλική) worn by Agrippa (v. 21) is described by Josephus as made of silver-woven fabric that glittered spectacularly in the morning sun. The people's cry, "A god's voice, and not a man's!" (Θεοῦ φωνὴ καὶ οὐκ ἀνθρώπου), and Agrippa's acceptance of this homage without giving glory to God led, according to Luke, to his immediate judgment.

## Key Terms

**"It is his angel"** (Ὁ ἄγγελός ἐστιν αὐτοῦ) – When the believers say this in verse 15, it reflects a widespread Jewish belief that each person had an angel who could take on their appearance. This idea also appears in other Jewish texts from that period.

**Σκωληκόβρωτος** (eaten by worms) in verse 23 was a description used for tyrannical rulers in antiquity. Josephus gives a parallel account of Agrippa's death, where the king was struck with severe abdominal pain and died after five days. Luke interprets this theologically as God's judgment on one who did not give glory to God.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Josephus' account in *Antiquities of the Jews* (XIX.343–350) independently confirms several details in this chapter: Agrippa's death at Caesarea during a public event, his magnificent robe, the people's deification of him, and his sudden illness. Although Josephus does not mention Christian persecution, his characterization of Agrippa as a popular king seeking Jewish favor aligns well with Luke's portrayal. Dating Agrippa's death to AD 44 provides us with a secure anchor for the chronology in Acts.
