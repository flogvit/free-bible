# Historical and Cultural Context for Amos 7

## People

**Amaziah, the priest of Bethel** (vv. 10–17), was the high priest at the royal sanctuary in Bethel. His title "priest of Bethel" (כֹּהֵן בֵּית־אֵל) indicates that he was the leading religious official at the Northern Kingdom’s most important shrine. Amaziah represented institutionalized religion closely tied to royal power. His actions show that he prioritized political loyalty: he reported Amos’ message to the king as a "conspiracy" (קָשַׁר, v. 10) rather than receiving it as divine revelation.

**Jeroboam II** (vv. 9–11) is mentioned in the judgment God pronounces through Amos. The prophecy that God would "rise up against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" (v. 9) was distorted by Amaziah to mean that Amos claimed the king himself would die by the sword (v. 11)—a crucial misrepresentation of the message that Amaziah used to portray Amos as a political threat.

## Places

**Bethel** (vv. 10, 13) was one of two royal sanctuaries established by Jeroboam I after the division of the kingdom (1 Kings 12:28–29). The name means "house of God," and the site had deep roots in Israel’s history—it was here that Jacob had his famous vision (Genesis 28:10–22). Amaziah’s description of Bethel as "the king’s sanctuary" (מִקְדַּשׁ־מֶלֶךְ) and "the temple of the kingdom" (בֵית מַמְלָכָה) in verse 13 reveals the tension in the confrontation: Amaziah asserts the site’s royal authority, while Amos asserts the Lord’s authority.

## Events and Customs

**The king’s first crop of grass** (v. 1) refers to a practice whereby the king had the right to the first growth of grass for his horses and cattle. The phrase "after the king’s first crop" (אַחַר גִּזֵּי הַמֶּלֶךְ) points to the late harvest (לֶקֶשׁ) that came in March–April and produced a second yield. If locusts destroyed this, the people would have nothing left for their own use after the king had already taken his share.

**Prophetic groups and professional prophets** (v. 14) were an established institution in Israel. The term "son of a prophet" (בֶן־נָבִיא) referred to members of prophetic communities who lived together, often in connection with sanctuaries, and received payment for their services. Amos’ denial of belonging to this group underscores that his calling came directly from God, independent of religious institutions.

## Key Terms

**Anakh** (אֲנָךְ, vv. 7–8) is a Hebrew word traditionally translated as "plumb line" or "plumb bob." The word occurs only here in the Old Testament, and its meaning is debated. Recent scholarship has suggested it may mean "tin"—that is, that חוֹמַת אֲנָךְ describes a solid tin wall. Regardless of interpretation, the vision symbolizes God’s testing and measuring of the people’s righteousness—and the conclusion is that He will no longer "pass over" them (לֹֽא־אוֹסִיף עוֹד עֲבוֹר לוֹ, v. 8).

**Boles shikmin** (בּוֹלֵס שִׁקְמִים, v. 14), translated "tender of sycamore figs," refers to a special practice in which one would scratch or pierce the fruit of the sycamore fig tree to hasten its ripening. This was low-status work, showing that Amos came from humble circumstances and was not a professional prophet who earned his living through his prophetic activity.
