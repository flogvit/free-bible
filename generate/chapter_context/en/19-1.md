# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 1

## Key Concepts

The psalm opens with the Hebrew word **אַשְׁרֵי** (*'ashre*), often translated as "blessed" or "happy". This word does not describe an emotional state, but rather an objective condition of being blessed—one who experiences God's favor and lives in harmony with His will.

The term **תּוֹרָה** (*tora*) in verse 2 fundamentally means "instruction" or "guidance", and here refers to God's revealed will. In the context of this psalm, it likely includes the five books of Moses, but may also encompass broader wisdom traditions. The verb **יֶהְגֶּה** (*jehge*, "meditate/muse") describes a quiet, murmuring recitation—an active, repetitive immersion in the text that was common practice in ancient Israel.

The contrast between **צַדִּיקִים** (*tsaddiqim*, "the righteous") and **רְשָׁעִים** (*resha'im*, "the wicked") forms the central opposition of the psalm. The righteous person is not morally perfect, but one who directs their life toward God's torah and lives within His covenant relationship.

## Events and Customs

The image of a tree planted by **פַּלְגֵי מָיִם** (*palge majim*, "streams of water") in verse 3 reflects artificial irrigation as practiced in the Middle East. In Israel's dry climate, such irrigation channels were vital for agriculture, and a tree beside these sources had guaranteed access to water year-round. The image of chaff blown away (verse 4) comes from the threshing process, where grain was tossed into the air so that the wind would separate the light chaff from the heavy kernels—a everyday picture that immediately communicated worthlessness and transience.

The reference to **מִשְׁפָּט** (*mishpat*, "judgment/legal case") and **עֲדַת צַדִּיקִים** (*'adat tsaddiqim*, "the assembly of the righteous") in verse 5 may point both to a future divine judgment and to the local court assembly that met at the city gate, where the wicked will have no position or voice.
