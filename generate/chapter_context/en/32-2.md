# Historical and Cultural Context for Jonah 2

## Key Concepts

**Sheol (שְׁאוֹל)**: In verse 3, Jonah uses the phrase "from the belly of Sheol" (מִבֶּטֶן שְׁאוֹל). In Old Testament thought, Sheol was the dwelling place of the dead—a dark, silent underworld beneath the earth where all the dead resided regardless of moral standing. Jonah employs this as a poetic description of his near-death state in the depths of the sea. In the ancient Near East, the sea was often associated with chaos, death, and the powers of the underworld.

**Tehom (תְּהוֹם)**: The word in verse 6, translated "the deep" or "the depths of the sea," resonates with the primeval waters in Genesis 1:2. In Canaanite and Mesopotamian mythology, the deep represented chaotic forces that threatened the ordered creation. As Jonah sinks into tehom, he is symbolically in the realm of anti-creation.

**Holy Temple (הֵיכַל קָדְשֶׁךָ)**: Mentioned in verses 5 and 8, this refers to the temple in Jerusalem as the place where God's presence dwelt. Being cut off from the temple meant being separated from God's presence and the community of worship—a spiritual death in addition to the physical threat.

## Events and Customs

Jonah's prayer follows the structure of an individual thanksgiving psalm, a well-known genre in Israelite worship (cf. Psalms 18, 30, 116). Such psalms typically included: an invocation of God, a description of distress, a confession of God's deliverance, and a promise to offer thanks. That Jonah prays this psalm *from* the fish's belly—before his final rescue—demonstrates faith that thanks God for salvation before it is fully completed.

The vow to fulfill promises and bring thank offerings (verse 10) reflects Israelite sacrificial practice. When a person was in distress, they would often make a vow (נֵדֶר) to bring a thank offering if God intervened. After rescue, they were obligated to fulfill this at the temple, often accompanied by a public testimony of God's deliverance.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The phrase "three days and three nights" (שְׁלֹשָׁה יָמִים וּשְׁלֹשָׁה לֵילוֹת) in verse 1 is a fixed expression in Semitic languages denoting a complete, bounded period—often associated with transition time or trial. Similar expressions appear in the Epic of Gilgamesh and other Mesopotamian texts where three days mark the journey to or from the underworld. In biblical contexts, this period is also used regarding illness and crisis (1 Samuel 30:12; Esther 4:16).
