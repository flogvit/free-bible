# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 17

## People

King Jehoshaphat reigned over Judah from approximately 873–849 B.C. and was one of the most successful kings after the division of the kingdom. His name means "the Lord judges" and reflects his parents' piety. The chapter highlights that he followed "the early ways of David" (verse 3), an expression suggesting an idealized early period in David's reign, before his moral decline. Jehoshaphat inherited a kingdom already strengthened under his father Asa, and he built upon this stability.

The chapter mentions five officials (Ben-Hail, Obadiah, Zechariah, Nethanel, and Micaiah), nine Levites (Shemaiah, Nethaniah, Zebadiah, Asahel, Shemiramoth, Jonathan, Adonijah, Tobijah, and Tob-Adonijah), and two priests (Elishama and Joram) who were sent out to teach the people. This represents a unique organized teaching campaign in ancient Israel, showing how royal authority took responsibility for the religious instruction of the people.

## Places

"The cities of Ephraim" (verse 2) refer to areas in the northern kingdom that Asa had conquered during war against Baasha king of Israel (2 Chron 16:1–6). That Jehoshaphat stationed garrisons there shows that Judah at this time controlled border regions within the territory of the northern kingdom. This demonstrated Judah's strength and required a military presence to maintain control.

## Events and Customs

The teaching journey in verses 7–9 is unique in the Old Testament. That the king sent officials together with Levites and priests to teach "the Book of the Law of the Lord" in all the cities of Judah represents an early form of organized religious education. This pattern—cooperation between royal administration and religious leaders—was unusual in the ancient Near East, where religious instruction was typically limited to temple circles.

The tribute from the Philistines and Arabs (verse 11) shows Jehoshaphat's regional influence. That Arab tribes brought 7,700 rams and 7,700 male goats as tribute reflects their nomadic economy based on livestock herding. Such tribute relationships were common in the ancient Middle East and signaled political submission without full incorporation into the kingdom.

## Key Terms

The expression "his heart was lifted up in the ways of the Lord" (וַיִּגְבַּהּ לִבּוֹ בְּדַרְכֵי יְהוָה, verse 6) uses a Hebrew phrase that usually carries a negative meaning ("proud heart"). Here, however, it is used positively to describe religious zeal and courage to carry out reforms. This is a rare positive use of this formulation in the Bible.

"Biranjot" (בִּירָנִיּוֹת, verse 12), translated as "fortresses" or "strongholds," is a Persian loanword that some scholars believe indicates a later editing of the text. Others argue that the word may have been in use earlier in Semitic languages.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The large army numbers in verses 14–18 (totaling over 1.1 million soldiers) have been the subject of much discussion. Some scholars suggest that the Hebrew word "elef" (אֶלֶף) here may mean "military unit" rather than "thousand," which would yield more realistic figures. Others view the numbers as symbolic expressions of Jehoshaphat's great military strength in the chronicler's theological presentation.
