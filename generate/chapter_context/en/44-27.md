# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 27

## People

**Julius** (Ἰουλίῳ, v. 1) was a centurion from the "Imperial Cohort" (σπείρης Σεβαστῆς). This was likely an auxiliary unit with special duties related to the emperor, possibly responsible for escorting prisoners and carrying reports between provinces and Rome. The name "Sebastes" (Greek for "Augustus") indicates an honorific title. Julius's friendly treatment of Paul (φιλανθρώπως, v. 3) was unusual toward a prisoner and may reflect Paul's Roman citizenship and status.

**Aristarchus from Thessalonica** (Ἀριστάρχου Μακεδόνος Θεσσαλονικέως, v. 2) was a Macedonian from Thessalonica and one of Paul's coworkers who had previously been present during the riot in Ephesus (19:29). His presence on the journey may indicate that he served as Paul's personal servant—a role that allowed him to accompany a prisoner.

## Places

The travel route described in this chapter provides a detailed picture of ancient seafaring in the eastern Mediterranean:

**Sidon** (Σιδῶνα, v. 3) was an important Phoenician port city where Paul was permitted to visit friends (πρὸς τοὺς φίλους).

**Myra in Lycia** (Μύρα τῆς Λυκίας, v. 5) was a hub for the grain fleet traveling from Alexandria to Rome—therefore the centurion found an Alexandrian grain ship there (πλοῖον Ἀλεξανδρῖνον, v. 6).

**Fair Havens** (Καλοὺς Λιμένας, v. 8) on the southern coast of Crete, near the city of Lasea (Λασαία), still exists under the same name and is identified as a small bay. Archaeological investigations confirm that it was poorly suited for wintering (ἀνευθέτου... πρὸς παραχειμασίαν, v. 12), just as the text suggests.

**Phoenix** (Φοίνικα, v. 12) was a harbor on Crete that most wanted to reach for wintering. It is described as a harbor facing southwest and northwest (βλέποντα κατὰ λίβα καὶ κατὰ χῶρον).

**Cauda** (Καῦδα, v. 16) was a small island (νησίον) south of Crete where the crew, sheltered by the island, managed to secure the ship's boat.

**Adriatic Sea** (τῷ Ἀδρίᾳ, v. 27) referred in antiquity not only to today's Adriatic Sea but also included the central Mediterranean between Crete, Malta, Italy, and North Africa.

## Events and Customs

**The Fast** (τὴν νηστείαν, v. 9) refers to Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, which falls in September or October. After this date, sailing was considered risky, and the season normally closed from November to March. The fact that they continued sailing after the fast shows how tight their schedule was.

**Handling the ship during the storm** reflects documented ancient practices: "undergirding" the ship (ὑποζωννύντες, v. 17) meant tightening ropes around the hull to prevent planks from loosening—a known emergency procedure. The fear of **Syrtis** (τὴν Σύρτιν, v. 17) was well-founded; these shallow waters off the coast of Libya were notorious for shipwrecks. Throwing cargo and equipment overboard (vv. 18–19, 38) was standard practice to lighten the ship in emergencies.

**Paul's vision** (vv. 23–26): In the midst of crisis, Paul stood up with a message from an angel of God (ἄγγελος τοῦ θεοῦ), assuring him that all 276 people on board would survive and that he must stand before Caesar (Καίσαρί σε δεῖ παραστῆναι). This prophetic element gave hope to everyone on board.

**The meal before the shipwreck** (vv. 33–36): Paul encouraged everyone to eat after fourteen days of little food. He took bread, thanked God (εὐχαρίστησεν τῷ θεῷ), and broke it—an action reminiscent of the Lord's Supper tradition, but here primarily a practical meal to strengthen them before the coming trial.

**The soldiers' plan to kill the prisoners** (βουλὴ... ἵνα τοὺς δεσμώτας ἀποκτείνωσιν, v. 42) reflects Roman military law: a guard who allowed a prisoner to escape risked death himself. Julius's intervention to save Paul (βουλόμενος διασῶσαι τὸν Παῦλον, v. 43) shows his authority and personal favor toward the apostle.

## Key Terms

**Euraquilo** (v. 14) is a hybrid Greek-Latin word combining "Eurus" (east wind) with "Aquilo" (north wind), meaning a northeasterly hurricane wind. This technical term shows the author's familiarity with sailors' jargon.

**The depth measurements** (v. 28) of twenty and then fifteen fathoms (ὀργυιάς)—about 37 and 27 meters—demonstrate the sailors' navigation technique by sounding the bottom to determine position and danger.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Luke's detailed account has been confirmed by maritime historical studies. The ship that held 276 people (διακόσιαι ἑβδομήκοντα ἕξ, v. 37) and carried grain cargo matches known Alexandrian grain freighters, which could be up to 55 meters long. The route, weather conditions, and nautical decisions are consistent with what we know about ancient navigation and seasonal wind patterns in this sea area. The British admiral James Smith conducted a thorough analysis of the chapter's nautical details in the nineteenth century and concluded that the author must have been an eyewitness with firsthand experience of the journey.
