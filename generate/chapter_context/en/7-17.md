## People

**Micah (מִיכָיְהוּ/מִיכָה)** is the main character in this chapter. His name means "Who is like YHWH?" – an ironic contrast to his actions. He comes from the hill country of Ephraim and establishes a private sanctuary with idols. Micah represents the religious confusion during the period of the judges, where he mixes worship of YHWH with Canaanite practices, believing this will bring blessing.

**The young Levite from Bethlehem** is introduced as a wandering priest without a fixed assignment. The fact that a Levite from the region of Judah lived in Bethlehem as a "foreigner" (גָר) suggests that the Levitical system of cities and support (Numbers 35) was not functioning as prescribed. His willingness to serve in a private cult with idols shows how far religious decay had progressed.

## Places

**The hill country of Ephraim (הַר־אֶפְרָיִם)** was the central highland north of Jerusalem. The fact that these events take place here, far from Shiloh where the tabernacle stood, underscores the decentralized and fragmented state of Israel.

**Bethlehem in Judah** is mentioned as the Levite’s hometown. The city was not one of the designated Levitical cities, confirming that the prescribed system for supporting the Levites had broken down.

## Events and Customs

**The consecration of silver and the idol**: The mother’s vow to "consecrate" (הַקְדֵּשׁ הִקְדַּשְׁתִּי) the silver to YHWH in order to make a carved image (פֶּסֶל) and a cast image (מַסֵּכָה) represents a direct violation of the second commandment (Exodus 20:4). The practice of making idols was widespread in Canaanite religion, and the Israelites adopted this custom despite the prohibition. That only 200 of the 1,100 silver pieces were used for the image raises questions about what happened to the rest.

**Curse and blessing**: The mother’s curse (אָלִית) on the thief and her subsequent blessing when her son confessed reflect the ancient belief in the power of spoken words. People feared that a pronounced curse would take effect, and the blessing was meant to neutralize it.

## Key Terms

**Ephod (אֵפוֹד)** was originally a priestly garment worn by the high priest (Exodus 28), but in private cultic practice it often became an instrument for seeking oracle answers. Here it is used as part of Micah’s private cult equipment.

**Teraphim (תְרָפִים)** were household gods or idols used for divination purposes. They are mentioned in several biblical texts (Genesis 31:19; 1 Samuel 19:13). The fact that Micah had teraphim in his "house of God" shows the mixture of Israelite and Canaanite practices.

**"To fill someone’s hand" (וַיְמַלֵּא אֶת־יַד)** is a technical expression for priestly consecration (compare Exodus 29:9). The fact that Micah first consecrated his own son, and then the Levite, shows that he imitated legitimate practice but without divine authorization.

**"To be father and priest" (לְאָב וּלְכֹהֵן)** was an honorific title for a religious advisor, also known from other Semitic cultures. The title suggests both spiritual authority and a patron-client relationship.
