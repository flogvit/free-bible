# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 11

## People

**The Seventy Elders** form a central group in this chapter. The institution of the seventy elders has roots going back to Exodus 24:1, 9, where seventy of Israel's elders accompanied Moses up Mount Sinai. The number seventy held symbolic significance in Israelite tradition and represented completeness in leadership. These elders are described as "zikne ha'am" (זִקְנֵי הָעָם) and "shoterav" (שֹׁטְרָיו) – the people's elders and its officials (verse 16). The role of officials (shoterim) was known from Egyptian administration, where Hebrew foremen had similar functions.

**Eldad and Medad** are two prophets mentioned only in this chapter. Their names are preserved nowhere else in the Bible. The name Eldad probably means "God has loved," while Medad may mean "love" or "beloved." There is a later apocryphal text called "The Prophecy of Eldad and Medad," mentioned in the early Christian writing The Shepherd of Hermas (Pastor Hermae), but this text is lost.

**Joshua, son of Nun** is presented here as Moses' "mesharet" (מְשָׁרֵת) – his personal servant or assistant. The expression "mibehurav" (מִבְּחֻרָיו) can be translated either "from his youth" or "from his chosen men"; the context supports the meaning of long-term and trusted service. This provides an important glimpse into Joshua's early connection with Moses, long before he would become Moses' successor as Israel's leader.

## Places

**Taberah** (תַּבְעֵרָה) means "burning" and marks the first place mentioned in the chapter. The name is an etiological place name – it explains why the place received its name through the story of the fire that broke out at "the edge of the camp" (בִּקְצֵה הַמַּחֲנֶה). The exact location is unknown, but it lay between Sinai and Hazeroth along the northern route through the Sinai Peninsula.

**Kibroth-hattaavah** (קִבְרוֹת הַתַּאֲוָה) means "graves of craving" or "graves of desire." This place has traditionally been attempted to be identified with Rues el-Ebeirig, about 50 kilometers northeast of Jebel Musa (the traditional Mount Sinai), but this identification is uncertain. The name serves as a lasting memorial over the consequences of the people's complaints.

**Hazeroth** (חֲצֵרוֹת) means "enclosures" or "courtyards," and was the next stop on the journey. The place has been suggested to be identified with Ain Hadra, an oasis along the route toward the Gulf of Aqaba.

## Events and Customs

**The Preparation of Manna**, described in verses 7-8, provides valuable cultural insight. The people ground the manna "in hand mills" (בָרֵחַיִם) or "beat it in mortars" (בַּמְּדֹכָה), boiled it in pots (בַּפָּרוּר), and made cakes (עֻגוֹת). This process reflects standard food preparation in the ancient Near East. Stone hand mills are among the most common finds at archaeological excavations from the Bronze Age.

**Consecration before Theophany** (verse 18) – the command "consecrate yourselves for tomorrow" (הִתְקַדְּשׁוּ לְמָחָר) before God's intervention reflects a widespread practice in ancient Israel. Such consecration typically involved ritual washing, abstinence from sexual activity, and perhaps fasting. The same pattern is seen before the theophany at Sinai (Exodus 19:10-15).

**The Transfer of the Spirit** (verses 17, 25) describes a unique prophetic event. The verb "'atsal" (אצל, here וְאָצַלְתִּי) – "take away" or "set apart" – suggests that the Spirit was distributed from Moses to the elders without reducing Moses' own portion. This differs from the usual transfer of material resources and points toward the inexhaustible nature of the Spirit.

## Key Terms

**Ha'safsuf** (הָאסַפְסֻף) in verse 4 refers to "the mixed multitude" or "the rabble" – non-Israelites who had joined the exodus from Egypt (cf. Exodus 12:38). The word is related to the verb "asaf" (to gather) and has a derogatory connotation. This group is pointed out as the origin of the complaints.

**Hit'onenim** (מִתְאֹנְנִים) in verse 1 describes the people's complaining. The exact meaning of the word is debated – it may mean "to complain" or "to imagine disaster." Some scholars connect it to the word "aven" (evil/disaster), suggesting that the people "spoke evil" or "uttered curses."

**Velo yasafu** (וְלֹא יָסָפוּ) in verse 25 – "and they did not continue" – has been subject to various interpretations. It may mean that the elders prophesied only on this one occasion, or that they "did not stop" (i.e., continued). The Masoretic accentuation supports the first interpretation.

**Selav** (שְׂלָו) – quail – is a migratory bird (Coturnix coturnix) that still crosses the Sinai Peninsula in large flocks during its annual migrations between Europe and Africa. The birds are known to fly low and become exhausted, especially when encountering headwinds over the sea, making them easy to catch.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Quail Migration over Sinai** is well documented in modern ornithology and historical sources. Quails migrate over the Sinai Peninsula in enormous numbers, particularly in autumn (September-October) and spring (March-April). Pliny the Elder and other ancient authors described how the birds could cover the ground in such quantities that they were caught by hand. Bedouins in the area traditionally caught quail with nets, and the birds were dried in the sun for storage – possibly reflected in the description of the Israelites "spreading them out" (verse 32).

**Food from Egypt**, which the people longed for (verse 5) – fish, cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions, and garlic – are all well documented as common foods in ancient Egypt. Egyptian tombs from the New Kingdom (1550-1070 BC) show depictions of these vegetables. Archaeological finds from the workers' village at Deir el-Medina confirm that ordinary workers had access to such foodstuffs. The cry of longing "we remember the fish we ate in Egypt for free" (חִנָּם) may reflect that slaves received food as part of their rations rather than having to buy it.
