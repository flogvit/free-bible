# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 5

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes several types of offerings that address various forms of guilt and sin. The **sin offering** (חַטָּאת, *chaṭṭat*) in verses 1–13 covers specific transgressions: failing to give testimony when called upon, ritual uncleanness through contact with dead animals or human impurity, and careless oath-taking. The graded system of offerings—from a lamb or goat to two doves to flour—demonstrates a social sensitivity that was unusual in ancient times, where religious participation was often reserved for the wealthy.

The **guilt offering** (אָשָׁם, *asham*) in verses 14–26 differs from the sin offering by requiring restitution in addition to the sacrifice. In cases of violation against holy things or against a neighbor, the offender had to repay the full amount plus one-fifth (20%). This combines ritual atonement with practical justice and compensation for the injured party.

## Key Terms

The term **אָלָה** (*alah*, "curse/oath") in verse 1 refers to a public appeal for witnesses, often pronounced at the city gate. Anyone who heard such an oath, possessed relevant information, yet remained silent, incurred guilt. This reflects society's dependence on oral testimony within a largely oral legal culture.

**מָעַל** (*ma'al*, "treachery/breach of faith") in verses 15 and 21 describes a breach of trust—either against God (by misusing holy things) or against fellow humans. The word implies to "cross a boundary" into what belongs to another. That both types of treachery are addressed in the same chapter highlights the connection between the vertical (relationship with God) and the horizontal (relationship with others).

**שֶׁקֶל הַקֹּדֶשׁ** (*shekel ha-qodesh*, "the holy shekel") in verse 15 was a standardized unit of weight used in temple contexts, likely heavier than ordinary commercial shekels (about 11.4 grams of silver). This ensured that the valuation of sacrificial animals followed a fixed, unmanipulable standard.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of standardized weights from the Iron Age, marked with Hebrew letters, confirm the use of a system with fixed weight units. Several such weights have been found in excavations in Jerusalem and other locations in Judah, including some marked with the word "beka" (half a shekel). These findings confirm that the economic system described in verses 15–18, where sacrificial animals were assessed according to a fixed silver standard, was a practical reality and not merely a theoretical construct.
