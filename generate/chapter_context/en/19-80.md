# Historical and Cultural Context for the Book of Psalms Chapter 80

## Places

The psalm specifically mentions the tribes of Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh (v. 3), all of which were associated with the Northern Kingdom of Israel, with Benjamin serving as a border tribe. This grouping likely reflects the geographical and political situation following the division of the kingdom. The Joseph tribes (Ephraim and Manasseh) formed the core of the Northern Kingdom, and the psalm appears to have been written at a time when these areas were threatened or already devastated—possibly during the Assyrian invasion that culminated in the fall of Samaria in 722 B.C. The geographical description in verse 12, "to the sea" (the Mediterranean) and "to the river" (the Euphrates), reflects the ideal boundaries of Israel's territory as promised in God's covenant.

## Events and Customs

The vineyard as a metaphor for Israel (vv. 9–17) was a well-known image in the ancient Near East. The prophet Isaiah uses the same imagery in his "Song of the Vineyard" (Isa 5:1–7), and the metaphor also appears in Hosea and Jeremiah. In ancient agricultural societies, the vineyard symbolized careful cultivation and the expectation of fruit. That the fence around the vineyard has been torn down (v. 13) and that wild boars and other animals are destroying it describes a situation of military invasion and national devastation. The image of God as "the Shepherd of Israel" (v. 2) and as the one who "sits enthroned above the cherubim" connects the psalm to covenant tradition and the tabernacle/temple, where the ark of the covenant with the cherubim represented God's throne.

## Key Terms

The expression "הֲשִׁיבֵנוּ" (hashivenu, "restore us" or "bring us back") functions as a refrain in the psalm (vv. 4, 8, 20) and expresses both a prayer for physical restoration and spiritual renewal. The word shares the same root as "teshuva" (repentance). "שֹׁשַׁנִּים עֵדוּת" (shoshannim edut) in the heading likely means "lilies—a testimony" and probably refers to a musical direction or instrument, although its exact meaning remains uncertain. The term "בֶּן־אָדָם" (ben-adam, "son of man") in verse 18 is used here parallel to "the man at your right hand" and primarily refers to a royal or leadership figure chosen by God, although the expression later acquired messianic significance in Jewish and Christian tradition.
