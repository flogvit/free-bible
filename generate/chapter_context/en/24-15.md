# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 15

## People

**Moses and Samuel (v. 1)** are mentioned as the foremost intercessors in Israel’s history. Moses interceded for the people after the golden calf incident (Exod 32:11–14) and on several other occasions, while Samuel prayed for Israel throughout his entire ministry (1 Sam 7:5–9; 12:19–23). The fact that even these two mighty intercessors could not avert judgment underscores the seriousness of Judah’s situation. This was a radical message for a people who trusted in the power of intercession.

**King Manasseh (v. 4)** reigned over Judah for 55 years (approximately 697–643 B.C.) and is described in 2 Kings 21 as the worst of Judah’s kings. He reintroduced idolatry, built altars to Baal, practiced child sacrifice in the Valley of Hinnom, and filled Jerusalem with innocent blood. Although Josiah later carried out reforms, Manasseh’s sins were so severe that, according to biblical tradition, they made judgment over Judah unavoidable (2 Kgs 24:3–4).

## Events and Customs

**The four punishments (vv. 2–3)**—death, sword, famine, and captivity—form a formulaic listing of the consequences of war well known in the ancient Near East. The further description involving sword, dogs, birds, and wild animals refers to ultimate disgrace: remaining unburied. For Israelites, proper burial was essential, and being eaten by animals was considered the worst fate imaginable (cf. Deut 28:26).

**"The sun goes down while it is still day" (v. 9)** is a poetic image of a mother who has borne seven children (a symbol of completeness and blessing) yet experiences losing them all. This completely overturns the cultural expectation that many children guaranteed security in old age.

## Key Terms

**To be winnowed with a winnowing fork (מִזְרֶה, *mizreh*, v. 7)**—The winnowing fork was a tool used to separate grain from chaff by tossing the mixture into the air so that the wind could blow away the chaff. The image of being "thrown" at "the gates of the land" describes exile and scattering—the people will be scattered like chaff in the wind.

**"Iron from the north and bronze" (v. 12)** is a difficult verse. It may refer to Babylon’s invincible military strength, or it could be a rhetorical question asking whether Judah’s kingdom can break the iron-hard power of the enemy from the north. "From the north" was the traditional invasion route into Palestine, and iron and bronze symbolized unstoppable military might.

**"To eat the words" (v. 16)** expresses complete internalization of God’s message. This image is also found in Ezekiel (3:1–3). For Jeremiah, God’s word was both a source of deep joy and a burden that led to suffering and isolation.
