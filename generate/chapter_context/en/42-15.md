# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 15

## People

The chapter identifies two groups in the parable: "τελῶναι" (tax collectors) and "ἁμαρτωλοί" (sinners) who approached Jesus, while "Φαρισαῖοι" (Pharisees) and "γραμματεῖς" (scribes) criticized him. The tax collectors were Jews who collected taxes for the Romans, and they were despised both as collaborators and because they often demanded more than was required. The term "sinners" included people who lived outside the law, either through immoral behavior or occupations considered unclean. The Pharisees' accusation that Jesus "welcomes sinners and eats with them" (v. 2) reflects how serious table fellowship was—sharing a meal implied acceptance and community.

## Events and Customs

The son's demand for his inheritance while his father was still alive (v. 12) was extremely offensive in Jewish culture. According to Sirach 33:19–23, there is strong warning against giving away property before death. Such a request was culturally understood as wishing the father dead. The fact that the father nevertheless divides the estate ("διεῖλεν αὐτοῖς τὸν βίον") shows unusual generosity. The son's work of feeding pigs (v. 15) represents the absolute lowest point for a Jew, since pigs were unclean animals (Leviticus 11:7). Tending them meant constant contact with ritual impurity and was among the most despised occupations.

The father's behavior upon his son's return breaks patriarchal honor conventions. For an older man to "run" (δραμών, v. 20) was considered undignified—respectable men walked with dignity. The robe, ring, and sandals (v. 22) are all symbols of restoration: the robe marks honor, the ring (likely a signet ring) grants authority over the household, and the sandals distinguish the son from a slave. The fattened calf that is slaughtered indicates a significant celebration, since meat was reserved for special occasions.

## Key Terms

The word "δραχμή" (drachma) in the parable of the lost coin (vv. 8–9) was a Greek silver coin worth approximately one day's wage for a laborer. Ten drachmas could represent a woman's dowry or savings, which explains the intense searching. The expression "ἅπτει λύχνον" (lights a lamp) reflects that Palestinian houses were often dark with small windows, making a lamp necessary even during the day.

The expression "εἰς ἑαυτὸν ἐλθών" (v. 17), "when he came to himself," is an idiom denoting a turning point where a person realizes their true situation. "Κεράτια" (v. 16) refers to the pods of the carob tree (Ceratonia siliqua), an inexpensive food used as animal feed but also eaten by the poorest in times of crisis.

The older son's complaint mentions "ἔριφος" (a young goat, v. 29), a far less valuable animal than the fattened calf. This underscores his sense of injustice. The expression "μετὰ πορνῶν" (with prostitutes, v. 30) is the older son's assumption—it is not stated in the story that the younger son spent his money on this.
