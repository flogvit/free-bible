# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 21

## People

**The Gibeonites** were not Israelites but descendants of the Amorites who had made a covenant with Joshua (Josh 9). This covenant obligated Israel to spare their lives, and it remained binding in David's time—several hundred years later. Saul's attempt to exterminate them (not described elsewhere in the Old Testament) was a serious breach of covenant that, according to the text, brought collective guilt upon the nation.

**Rizpah daughter of Aiah** was one of Saul's concubines (*pilegesh*). She is also mentioned in 2 Sam 3:7 in connection with a conflict between Abner and Ish-bosheth. Her faithful watch over the dead bodies from the barley harvest until the rains came demonstrates a mother's deep devotion and highlights a woman's role in preserving her family's honor even after death.

**The descendants of Rapha** (Hebrew: *yelide harafah*) are mentioned in the second part of the chapter. The term *harafah* (with the definite article) may refer to an ancestor of a clan of giants in Gath, or it could be a title/designation for this race of giants. They were possibly connected to the Rephaim—a prehistoric people known for their large stature (Deut 2:11, 20). Four such descendants are mentioned in verse 22.

## Places

**Gibeon** was located about 10 km northwest of Jerusalem in the territory of Benjamin. Archaeological excavations at el-Jib have uncovered a significant city from the Bronze and Iron Ages, including an extensive water system with a deep well.

**Gibeah of Saul** (v.6), also called Gibea, was Saul's hometown in the territory of Benjamin. The execution there had symbolic significance—the punishment was carried out at the sinner's own place.

**Gob** (vv.18-19), where several battles against the Philistines took place, is geographically uncertain. Some scholars identify it with Gezer or a location near the Philistine border region. The parallel account in 1 Chron 20:4 mentions "Gezer" instead.

**Beth-shan** (v.12), where the Philistines hung the bodies of Saul and Jonathan, was a strategically important city in the Jezreel Valley. Archaeological excavations have confirmed strong Philistine influence in the area at that time, as well as the discovery of Egyptian temples that may have been used to hang defeated enemies.

## Events and Customs

**Hanging/Display of Corpses** (*hoqa'*) was a practice where dead bodies were publicly displayed, likely as an act of atonement or to demonstrate divine judgment. This was not the method of execution itself but a subsequent display. In the ancient Near East, such practices were used to mark that those executed were under divine curse (cf. Deut 21:22-23).

**Collective Guilt and Atonement** was a central concept in ancient Israel and the Near East. The sin of a king or leader could bring disaster upon the entire people, and atonement often required representative punishment. The famine was interpreted as God's judgment for breach of covenant, and bloodguilt had to be settled through blood—not through financial compensation, which the Gibeonites explicitly rejected.

**Rizpah's Month-Long Watch** (from the barley harvest in April until the autumn rains in October/November) reflects a deep cultural duty to protect the dignity of the dead. Allowing bodies to be torn apart by animals was the ultimate disgrace. Her actions moved David and led to a dignified burial.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **כָּפַר** (*kipper*, "to atone") in verse 3 is central to the chapter. The word literally means "to cover over" and is used for removing guilt or impurity. David asks the Gibeonites how he can make atonement (*kapparah*) so that they may bless the Lord's inheritance. This shows the understanding that unresolved sin hindered blessing upon the land.

The expression **נֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל** (*ner yisra'el*, "Israel's lamp/light") in verse 17 is used of David by his men. This is a poetic expression for the king as the nation's life force and hope. David's men refused to let him go out into battle again because his death would "extinguish Israel's light"—a loss that would be catastrophic for the entire nation.
