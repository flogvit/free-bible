# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 2

## Key Terms

**"Son of Man" (בֶּן־אָדָם, ben-adam):** This expression is introduced here as God's characteristic way of addressing the prophet. The term emphasizes Ezekiel’s humanity and mortality in contrast to the holy God he stands before in his vision (chapter 1). Unlike its use in Daniel and the New Testament, it functions primarily here as a reminder of the distance between the divine and the human.

**"House of Rebellion" (בֵּית מְרִי, beit meri):** This expression describes Israel as a people defined by resistance against God. The Hebrew word *meri* implies conscious defiance and disobedience, not merely weakness or ignorance. The description "hard of face and stubborn of heart" (קְשֵׁי פָנִים וְחִזְקֵי־לֵב) uses bodily metaphors common in Semitic culture to express character traits.

**"Thorns and thistles" (סָרָבִים וְסַלּוֹנִים, saravim vesallonim):** These rare Hebrew words for prickly plants create an image of a hostile environment. Together with the reference to scorpions (עַקְרַבִּים), the text paints a picture of the prophet’s dangerous calling—a metaphor that his audience would have immediately recognized.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

**The Scroll Written on Both Sides:** In verses 9–10, a scroll (מְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר) is described as being written on both the front and back sides. This was unusual in antiquity, since papyrus and parchment were normally written on only one side (recto). A scroll written on both sides (opisthograph) signaled an overwhelming message—so much prophecy of judgment that one side was not enough. The three words "lamentations, sighs, and woes" (קִנִים וָהֶגֶה וָהִי) summarize the content of Ezekiel’s coming proclamation.

**Eating God’s Word:** The command to open the mouth and eat what God gives (verse 8) introduces a symbolic action illustrating the total internalization of the prophetic message. The prophet was not merely to deliver God’s words but to take them fully into himself. The actual eating of the scroll is completed only in Ezekiel 3:1–3. This act would have been understood as a concrete demonstration of the prophet’s close connection with the divine message.
