# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 29

## Places

**Ariel (אֲרִיאֵל)** is the central place in this chapter and serves as a poetic name for Jerusalem. The name literally means "Lion of God" or "God's hearth/altar." The phrase "the city where David encamped" (v. 1) confirms that it refers to Jerusalem, which David conquered and made his capital (2 Sam 5:6-9). The name "Ariel" also appears in Ezek 43:15-16 as a designation for the altar in the temple, suggesting a double meaning: Jerusalem as both God's protected city and the place of the sacrificial hearth. The Assyrian siege under Sennacherib in 701 BC forms the likely historical background for the prophecy in verses 1-8.

**Lebanon and Carmel** (v. 17) are mentioned as examples of nature's transformation. Lebanon was known for its mighty cedar trees and mountain forests, while Carmel (which means "fruitful garden" or "vineyard") was a fertile highland on the Mediterranean coast. The prophet's image of Lebanon becoming fruitful land and Carmel being regarded as a forest illustrates the radical upheaval God will bring.

## Key Terms

**Tardemah (תַּרְדֵּמָה)** – "deep sleep" (v. 10) – is a charged term in the Old Testament. The same word is used for the sleep that God caused to fall upon Adam (Gen 2:21) and Abram (Gen 15:12). Here it describes a spiritual dullness that God Himself has inflicted on the people as judgment for their hypocrisy.

**Chatum (חָתוּם)** – "sealed" (vv. 11-12) – refers to the practice of sealing scrolls with clay or wax to protect their contents or keep them secret. The image illustrates how God's revelation has become inaccessible to the people, whether they are learned or unlearned.

**Ov (אוֹב)** – "familiar spirit" (v. 4) – denotes the sound of one who practices necromancy, a whispering, piping voice supposedly coming from the ground. The image describes Jerusalem's humiliation: the city that once spoke with authority will speak weakly from the dust like a medium.

## Events and Customs

The chapter criticizes a form of ritual piety without inner devotion (v. 13). The expression "honor me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me" describes a cultic practice where outward observance of festivals and sacrifices (cf. "the feasts that go on" in v. 1) was performed mechanically, as "commandments taught by men." This contrasts with the Israelite ideal that worship should spring from love for God in the heart (Deut 6:5-6). Jesus quotes this verse directly in His criticism of the Pharisees (Matt 15:8-9).

The reference to those who "lay a snare for him who reproves at the gate" (v. 21) reflects the Israelite legal system, where the city gate functioned as both court and public gathering place. Elders and witnesses gathered there to settle disputes, and it was here that prophets often proclaimed their criticism of social injustice.
