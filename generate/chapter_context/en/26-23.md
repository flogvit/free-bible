# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 23

## Characters

The chapter introduces two allegorical figures: Oholah (אָהֳלָה) and Oholibamah (אָהֳלִיבָה), representing Samaria (the capital of the northern kingdom) and Jerusalem (the capital of the southern kingdom), respectively. The names are symbolic: Oholah means "her own tent"—possibly a reference to the illegitimate sanctuaries of the northern kingdom—while Oholibamah means "my tent is in her," pointing to the fact that God's temple was in Jerusalem. They are portrayed as sisters with the same mother, emphasizing that both kingdoms descended from the same people and covenant relationship with God.

## Places

The chapter mentions several nations with which Israel and Judah formed political alliances. **Egypt** represents the earliest "lover" and symbolizes Israel's original slavery and persistent temptation to seek Egyptian military support. **Assyria** was the dominant power in the 800s and 700s BC, and both the northern kingdom and Judah at times sought Assyrian protection and influence, which brought religious syncretism. **Babylon** (the land of the Chaldeans) was the new superpower during Ezekiel's time. Verse 23 also mentions **Pekod, Shoa, and Koa**—these were Aramean or Chaldean tribal groups east of the Tigris who served in the Babylonian army, known from Assyrian cuneiform texts.

## Events and Customs

The chapter uses explicit sexual language to describe political and religious unfaithfulness. In the ancient Near Eastern context, political alliances were often sealed with religious rituals and acknowledgment of the alliance partner's gods. Verses 14–15 describe wall paintings of Babylonian warriors with distinctive features: belts around their hips, turban-like headgear, and appearances like "officers" (שָׁלִשִׁים). Such reliefs and paintings of Babylonian and Assyrian soldiers are known from archaeological finds and show that Ezekiel described recognizable cultural traits. Verses 37–39 refer to child sacrifice ("made their children pass through the fire for idols") and the grotesque combination of idol worship and temple service on the same day.

## Key Terms

Several Hebrew expressions are central to understanding. The verb **עָגַב** ('agav, "to lust after, long for") is repeatedly used regarding the sisters' passion for foreign nations. **גִּלּוּלִים** (gillulim, "idols") is a derogatory term Ezekiel often uses, possibly derived from a word meaning "dung" or "stone blocks." **כּוֹס** ("cup/chalice") in verses 31–34 is a powerful image of God's judgment—a poisoned cup that must be drunk to the dregs, a motif recurring throughout prophetic literature. The term **תַּזְנוּת** (taznut, "whoredom/prostitution") is consistently used as a metaphor for covenant breaking, both political and religious.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The detailed descriptions of Babylonian and Assyrian soldiers (verses 6, 12, 14–15, 23)—with blue-colored garments (תְכֵלֶת), headgear, and cavalry—correspond with reliefs from Nineveh and Babylon depicting military scenes. Especially the Sennacherib reliefs from Nineveh (circa 700 BC) and later Babylonian representations show warriors with precisely such distinctive features. This confirms that Ezekiel had firsthand knowledge of Mesopotamian culture and art, which is natural given that he himself was exiled to Babylon.
