# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 22

## People

**Shebna (שֶׁבְנָא)** was a high-ranking official with the title "the one over the house" (אֲשֶׁר עַל־הַבָּיִת), comparable to a prime minister or chief of staff. His name lacks a patronymic (father's name), leading several scholars to suggest he may have been of foreign origin. His ambitious tomb project in the rock (verse 16) testifies to self-exaltation and materialistic priorities during a time of crisis. Isaiah prophesies his downfall and exile.

**Eliakim, son of Hilkiah (אֶלְיָקִים בֶּן־חִלְקִיָּהוּ)** is the one God has chosen to take over Shebna's position. His name means "God raises up," and his father's name means "the Lord is my portion"—both genuinely Hebrew names that contrast with Shebna's possible foreign background. In 2 Kings 18:18 and Isaiah 36:3, we find Eliakim as "the one over the house" while Shebna has been demoted to scribe (סֹפֵר), confirming the fulfillment of the prophecy.

## Places

**"Valley of Vision" (גֵּיא חִזָּיוֹן)** is a poetic name for Jerusalem itself. The term "valley" may seem paradoxical for a city on high ground, but Jerusalem lies between higher hills and is surrounded by valleys (the Hinnom Valley, the Kidron Valley). "Vision" points to Jerusalem's role as the center of prophecy.

**"Armory of the House of the Forest" (נֶשֶׁק בֵּית הַיָּעַר)** refers to Solomon's "House of the Forest of Lebanon" (1 Kings 7:2–5), an arsenal and treasury built with cedar trees from Lebanon. Jerusalem's leaders turned here for military help instead of turning to God.

**"The Lower Pool" and "the Old Pool" (הַבְּרֵכָה הַתַּחְתּוֹנָה / הַבְּרֵכָה הַיְשָׁנָה)** were water reservoirs in Jerusalem's water system. The reservoir "between the two walls" (verse 11) may be connected to the Pool of Siloam.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes Jerusalem's preparations under the Assyrian threat, likely Sennacherib's siege in 701 BC. The measures in verses 8–11—inspecting cracks in the walls, counting houses, tearing down buildings to strengthen the wall, and organizing the water supply—are typical siege preparations from this period. King Hezekiah carried out exactly such measures, including the famous Siloam Tunnel.

Verses 12–13 contrast God's call to mourning and repentance (weeping, lamenting, shaving one's head bald, putting on sackcloth) with the people's hedonistic response: slaughtering cattle and sheep, feasting, and drinking wine. The expression "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (אָכוֹל וְשָׁתוֹ כִּי מָחָר נָמוּת) represents a fatalistic attitude that rejects repentance.

Shebna's tomb construction (verse 16) reflects a widespread practice among the elite in the ancient Near East. Carving a tomb out of rock was a status symbol and required significant resources.

## Key Terms

**"The one over the house" (אֲשֶׁר עַל־הַבָּיִת)** was the highest administrative position after the king himself, comparable to a prime minister or vizier. The office had responsibility for royal property and administration.

**"The key of the House of David" (מַפְתֵּחַ בֵּית־דָּוִד)** in verse 22 symbolizes full authority over access to the king and the royal household. The expression "he opens, and no one shuts; he shuts, and no one opens" describes absolute authority.

**"The peg in a sure place" (יָתֵד בְּמָקוֹם נֶאֱמָן)** is an image of stability and reliability. Such a peg in the wall held the family's valuable items. Paradoxically, verse 25 prophesies that even this peg will fall—which can be understood as a warning that even the most promising leader may ultimately fail, or that the burden placed upon him will become too great.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

In the Silwan village east of the City of David, several Iron Age tomb chambers carved out of rock have been discovered. One of these has a fragmentary inscription mentioning an official "who is over the house" (אשר על הבית). Several scholars have identified this as Shebna's tomb, although the identification is not certain. The inscription is now in the British Museum and reads partially: "This is [the tomb of ...ja]hu who is over the house. There is no silver or gold here, only [his bones] and his servant woman's bones with him."

The Siloam Inscription, found in Hezekiah's Tunnel, documents the completion of the water tunnel that led water from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam. This corresponds with the water supply work mentioned in verses 9–11 and confirms the historical preparations for the Assyrian siege.
