# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 10

## People

**Aaron and Eleazar** are mentioned in verse 6 in connection with Aaron’s death and Eleazar’s assumption of the high priesthood. This represents the first priestly succession after Aaron’s death. The transition from father to son ensured continuity in worship and reflected a common pattern in the ancient Near East, where priestly offices were often hereditary.

## Places

**Beeroth Bene-Jaakan, Moserah, Gudgodah, and Jotbathah** (verses 6–7) are stations along Israel’s wilderness journey. The order here differs from Numbers 33:31–33, which has concerned biblical scholars. Jotbathah is described as "a land of brooks", suggesting an oasis in the desert landscape. Modern scholars have attempted to identify these places in the Negev or the Sinai region, but secure localization remains difficult. The description of water-rich areas corresponds with known oases along traditional trade routes through the Sinai Peninsula.

## Events and Customs

**The Ark of Acacia Wood** (verses 1–5) was a chest for storing the tablets of the law. Acacia wood (*שִׁטִּים*, shittim) grows in desert regions and was available on Sinai. Chests for storing sacred documents were common in Egyptian and Mesopotamian culture—pharaoh had chests for important documents, and Mesopotamian temples had similar objects. The Hebrew term *אָרוֹן* (aron) is used both for this ark and for ordinary chests or coffins, but here it takes on a specially holy function.

**The Setting Apart of the Levites** (verses 8–9) describes the establishment of a tribe without territorial inheritance, dedicated to religious service. This was unusual in the ancient Near East, where priests often possessed temple property and land. The Levites’ dependence on tithes and offerings created a unique system in which religious leaders were directly dependent on the people’s faithfulness to the covenant.

## Key Terms

**"Circumcise the foreskin of your heart"** (*וּמַלְתֶּם אֵת עָרְלַת לְבַבְכֶם*, verse 16) is a powerful metaphor that transfers the physical covenant act to inner life. The term *עָרְלָה* (orla, "foreskin") is used here figuratively for what hinders openness toward God. This spiritualization of circumcision also appears in the prophet Jeremiah (4:4) and represents an early tradition of understanding outward rituals as signs of inward realities.

**"God of gods and Lord of lords"** (*אֱלֹהֵי הָאֱלֹהִים וַאֲדֹנֵי הָאֲדֹנִים*, verse 17) is a superlative construction typical of Hebrew. The expression places YHWH above all other deities and powers—a polemical claim in a polytheistic context. Similar superlative constructions are used for kings and gods in the ancient Near East, but here they are applied to highlight YHWH’s sovereignty over all powers.

**"The foreigner"** (*גֵּר*, ger, verses 18–19) refers to non-Israelites living among the people. The command to love the foreigner is grounded in Israel’s own experience as foreigners in Egypt—a remarkable ethical argument based on historical memory. In the ancient Near East, foreigners generally had few rights, making this legislation distinctive.
