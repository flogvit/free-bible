# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 45

## People

**Cyrus (כּוֹרֶשׁ, Koreš)** is the main figure in this chapter and one of the most significant characters in Isaiah’s prophecies. Cyrus II, known as Cyrus the Great, founded the Persian Achaemenid Empire and ruled from approximately 559–530 BC. He conquered Babylon in 539 BC and shortly afterward issued a decree allowing the Jewish exiles to return to their homeland. The most striking aspect of Isaiah 45 is that Cyrus is called the Lord’s "anointed" (מָשִׁיחַ, masjiach) in verse 1—the only non-Israelite in the entire Old Testament to receive this title. This shows that God uses even pagan rulers to carry out His plans of salvation.

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical regions in verse 14: **Egypt** (מִצְרַיִם), **Cush** (כּוּשׁ, Nubia/northern Sudan), and **Seba** (סְבָאִים, likely in southern Arabia or Ethiopia). These areas represented important trading nations and military powers in the ancient world. The description of "tall men" from Seba points to the famous merchants of the Sabaean kingdom. The prophecy that the wealth of these nations would belong to Israel was partially fulfilled during the Persian period, when the Persian Empire included all these regions.

## Events and Customs

The description of "bronze doors" and "iron bars" (verse 2) reflects Babylon’s famous fortifications. The Greek historian Herodotus described Babylon’s hundred bronze gates. The expression "to loosen the belts of kings" (verse 1) refers to the custom where a ruler’s belt symbolized his power and readiness—to loosen it meant to render him defenseless. The prophecy that "gates will not be shut" may refer to the historical account that Babylon was conquered while its river gates stood open.

## Key Terms

**Masjiach (מָשִׁיחַ)** – "the anointed one" – is surprisingly used here of Cyrus (verse 1). Usually, this title was reserved for Israelite kings and priests who were consecrated to service through anointing with oil. That a pagan king receives this title emphasizes God’s sovereign rule over all nations.

**"Creator of light and darkness, peace and disaster"** (verse 7) uses the phrase pair יוֹצֵר אוֹר וּבוֹרֵא חֹשֶׁךְ (jotser or u-bore chosjekh). This is a polemic against Persian Zoroastrian dualism, which taught that there were two deities—a good god of light (Ahura Mazda) and an evil god of darkness (Angra Mainyu). Isaiah insists that the God of Israel alone is the source of everything.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Cyrus Cylinder**, discovered in Babylon in 1879 and now housed in the British Museum, is a clay document from 539 BC in which Cyrus describes his conquest of Babylon and his policy of allowing deported peoples to return home and rebuild their temples. Although the cylinder does not specifically mention the Jews, it confirms the religious tolerance policy that made the Jewish return possible. The text on the cylinder shows that Cyrus attributed his victory to the god Marduk, while Isaiah 45 insists that it was Israel’s God who guided him, even though Cyrus "did not know Him" (verses 4–5).
