# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 14

## People

**Herod Antipas** (Greek: Ἡρῴδης ὁ τετραάρχης) was the son of Herod the Great and ruled as tetrarch over Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39. The title "tetrarch" (τετραάρχης) originally meant "ruler of a fourth part," but was used for a subordinate prince under Roman authority. That Matthew refers to him as "king" (ὁ βασιλεύς) in verse 9 reflects common speech, even though he never attained the royal title he coveted.

**Herodias** (Ἡρῳδιάς) was the daughter of Aristobulus and granddaughter of Herod the Great. She left her first husband Philip (another son of Herod the Great) to marry his half-brother Antipas. This marriage violated Jewish law in several ways: she was still married, and it was forbidden to marry one's brother's wife while he was alive (Lev 18:16; 20:21). John's public condemnation of this relationship (verse 4) posed a serious political threat to Herod's legitimacy.

**Herodias' daughter** is not named in the Gospels, but the Jewish historian Josephus identifies her as Salome.

## Places

**The fortress of Machaerus**, although not mentioned by name in Matthew, is identified by Josephus as the place where John was imprisoned and executed. This fortress lay east of the Dead Sea in Perea, on a mountain peak 1,100 meters high. Archaeological excavations have revealed both an upper fortress and a lower palace with a banquet hall that fits the description of the birthday feast. An underground cistern has also been found, which may have served as a prison cell.

**Gennesaret** (Γεννησαρέτ, verse 34) was a fertile plain on the northwest side of the Sea of Galilee, known in antiquity for its rich agricultural production. Josephus describes the area as unusually fertile, where many different fruits and vegetables grew. Several villages were located along this coastal strip, which explains how people could gather quickly when Jesus arrived.

**The Sea of Galilee** (τὴν θάλασσαν) is surrounded by mountains that create sudden and powerful storms when cold air rushes down over the water. That the boat was "a great distance from land" (verse 24) and "tossed by the waves" reflects the dangerous conditions fishermen regularly faced. A stadion corresponded to about 185 meters.

## Events and Customs

**Birthday celebrations** (γενέσια, verse 6) were more common in Greco-Roman culture than in Jewish tradition, where they were often viewed with suspicion. The fact that Herod celebrated his birthday with dancing and extravagant vows shows his Hellenistic lifestyle. Dancing performed by women of high rank was controversial, and the fact that Herodias' daughter danced "among them" suggests an intimate and morally questionable atmosphere.

**The oath and the guests** (verse 9) illustrate the honor-shame dynamics in Mediterranean culture. Herod's public oath bound him legally and socially. Breaking such a promise before guests of high rank would have been a serious loss of honor. This cultural pressure explains why Herod, despite his reluctance, carried out the execution.

**The blessing of the bread**, with thanksgiving, breaking, and distribution (verse 19), followed the Jewish meal ritual. The fact that the crowd sat on the grass in groups reflects the practical organization also mentioned in Mark's parallel account. The twelve baskets (κοφίνους) that were gathered up use a Greek word that specifically referred to the woven baskets that Jewish travelers traditionally carried with them.

## Key Terms

**"The fourth watch of the night"** (τετάρτῃ φυλακῇ τῆς νυκτός, verse 25) refers to the Roman division of the night into four watches. The fourth watch was from about 3:00 AM to 6:00 AM, just before dawn. This was the darkest and most exhausting time of the night, which heightens the dramatic setting for Jesus' coming.

**"I am"** (ἐγώ εἰμι, verse 27) is Jesus' self-identification to the frightened disciples. The expression resonates with God's self-revelation in Exodus 3:14 and is used several times in the Gospels in contexts that suggest divine identity. Combined with the action of walking on water, this points to the Old Testament image of God as ruler over the sea and the waters.

**"You of little faith"** (Ὀλιγόπιστε, verse 31) is a characteristic Matthean expression describing faith mixed with doubt. It is used several times in the Gospel of Matthew and describes an intermediate state—not unbelief, but insufficient faith when facing trials.
