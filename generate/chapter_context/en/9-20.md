# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 20

## People

**Jonathan** appears in this chapter as the central mediator between David and Saul. His loyalty is tested when Saul confronts him directly (verses 30–31) and even throws his spear at him (verse 33). Jonathan’s choice to prioritize his covenant with David over his own dynastic interests is explicitly stated by Saul: "As long as the son of Jesse lives on the earth, neither you nor your kingdom shall stand firm" (verse 31).

**Abner**, mentioned in verse 25, was Saul’s army commander and the son of Saul’s uncle Ner (1 Sam 14:50–51). His position at the king’s table (מִצַּד שָׁאוּל) shows the significance military leaders held within the court hierarchy. The fact that he sits beside Saul while David’s place remains empty underscores the tension between the established military structure and the emerging leader, David.

## Events and Customs

**The New Moon Festival** (חֹדֶשׁ, *chodesh*) was an important religious observance in ancient Israel. It fell on the first day of each month, determined by the appearance of the new moon, and was celebrated with sacrificial meals and rest from work (Num 28:11–15; Amos 8:5). The king holding a formal meal at the new moon demonstrates how religious and political functions were intertwined in the monarchy. David’s absence from this meal would have been highly noticeable and required explanation.

**The Family Sacrifice in Bethlehem** (verses 6, 29), which David uses as an excuse, reflects a genuine custom. The expressions "זֶבַח הַיָּמִים" (*zevach hajamim* – "the annual sacrifice") and "זֶבַח מִשְׁפָּחָה" (*zevach mishpacha* – "family sacrifice") refer to periodic offerings in which the entire clan gathered. Such sacrifices strengthened family bonds and were religiously obligatory, making them a plausible excuse.

**Purity Requirements for Meals** are expressed in Saul’s reaction on the first day (verse 26). He assumes that David is "לֹא טָהוֹר" (*lo tahor* – "unclean"), which could result from various conditions such as sexual contact, touching a dead body, or other forms of ritual impurity (Lev 15; 22:3–7). It was normal to wait one day before participating in ceremonial meals after such impurity.

**The Arrow Signal System** that Jonathan and David arrange (verses 20–22, 35–38) was a practical method for secret communication. Archery was a common skill, and practicing with arrows toward a target would not arouse suspicion. The system of sending a boy to retrieve the arrows while shouting instructions allowed for encrypted communication in plain sight.

**Seating Arrangements at the King’s Table** (verse 25) reveal the court hierarchy. Saul sits "by the wall" (אֶל־מוֹשַׁב הַקִּיר) – a place of honor that provided protection and a clear view. The fact that Jonathan "rose up" (וַיָּקָם) may indicate movement during the meal. David’s fixed seat at the table shows that he had achieved significant status at court before his conflict with Saul escalated.

## Key Terms

**Covenant** (בְּרִית, *berit*) is used in verse 8 where David refers to "the Lord’s covenant" between himself and Jonathan. This was not merely an informal friendship but a formal, oath-bound agreement with religious foundation. Such covenants entailed mutual loyalty and protection and were binding on both parties and their descendants (verses 15, 42). The covenant formula in verses 14–17 resembles vassal treaties known from the ancient Near East.

**"The Lord’s Steadfast Love"** (חֶסֶד יְהוָה, *chesed JHVH*) in verse 14 is a key term that combines covenant loyalty with the grace and mercy characteristic of God’s nature. Jonathan asks David to show him this kind of faithfulness – a loyalty that goes beyond mere contractual obligation.

**The Stone Ezel** (הָאֶבֶן הָאָזֶל, *ha'even ha'azel*) in verse 19 serves as an agreed meeting point. The name אָזֶל (*azel*) is uncertain but may be related to the verb אָזַל (to go away/disappear), which fits the context where David must flee. Such prominent stones often served as landmarks and meeting places in ancient Israel.
