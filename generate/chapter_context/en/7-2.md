## Places

**Gilgal (הַגִּלְגָּל)** is mentioned as the starting point for the angel of the Lord (v. 1). This was the Israelites' first campsite after crossing the Jordan and served as an important cultic center during the conquest. The fact that the angel came from Gilgal emphasizes continuity with the covenant relationship established during the conquest period.

**Bochim (בֹּכִים)**, meaning "the weepers," received its name from the people's reaction to the angel's message (vv. 4–5). The exact location of this place is uncertain; the Septuagint places it near Bethel. The name was given as a memorial to this national sorrow, and the people offered sacrifices there to the Lord.

**Timnath-Heres (תִּמְנַת־חֶרֶס)** in the hill country of Ephraim, north of Mount Gaash, was Joshua's inheritance and burial place (v. 9). The name is written as "Timnath-Serah" (תִּמְנַת־סֶרַח) in Joshua 19:50 and 24:30 – the letters have been transposed (metathesis).

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the Israelites' practice of making covenants with the Canaanites and failing to tear down their altars – both actions directly contrary to God's command (Deut. 7:2–5). Such covenants often involved religious obligations toward the other party's gods, which explains the theological seriousness of the prohibition.

The expressions "they were gathered to their fathers" (v. 10) and that a new generation "did not know the Lord" describe the critical transition from the conquest generation to the next. "To know" (יָדַע) involves not only intellectual knowledge but personal experience and covenant relationship – the new generation had not personally experienced God's mighty deeds.

## Key Terms

**Baal and Ashtoreths (הַבְּעָלִים וְלָעַשְׁתָּרוֹת)** represent respectively male and female Canaanite deities (vv. 11, 13). Baal (plural: ba'alim) was the storm and fertility god. Ashtoreths (plural of Ashtoreth/Ashtart) was the goddess of fertility and war. The worship of these gods involved practices that stood in sharp contrast to the worship of the Lord.

**Judges (שֹׁפְטִים)** in this context do not primarily refer to legal judges, but charismatic leaders whom God raised up to deliver Israel from enemies (vv. 16, 18). The Hebrew verb שָׁפַט encompasses judging, ruling, and restoring justice. The Israelite judges were not hereditary rulers but were called by God in times of crisis.

**"To commit whoredom" (זָנוּ)** is used metaphorically in verse 17 regarding Israel's unfaithfulness to God. This marriage metaphor for the covenant relationship between God and Israel expresses that idolatry is spiritual adultery.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

Verses 6–9 repeat almost verbatim Joshua 24:28–31, but in a different order. In Joshua, the people's service comes before Joshua's death; here, Joshua's death (v. 8) comes before the description of the people's service. This structural difference emphasizes that Judges 2 focuses on the transition to a new era after Joshua's death.

Verses 11–19 introduce the cyclical pattern that characterizes the entire book of Judges: sin → oppression → crying out → deliverance → relapse. This is the theological key to the book and is presented programmatically here before the individual judge narratives begin.
