# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 17

## People

The chapter focuses on three main figures identified in the interpretation (vv. 12–21). **The first eagle** represents Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon, who besieged Jerusalem in 597 B.C. and deported King Jehoiachin along with the court and leading men to Babylon. **Zedekiah**, Jehoiachin’s uncle, was installed as a vassal king by Nebuchadnezzar and had to swear an oath of loyalty. The name "Zedekiah" (צִדְקִיָּהוּ, "the Lord is my righteousness") was given to him by Nebuchadnezzar himself, which ironically stands in sharp contrast to his breach of oath. **The second eagle** represents Pharaoh Hophra (Apries) of Egypt, who reigned from 589–570 B.C., and with whom Zedekiah formed a secret alliance in rebellion against Babylon.

## Places

**Lebanon** (v. 3) is used as a symbol for Judah or Jerusalem because of its famous cedar trees, which were associated with royal power and the temple. **"The city of merchants"** (עִיר רֹכְלִים, v. 4) refers to Babylon, which was a major commercial center in antiquity. Babylon’s location on the Euphrates River and its extensive trade networks made this description fitting. **Egypt** (v. 15) was the traditional superpower that smaller kingdoms in Canaan often turned to for military support against Mesopotamian powers, despite prophetic warnings against such alliances.

## Events and Customs

**Vassal treaties and oath-swearing** (vv. 13–19) were central elements of governance in the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires. When a conquered king was installed as a vassal, he had to swear an oath by his own gods—in Zedekiah’s case, by Yahweh. Breaking such an oath was therefore not only political treason but also religiously considered a desecration of God’s name. Babylonian sources confirm the practice of taking hostages from royal families ("the mighty of the land," אֵילֵי הָאָרֶץ, v. 13) to ensure obedience. **Oath-breaking** was regarded as one of the gravest sins throughout the ancient Near East, and texts from this period show that gods were invoked as witnesses and avengers in cases of treaty violation.

## Key Terms

**חִידָה** (ḥidah, "riddle") and **מָשָׁל** (mashal, "parable/allegory") in verse 2 describe the literary genre. A ḥidah is a puzzling statement requiring interpretation, while mashal is a figurative story with deeper meaning. The combination signals that the text operates on multiple levels. **צַמֶּרֶת** (ṣammeret, "top shoot") refers to the upper part of the tree and symbolizes the king—Jehoiachin who was taken away, and later the messianic king in the promise (v. 22). **אָלָה** (ʾalah, "oath/curse," vv. 13, 16, 18–19) indicates that the oath was a self-imposed curse that would fall upon the oath-breaker. That God calls it "my oath" (v. 19) emphasizes that by swearing in His name, He becomes the offended party.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Babylonian Chronicle** confirms Nebuchadnezzar’s campaign against Jerusalem in 597 B.C. and the deportation of King Jehoiachin. The text dates the conquest to "the second Adar" (March 597) in Nebuchadnezzar’s seventh regnal year. The **Jehoiachin Tablets** from Babylon, discovered in the ruins of the southern palace, mention rations of oil allotted to "Yaukin, king of Judah" and his sons, confirming both the deportation and that he retained his royal status in captivity—exactly as Ezekiel describes the "top shoot" being taken to "the city of merchants."
