# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 1

## People

**Paul** (Παῦλος, v. 1) introduces himself with three titles: "a slave of Christ Jesus" (δοῦλος), "called to be an apostle" (κλητὸς ἀπόστολος), and "set apart for the gospel of God" (ἀφωρισμένος εἰς εὐαγγέλιον θεοῦ). This triple introduction establishes both his humility and his authority.

**David** (Δαυίδ, v. 3) is mentioned as Jesus' ancestor "according to the flesh" (κατὰ σάρκα). Davidic lineage was essential for Jewish messianic expectation, based on God's promise to David of an eternal throne (2 Sam 7:12–16).

## Places

**Rome** (Ῥώμη, v. 7, 15) is named as the home city of the recipients. Paul greets "all God's beloved in Rome" (v. 7) and expresses his desire to preach the gospel "also to you who are in Rome" (v. 15). This establishes the letter's addressees and Paul's yet-unfulfilled wish to visit them.

## Events and Customs

Paul's description of idolatry (v. 23) specifically mentions images of "mortal man, birds, four-footed animals, and reptiles" (ὁμοιώματι εἰκόνος φθαρτοῦ ἀνθρώπου καὶ πετεινῶν καὶ τετραπόδων καὶ ἑρπετῶν). This reflects both Greco-Roman temple worship with divine statues and may also allude to Egyptian animal worship, which was well known throughout the Roman Empire.

Paul's criticism of homosexual acts (vv. 26–27) must be understood against the background that such relationships, especially between men, were socially accepted in parts of the Greco-Roman upper class. Jewish writers like Philo of Alexandria sharply criticized this and viewed it as a mark of pagan immorality. Paul stands here within a Jewish tradition that interpreted such behavior as a result of idolatry.

## Key Terms

**Doulos** (δοῦλος, v. 1) – "slave" or "servant" – was a powerful self-designation. The phrase "servant of the Lord" was an honorific title for leaders like Moses and David in the Old Testament, and Paul's use of the term combines humility with authority.

**Apostolos** (ἀπόστολος, v. 1) means "messenger" or "sent one," and in Greek legal usage carried the meaning of an authorized representative acting with the full authority of the sender. Paul uses this title to establish his legitimacy before a church he did not found.

**Euangelion** (εὐαγγέλιον, vv. 1, 9, 15, 16) – "gospel" or "good news/glad tidings" – was not merely a religious term. In the Roman world, the word was used for imperial proclamations, such as the announcement of a new emperor's accession to the throne or military victories. By applying this word to the message about Jesus, Paul placed it in direct contrast to the propaganda of the imperial cult.

**Hellēn** (Ἕλλην, vv. 14, 16) and **barbaros** (βάρβαρος, v. 14) represented the fundamental cultural division in antiquity. "Hellenes" were not only ethnic Greeks but all who participated in Greek culture and language. "Barbarians" were everyone else – the word imitates the sound of unintelligible speech. Paul declares himself a debtor to both groups, which was radically inclusive.

**Dikaiosynē theou** (δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ, v. 17) – "the righteousness of God" – is a key term that can mean both God's own righteous character and the righteousness God gives to people. The expression has roots in the Hebrew *tsedaqah*, which in the Old Testament often denotes God's saving intervention, not merely a legal standard.
