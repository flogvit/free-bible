# Historical and Cultural Context for Hebrews 9

## Places

The chapter provides a detailed description of the tabernacle (σκηνή, *skēnē*), the portable sanctuary constructed during the wilderness wanderings. The author describes two separate rooms: "the Holy Place" (Ἅγια, *Hagia*) with its lampstand, table, and bread of the Presence, and "the Most Holy Place" (Ἅγια Ἁγίων, *Hagia Hagiōn*) containing the ark of the covenant. Although Herod's temple still stood in Jerusalem when the letter was written, the author deliberately focuses on the original tabernacle from Moses' time rather than the temple. This emphasizes that the argument is built upon the authority of Scripture rather than contemporary temple practice.

## Events and Customs

The chapter centers on Yom Kippur (the Day of Atonement), the only day when the high priest was permitted to enter the Most Holy Place. According to Leviticus 16, he had to offer a bull for his own sins and a male goat for the people's sins committed in ignorance (ἀγνοημάτων), and carry the blood behind the veil. The ritual of "the red heifer" (σποδὸς δαμάλεως, v. 13) refers to Numbers 19, where the ashes of a red heifer mixed with water were used for cleansing from impurity caused by contact with the dead. The covenant ratification at Sinai (vv. 19–21) describes how Moses sprinkled blood on both the scroll, the people, the tabernacle, and all the vessels used in service—details that expand upon the biblical account in Exodus 24.

## Key Terms

The Greek word διαθήκη (*diathēkē*) in verses 15–17 carries a deliberate double meaning: both "covenant" (Hebrew *berit*) and "testament." The author exploits this duality rhetorically—a testament takes effect only after the one who made it has died, just as the new covenant became valid through Christ's death. The term ἱλαστήριον (*hilastērion*, "mercy seat," v. 5) denotes the cover of the ark of the covenant, the place where God's presence met with sacrificial blood. The expression ἐφάπαξ (*ephapax*, "once for all," v. 12) is theologically central and contrasts the repeated pattern of the Levitical system with Christ's final sacrifice.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from Qumran, particularly the Temple Scroll (11Q19), show that Jewish groups in the first century were deeply concerned with the structure of the temple and purity regulations. These texts confirm that detailed discussion regarding the furnishings of the sanctuary and the significance of sacrificial service was common in contemporary Jewish theology. Josephus provides similar descriptions of the tabernacle and the temple in his works (*Antiquities of the Jews* 3.6–7 and *The Jewish War* 5.5), demonstrating that the author's presentation aligns with first-century Jewish understanding of these holy spaces.
