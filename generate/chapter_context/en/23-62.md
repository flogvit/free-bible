# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 62

## Places

The chapter focuses intensely on **Zion** and **Jerusalem** as both the geographical and theological center. In prophetic literature, these names are often used interchangeably; originally, Zion referred to the fortified hill that David conquered, while Jerusalem encompassed the entire city. During the exile and afterward, Zion became a charged term symbolizing God’s presence and the people’s hope for restoration. The reference to Jerusalem’s **walls** (v. 6) may reflect both the physical destruction after 586 B.C.E. and the later rebuilding project, or it may be used metaphorically regarding the city’s protection and status.

## Events and Customs

**Name changes** (vv. 2, 4, 12) in ancient Israel were deeply symbolic acts marking a transformed identity and destiny. When God gives new names—from "Forsaken" (עֲזוּבָה, *azuvah*) to "My Delight Is in Her" (חֶפְצִי־בָּהּ, *Cheftsi-vah*), and from "Desolate" (שְׁמָמָה, *shemamah*) to "Married Woman" (בְּעוּלָה, *be'ulah*)—it signals a complete reversal of Jerusalem’s status. The **marriage imagery** (vv. 4–5) draws on the Israelite covenant model of marriage, where God is the bridegroom and the people or city is the bride.

**Watchmen on the walls** (vv. 6–7) reflect a well-known practice of guarding city walls, but here the role takes on a spiritual dimension: they are to "remind the Lord" (הַמַּזְכִּרִים, *hammazkirim*) and not keep silent day or night. This may mirror the function of Levitical singers or a form of continual intercession.

**Harvest festival in the temple courts** (v. 9) refers to the custom of enjoying the firstfruits and tithes within the sanctuary, as described in Deuteronomy 12:17–18 and 14:23. "My holy courts" (בְּחַצְרוֹת קָדְשִׁי) points to the temple area where such meals took place.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **צְדָקָה** (*tsedaqah*, "righteousness," vv. 1–2) in this context means "salvation" or "victory"—God’s righteous act that vindicates His people before the nations. The parallel expression **יְשׁוּעָה** (*yeshu'ah*, "salvation") reinforces this meaning.

**עֲטֶרֶת תִּפְאֶרֶת** (*ateret tif'eret*, "a crown of splendor," v. 3) and **צְנִיף מְלוּכָה** (*tsenif melukhah*, "a royal turban") are royal insignia. The striking image is that Jerusalem does not wear the crown but is held "in the Lord’s hand"—the city is God’s ornament and glory.

The term **בְּעוּלָה** (*be'ulah*, v. 4) comes from the verb **בָּעַל** ("to be master over, to marry") and means "married woman"—the opposite of a forsaken or childless woman, who held low social status in ancient Israel.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The personal names "Cheftsi-bah" and "Be'ulah" are not merely poetic constructions. Cheftsi-bah is known from 2 Kings 21:1 as the name of King Hezekiah’s wife and King Manasseh’s mother, giving these prophetic names a royal resonance for Israelite listeners. Thus, the use of these names connects Jerusalem’s future restoration to Davidic greatness.
