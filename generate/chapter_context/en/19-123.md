# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 123

## Key Concepts

The psalm uses central imagery drawn from the master-servant relationship in the ancient Near East. The expression "the eyes of servants to their master's hand" (עֵינֵי עֲבָדִים אֶל־יַד אֲדוֹנֵיהֶם) refers to how servants carefully watched their master's hand movements to receive instructions, protection, or gifts. The hand symbolized authority and provision. Similarly, the mention of "the eyes of a female servant to her mistress" (עֵינֵי שִׁפְחָה אֶל־יַד גְּבִרְתָּהּ) shows that this image applied to both genders in the household.

The Hebrew word "buz" (בּוּז)—contempt or scorn—and "la'ag" (לַעַג)—mockery—describe a social situation where the people experienced humiliation. The expressions "the arrogant" (הַשַּׁאֲנַנִּים) and "the proud" (לִגְאֵיוֹנִים) point to people who lived in self-assured prosperity and looked down on believers.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's plea for mercy amid contempt may reflect the situation of Jews living as a minority among foreign peoples, possibly during the Persian period after their return from exile. The book of Nehemiah (Neh 2:19; 4:1–4) describes exactly such mockery from surrounding nations against those rebuilding Jerusalem. The custom of lifting one's eyes to heaven in prayer (נָשָׂאתִי אֶת־עֵינַי) was a common posture expressing trust and expectation of God's help.
