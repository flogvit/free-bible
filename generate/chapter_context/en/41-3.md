# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 3

## People

**The Twelve** form the core of this chapter. Jesus "appointed" (ἐποίησεν, literally "made") twelve to be with him and to be sent out to preach and drive out demons. The list includes Simon, who was given the name "Peter" (Πέτρος, "rock"), and the sons of Zebedee, James and John, who were called "Boanerges" (Βοανηργές), an Aramaic expression that Mark translates as "sons of thunder." Simon "the Cananaean" (Καναναῖος) does not refer to Cana, but to the Aramaic word *qan'an* (zealous/fervent), meaning "the zealous one." Luke uses the Greek term "Zealot" (Luke 6:15). Judas Iscariot (Ἰσκαριώθ) likely means "man from Kerijoth," a town in Judea.

**The Herodians** (Ἡρῳδιανῶν) were supporters of the Herodian dynasty, particularly Herodes Antipas who ruled Galilee. Their alliance with the Pharisees (v. 6) is remarkable, since these groups were normally opponents—the Pharisees being religious traditionalists while the Herodians supported the Hellenized ruling class. This alliance shows how threatening they perceived Jesus to be.

## Places

The chapter mentions a broad geographical range of places from which people came: Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, the region east of the Jordan (Perea), and the areas around Tyre and Sidon. This spans from pagan Phoenician cities in the north to Edomite Idumea in the south—an area that was forcibly converted to Judaism under the Hasmoneans in the 100s BC. The Sea of Galilee ("the sea," τὴν θάλασσαν, v. 7) served as Jesus' refuge from the crowds, and the small boat (πλοιάριον) was a practical solution to the pressing throng.

"The mountain" (τὸ ὄρος, v. 13) where Jesus chose the twelve is not named, but mountain tops held strong symbolic significance in Jewish tradition as meeting places with God—such as Sinai where Moses received the law and appointed seventy elders.

## Events and Customs

**The Sabbath Controversy** (vv. 1-6) concerns rabbinic interpretations of the Sabbath commandment. According to Pharisaic tradition, healing on the Sabbath was permitted only in cases of immediate life-threatening danger. A withered hand was a chronic condition and could therefore wait until after the Sabbath. Jesus' question about whether it is lawful "to do good or to do evil, to save life or to kill" (v. 4) turns the debate around: To refrain from helping when one can is itself an act of wickedness.

**Accusations of Demon Possession** (vv. 21-22) illustrate two different reactions to Jesus. His family thought he was "out of his mind" (ἐξέστη), possibly in religious ecstasy or madness. The scribes from Jerusalem advanced a more serious accusation: that he operated by Beelzebul (Βεελζεβούλ), "the prince of demons." The name is likely a distortion of "Baal-Zebul" (prince's lord) to "lord of the flies" as mockery.

## Key Terms

**"Hardness of heart"** (τῇ πωρώσει τῆς καρδίας, v. 5) uses the Greek *pōrōsis* which means calcification or petrification—a medical term for the formation of hard bone tissue. It describes a spiritual condition where the ability to see and understand God's work is blocked.

**"Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit"** (v. 29) is described as an "eternal sin" (αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος). The context clarifies what this entails: attributing the works of the Holy Spirit to Satan (v. 30). It is not about isolated doubts, but a persistent attitude that rejects God's saving presence even when it is clearly manifested.

**The number twelve** for the group of apostles has clear symbolic significance as a parallel to Israel's twelve tribes. By establishing this group, Jesus signaled the restoration of all God's people in the end times.
