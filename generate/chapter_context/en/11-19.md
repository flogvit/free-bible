# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 19

## People

**Elijah (אֵלִיָּהוּ)** appears in this chapter in a vulnerable state following his triumph on Mount Carmel. His flight and depression reveal the human side of prophetic ministry. Under the broom bush, he asks to die, but is strengthened by God to continue.

**Jezebel (אִיזֶבֶל)** threatens Elijah with death after the defeat of the prophets of Baal. Her oath in verse 2 invokes "gods" (plural - אֱלֹהִים), revealing her polytheistic background.

**Elisha (אֱלִישָׁע)** is introduced as Elijah's successor. His name means "My God saves." He came from Abel-meholah and clearly belonged to a wealthy family, since he was plowing with twelve yoke of oxen (v. 19).

**Hazael (חֲזָאֵל)** and **Jehu son of Nimshi (יֵהוּא בֶן־נִמְשִׁי)** are mentioned as two of the three people Elijah is commissioned to anoint. Together with Elisha, they form a threefold instrument for God's judgment against Baal worship in Israel (v. 17).

## Places

**Beersheba (בְּאֵר שֶׁבַע)** was located at the southern border of the kingdom of Judah. By traveling there, Elijah crossed into the southern kingdom, outside Jezebel's jurisdiction. There he left his servant before continuing into the wilderness (v. 3).

**Horeb (חֹרֵב)**, "the mountain of God" (הַר הָאֱלֹהִים), was the destination of Elijah's forty-day journey. There he met God in a cave and received new assignments. The journey of forty days and forty nights mirrors Moses' stay on the mountain.

**The wilderness of Damascus (מִדְבַּר דַמָּשֶׂק)** is mentioned as the place Elijah is to go to anoint Hazael as king over Aram. That Elijah is commissioned to anoint an Aramean king shows that the God of Israel also works beyond Israel's borders.

**Abel-meholah (אָבֵל מְחוֹלָה)** was Elisha's hometown where Elijah found him while he was plowing. The fertile agricultural land explains why Elisha could plow with twelve yoke of oxen.

## Events and Customs

**The broom bush (רֹתֶם)** under which Elijah slept (vv. 4-5) was a desert plant that could provide shade. Under this bush, Elijah wished to die, but instead was met by an angel with food and drink.

**The mantle (אַדֶּרֶת)** that Elijah cast over Elisha (v. 19) was the prophet's characteristic garment. The action symbolized the call to prophetic ministry and the transfer of authority.

**The slaughter of the oxen** and burning of the yoke represented Elisha's radical break with his former life. By using farming tools as fuel and sacrificing the work oxen, he demonstrated there was no turning back. The meal he shared with the people marked a public farewell to his family and village.

## Key Terms

**"A still, small voice" (קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה)** in verse 12 is an expression difficult to translate precisely. The words literally mean "a sound of thin silence" or "a quiet, gentle whisper." This stands in contrast to the dramatic forces of nature (wind, earthquake, fire) and suggests that God often reveals Himself in stillness rather than in the spectacular.

**"Jealousy" (קַנֹּא קִנֵּאתִי)** is a strong expression Elijah uses twice (vv. 10, 14). The Hebrew verb *qana* describes an intense, burning zeal for God's honor, the same word used of God's own jealousy. Elijah's complaint expresses both his passion for God and his experience of isolation.

**The seven thousand (שִׁבְעַת אֲלָפִים)** who have not bowed to Baal (v. 18) represent a faithful remnant in Israel. The number seven symbolizes completeness in biblical context, and the expression shows that God had preserved a significant group of faithful people even amid widespread apostasy. This was God's answer to Elijah's complaint about being alone.
