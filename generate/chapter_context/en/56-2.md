# Historical and Cultural Context for Titus 2

## Events and Customs

The chapter's division of exhortations by age and gender (older men, older women, younger women, younger men, slaves) reflects the Roman household structure known as *domus*. In the first-century Roman Empire, the household was the fundamental social unit, and there were established expectations for how each group should behave to maintain the family's honor. Paul's exhortations are not random categories but follow the pattern of Greco-Roman "household codes" (*Haustafeln*), which also appear in philosophers such as Aristotle and in later Stoic ethics.

The exhortation to older women to "teach what is good" (*kalodidáskalous*) was culturally significant. In antiquity, it was primarily older women who were responsible for passing on knowledge about household management and family life to the next generation. The warning against being "enslaved to much wine" (v. 3) reflects a genuine concern in Greco-Roman culture, where older women were sometimes associated with excessive drinking.

## Key Terms

The Greek adjective **σώφρων** (*sṓphrōn*) and related forms such as **σωφρονίζω** (*sōphronízō*), **σωφρονεῖν** (*sōphroneín*), and **σωφρόνως** (*sōphrónōs*) appear repeatedly in this chapter (vv. 2, 4, 5, 6, 12). This word group expressed one of the four cardinal virtues in Greek philosophy and meant self-control, moderation, and sound judgment. By using this term, Paul communicates with values highly regarded in Hellenistic culture but gives them new content through Christian faith.

The expression **λαὸν περιούσιον** (*laòn perioúsion*, "a treasured people") in verse 14 is drawn directly from the Septuagint's rendering of Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 14:2. This was Israel's self-understanding as God's chosen people, and Paul now applies this to the Christian church—a theologically significant transfer of covenant terminology.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The term **ἐπιφάνεια** (*epifáneia*, "appearing/revelation") in verse 13 and the related verb **ἐπεφάνη** (*epefánē*, "has appeared") in verse 11 were known from Hellenistic ruler cults, where an emperor could be described as a god's "appearance" on earth. Inscriptions from the first century show that Emperor Augustus was hailed as a savior (*sōtēr*) whose birth brought peace. By using this language about Christ, both regarding his first coming (v. 11) and his future return (v. 13), Paul makes a clear theological point: It is Jesus Christ, not the emperor, who is the true Savior and God.
