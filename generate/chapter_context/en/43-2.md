# Historical and Cultural Context for John 2

## Places

**Cana in Galilee** (vv. 1, 11) was a village in Galilee, but its exact location is debated. The two most likely sites are Khirbet Kana, about 13 km north of Nazareth, and Kafr Kanna, about 6 km northeast of Nazareth. Khirbet Kana has the strongest support from first-century archaeological finds, including remains of a Jewish village setting with stone jars used for purification rituals.

**Capernaum** (v. 12) was located on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Archaeological excavations have revealed a significant fishing village with a synagogue and residential houses dating to the first century. The expression "went down" (κατέβη) reflects the geographical reality—Cana is situated higher than Capernaum by the sea (about 200 meters below sea level).

**The Temple in Jerusalem** (vv. 13–21) at this time was the Herodian Temple, begun by Herod the Great in 20/19 BC. The Jewish remark about "forty-six years" (v. 20) places the event around AD 27/28. The trade in sacrificial animals and money changing likely took place in the "Court of the Gentiles," the outermost area of the temple complex.

## Events and Customs

**Jewish wedding celebrations** typically lasted seven days and were a major social event for the entire local community. The host family was responsible for providing food and wine to the guests throughout the celebration. Running out of wine was a serious social disgrace that could bring shame upon the groom's family and potentially lead to legal claims from the bride's family.

**The purification jars** (v. 6) were made of stone because, unlike clay pots, stone could not become ritually unclean according to rabbinic tradition. The six jars held a total of 450–680 liters and were used for handwashing and other purification rituals required before meals under Jewish purity laws. Water was poured over the hands, not dipped into.

**Temple trade** served a legitimate purpose: pilgrims needed to purchase approved sacrificial animals and exchange foreign currency for the Tyrian silver shekel required for the temple tax. The problem was likely that the trade had moved into the temple area itself and that prices were unreasonable, hindering genuine worship.

## Key Terms

**Ἀρχιτρίκλινος** (architriklinos, vv. 8–9) was the "master of the feast" or "head waiter"—a trusted person, often a friend or relative, responsible for managing the wedding banquet, tasting the wine before serving it, and ensuring that guests were well cared for. This was an honorable position requiring good judgment.

**Μετρητής** (metrētēs, v. 6) was a liquid measure equivalent to about 40 liters (roughly one Hebrew "bat"). Two to three metrētai per jar means 80–120 liters per jar.

**Σημεῖον** (sēmeion, "sign," vv. 11, 18, 23) is John's preferred term for Jesus' miracles. The word emphasizes that the miracles are not merely acts of power but signs pointing to a deeper spiritual reality—in this case, Jesus' glory (δόξα) and divine identity.

**Ναός versus ἱερόν** (vv. 14–21): John uses ἱερόν to refer to the entire temple complex where the trade took place, but ναός in Jesus' statement about "this temple"—a word that specifically denotes the sanctuary itself. This creates a double meaning pointing to Jesus' body as the new place of God's presence.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Kana have uncovered several first-century stone jars similar to those described in the narrative. These findings confirm the widespread use of stone vessels in Jewish households at that time for maintaining ritual purity.

Inscriptions and literary sources confirm the scale of temple trade. Josephus describes the enormous number of sacrificial animals slaughtered during Passover—according to his figures, around 256,500 lambs. This required extensive logistics for sales and currency exchange, explaining the established trade that Jesus confronted.
