# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 15

## Events and Customs

### Shemittah – The Year of Release
The chapter opens with the command concerning *shemittah* (שְׁמִטָּה), which means "release" or "liberation." Every seventh year, debts between Israelites were to be forgiven. This was a radical social arrangement in ancient times. In Mesopotamia, there were sporadic debt cancellations issued by kings (so-called *mīšarum* edicts), but these were irregular and dependent on royal goodwill. Israel's system was unique because it was cyclical and established by law, not reliant on the favor of a ruler.

### Release of Hebrew Slaves
Verses 12–18 address the release of Hebrew slaves after six years of service. Unlike surrounding cultures, where debt slaves often remained in lifelong bondage, the Mosaic Law imposed time-limited servitude. The ritual of piercing the ear with an awl (מַרְצֵעַ, *martse'a*) against the doorpost (v. 17) marked voluntary, permanent service. In the ancient Near East, the ear symbolized obedience and attentiveness, giving the ritual deeper cultural resonance.

### The Firstborn Animals
Verses 19–23 concern the consecration of firstborn male animals from cattle and sheep. These were not to be used for work or sheared but were to be eaten annually before the Lord at the chosen place. Animals with defects could be eaten locally as ordinary food, but they were not to be offered as sacrifices. The prohibition against eating blood (v. 23) reiterates a fundamental principle of the Mosaic Law.

## Key Terms

### Evyon (אֶבְיוֹן)
The word *evyon* (poor, needy) appears seven times in this chapter, underscoring its thematic weight. The term describes not only material poverty but also social vulnerability – people who lacked the networks and resources to support themselves.

### Ha'aneq (הַעֲנֵיק)
The verb in verse 14 (הַעֲנֵק תַּעֲנִיק), often translated "supply generously," comes from the root ענק, which is related to "neck." It implies that the freed slave was to be sent away with generous gifts – from livestock, threshing floors, and winepresses – as a visible marker of new status and the opportunity for economic renewal.

### Beliyya'al (בְלִיַּעַל)
In verse 9, there is a warning against a "beliyya'al thought" in the heart – a base or worthless thought. The term was later used to refer to evil in general and here refers to calculated stinginess: refusing to lend because the year of release was approaching.

## Chapter-Specific Findings
The Laws of Hammurabi (c. 1750 B.C.) from Babylon contain provisions regarding debt slaves who were to be released after three years (§117), offering an interesting parallel to the Israelite six-year system. The Israelite system stands out due to its humanitarian rationale: "Remember that you yourself were a slave in Egypt" (v. 15) – a theological motivation absent from Mesopotamian law codes.
