## People

**Hananiah son of Azzur from Gibeon** (חֲנַנְיָה בֶן־עַזּוּר) is the central opponent in this chapter. He is presented as a prophet (נָבִיא, *navi*), but Jeremiah exposes him as a false prophet. The name Hananiah (חֲנַנְיָה) means "the Lord has shown favor," an ironic name given his false message of imminent deliverance. That he was from Gibeon, a Levitical city in the tribe of Benjamin (Joshua 21:17), likely gave him some religious authority. Gibeon lay only about 10 km northwest of Jerusalem and was known as an important cultic site.

**Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim** (יְכָנְיָה בֶן־יְהוֹיָקִים) is mentioned as the exiled king. He was deported to Babylon in 597 BC along with leading citizens and temple treasures.

## Places

**The house of the Lord (the temple)** is the scene of the dramatic confrontation. That this takes place in the temple, before the priests and the people, underscores the seriousness and public nature of the dispute. The temple served as a natural forum for prophetic proclamation, and Hananiah’s message about the imminent return of the temple treasures would have had special resonance in that setting.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains a powerful **prophetic sign** (אוֹת, *ot*). The yoke (מוֹטָה, *mota*) that Jeremiah wore (see chapter 27) was a concrete symbolic act illustrating submission to Babylon. When Hananiah breaks this yoke, he performs a counter-action intended to symbolize the collapse of Babylon’s power. Such symbolic acts were common in prophetic practice throughout the ancient Near East—the action was understood as setting in motion what it symbolized. The Lord’s response through Jeremiah—that the wooden yoke is replaced by an iron yoke—shows that Hananiah’s action actually worsened the situation rather than improving it.

## Key Terms

The term **סָרָה** (*sara*) in verse 16, meaning "rebellion" or "apostasy," is a strong expression denoting religious treachery. The same word is used in Deuteronomy 13:6 concerning false prophets who lead the people away from the Lord. The judgment against Hananiah—death within two months (from the fifth to the seventh month)—follows the pattern of Deuteronomy 18:20–22, where a prophet’s death confirms that he spoke on his own initiative.

The time reference "two years" (שְׁנָתַיִם יָמִים, *shnatayim yamim*) in Hananiah’s prophecy stands in sharp contrast to Jeremiah’s message of 70 years of exile (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). This was the core of the theological conflict between the two prophets.
