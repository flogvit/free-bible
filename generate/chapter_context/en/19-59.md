# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 59

## People

The heading links the psalm to a specific episode in David’s life: when Saul sent men to guard his house with the intent of killing him (1 Sam 19:11–17). According to the biblical account, it was Michal, David’s wife and Saul’s daughter, who warned David and helped him escape through a window. Saul’s men surrounded the house at night and waited until morning to kill David. This background explains the psalm’s imagery of enemies who "come back in the evening" and "wander about the city" (vv. 7, 15), as well as the desperate plea for deliverance from those lying in ambush.

## Key Terms

The heading contains the expression "al-tasjḥet" (אַל־תַּשְׁחֵת, "do not destroy"), a musical direction, as well as the genre designation "miktam" (מִכְתָּם).

The image of dogs wandering about the city (vv. 7, 15) reflects a well-known reality in the ancient Near East. Wild or semi-wild dogs lived off refuse on the outskirts of cities and were known for their noisy, threatening behavior at night. Comparing enemies to such dogs was a powerful insult that emphasized their predatory and unclean nature.

## Events and Customs

The psalm’s prayer that God should not kill the enemies immediately but rather "make them stumble" and "bring them down" (v. 12) reflects the idea that prolonged humiliation would serve as a more lasting testimony to God’s justice than immediate destruction. The people were to remember ("so that my people may not forget") God’s intervention. This educational dimension of divine punishment was a familiar concept in Israelite thought.
