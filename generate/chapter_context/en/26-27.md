# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 27

## Places

Ezekiel 27 contains a remarkably detailed list of trade connections that span the entire known world of the sixth century BC. **Tyre** (צוֹר, *Tsor*) – the Phoenician port city portrayed here as a magnificent ship – was strategically located on an island off the coast of present-day Lebanon.

The chapter mentions several geographical regions: **Senir** (שְׂנִיר, possibly part of or another name for the Hermon mountain range) and **Lebanon** as suppliers of timber; **Bashan** (בָּשָׁן) east of Galilee, known for its oak trees; **Kittim** (כִּתִּיִּם, Cyprus) as a supplier of precious materials. **Tarshish** (תַּרְשִׁישׁ) likely refers to the western Mediterranean, possibly Spain, known for metal trade. **Javan** (יָוָן) is the Hebrew name for Greece/Ionia, while **Tubal** (תֻּבַל) and **Meshech** (מֶשֶׁךְ) were regions in eastern Anatolia. **Beth-Togarmah** (בֵּית תּוֹגַרְמָה) was likely Armenia, known for horse trade. **Sheba** (שְׁבָא) and **Rama** (רַעְמָה) in Arabia were known for spices and gold. **Damascus** (דַּמֶּשֶׂק) and **Helbon** (חֶלְבּוֹן, north of Damascus) were famous for wine.

## Events and Customs

The chapter presents Tyre as a magnificent trading ship – an extended metaphor that was highly appropriate for a maritime nation. The ship's construction (vv. 5-7) reflects actual Phoenician shipbuilding traditions: fir planks (בְּרוֹשִׁים) from Senir, cedar masts from Lebanon, oak oars from Bashan, and embroidered linen from Egypt for sails.

The mourning rituals described in verses 30-31 – throwing dust on the head, rolling in ashes, shaving the head, and putting on sackcloth – were standard death rituals in the ancient Near East. The fact that these rituals are performed by merchants and sailors underscores the universal grief over Tyre's fall. The lament (קִינָה, *qinah*), which Ezekiel is asked to raise over Tyre (vv. 2, 32), follows a traditional pattern used at funerals.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **מַעֲרָב** (*ma'arav*), translated as "merchandise" or "wares," appears repeatedly in the chapter and refers to goods exchanged in trade. The expression **כְּלִילַת יֹפִי** (*kelilat yofi*, "perfect in beauty," v. 3) describes Tyre's self-perception – a pride that contrasts with the coming destruction.

The term **רֹכֵל** (*rokel*, "merchant" or "peddler") is used for middlemen and buyers, while **סֹחֵר** (*socher*) denotes a larger merchant. This distinction reflects the sophisticated trade system of Phoenicia. **עִזְבוֹנַיִךְ** (*'izvonayikh*, "your merchandise") is used specifically for export goods.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Phoenician cities have confirmed many details in this chapter. Phoenician trade inscriptions from Kition on Cyprus and other locations reveal a trade network that corresponds with Ezekiel's description. The detailed list of merchandise – from metals (silver, iron, tin, lead) to textiles, horses, spices, and slaves – reflects the actual complexity of eastern Mediterranean trade.

A Babylonian text collection describes Nebuchadnezzar's prolonged siege of Tyre, which was likely ongoing when this chapter was written. This provides historical context for the prophecy concerning Tyre's fall.
