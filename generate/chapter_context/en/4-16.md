# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 16

## People

**Korah** (קֹרַח) was a Levite from the Kohathite clan, the same clan to which Aaron and Moses belonged. The Kohathites had a special duty among the Levites—to carry the most holy items of the tabernacle (Num 4:4-15). Korah’s rebellion can be understood as dissatisfaction with the priesthood being restricted to Aaron’s line, while he himself, despite his close kinship, was excluded from this role.

**Dathan and Abiram** (דָּתָן וַאֲבִירָם), sons of Eliab, were from the tribe of Reuben. As descendants of Jacob’s firstborn son, their participation in the rebellion may reflect frustration over the loss of the birthright—leadership had passed to Levi (the priesthood) and Judah (the kingship). **On, son of Pelet** (אוֹן בֶּן־פֶּלֶת), is mentioned in verse 1 but then disappears from the narrative; later Jewish tradition (Talmud, Sanhedrin 109b-110a) relates that his wife persuaded him to withdraw. The 250 men who supported the rebellion are described as "princes of the congregation" (נְשִׂיאֵי עֵדָה), showing that this was a revolt by the elite, not from the grassroots of the people.

## Events and Customs

**The incense test** proposed by Moses (vv. 6-7) was a "divine judgment"—a trial in which God himself would decide whom he had chosen. Offering incense was reserved for priests (Exod 30:7-8). Nadab and Abihu died when they offered "strange fire" (Lev 10:1-2), demonstrating the consequences of unauthorized approach to God. By allowing the rebels to burn incense before the Lord, Moses let God himself demonstrate who held legitimate authority.

**The form of punishment**—the earth opening up and swallowing the rebels—was a "new thing" (בְּרִיאָה, v. 30) that distinguished this judgment from natural deaths. This emphasized that the punishment came directly from God. Fire from the Lord consumed the 250 who burned incense (v. 35), paralleling the fate of Aaron’s sons Nadab and Abihu (Lev 10:2).

## Key Terms

The expression **"the whole congregation is holy"** (כָל־הָעֵדָה כֻּלָּם קְדֹשִׁים, v. 3) echoes God’s word in Exodus 19:6 that Israel should be "a kingdom of priests." The rebels’ argument was that if the whole people were holy, there was no need for a separate priesthood. However, they overlooked that God himself had established different roles within the holy community.

**"Sheol"** (שְׁאוֹל, vv. 30, 33) refers to the realm of the dead or the dwelling place of the departed in Old Testament thought. That the rebels went "down alive into Sheol" underscored the complete and immediate nature of God’s judgment.

The title **"God of the spirits of all flesh"** (אֵל אֱלֹהֵי הָרוּחֹת לְכָל־בָּשָׂר, v. 22) is a rare designation for God that highlights his sovereignty over all life and his ability to distinguish between individuals—an appeal that he should not punish everyone for one man’s sin.
