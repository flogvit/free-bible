## People

**The Queen of Sheba** (מַלְכַּת־שְׁבָא, *malkat-sheva*) is a prominent figure in this chapter. She came from Sheba, a wealthy kingdom in southwestern Arabia (modern-day Yemen). Her visit with a large caravan loaded with gold, precious stones, and exceptionally costly spices (verses 1, 9) represents both diplomatic recognition and commercial interests, as Solomon's control over trade routes could affect the Sabaean economy.

**Huram** (חוּרָם, also known as Hiram) of Tyre is mentioned in verses 10 and 21. His sailors cooperated with Solomon's servants on long-distance trading expeditions to Ophir and Tarshish.

**The prophets Nathan, Ahijah from Shiloh, and the seer Iddo** are mentioned in verse 29 as sources for Solomon's history. This shows that prophetic writings were used as historical sources in the books of Chronicles.

## Places

**Ophir** (אוֹפִיר) is mentioned in verse 10 as the source of gold and almug wood. The exact location of Ophir is debated – suggested locations include southern Arabia, East Africa, or India. Archaeological finds of Hebrew ostraca from Tell Qasile with the text "Ophir gold to Beth-Horon" confirm that Ophir was a real trading partner.

**Tarshish** (תַּרְשִׁישׁ) in verse 21 refers either to a specific destination (possibly Tartessos in Spain) or to a type of seagoing ship designed for long voyages.

**The House of the Forest of Lebanon** (בֵּית יַעַר הַלְּבָנוֹן) in verses 16 and 20 was an impressive building in Jerusalem, named after the many cedar pillars that reminded people of a Lebanese forest. The building served as an arsenal and treasury for gold shields and drinking vessels.

## Events and Customs

**Exchange of riddles** (חִידוֹת, *khidot*) as described in verse 1 was an accepted diplomatic practice in the ancient Near East. Wisdom competitions between rulers served to establish intellectual prestige and diplomatic ties. The fact that Solomon could answer all her questions (verse 2) demonstrated the divine wisdom he had received.

**Diplomatic gifts** followed established protocols in ancient diplomacy. The amount of gold the queen gave – 120 talents (verse 9), equivalent to about 4 tons – represents an enormous fortune and signals a significant trade and diplomatic partnership.

## Key Terms

**Almug wood** (עֲצֵי הָאַלְגּוּמִּים) in verses 10-11 was a valuable imported wood used for steps in the temple and royal palace, as well as for musical instruments (lyres and harps). The wood was so rare that the text emphasizes that nothing like it had ever been seen in Judah before.

**666 talents of gold** (verse 13) indicates Solomon's annual income in gold. This amounts to about 22 tons of gold – an astronomical sum that illustrates the extent of his wealth.

**Ivory, apes, and peacocks** (שֶׁנְהַבִּים וְקוֹפִים וְתוּכִּיִּים) in verse 21 indicate trade with tropical regions. The words may have Tamil/Dravidian origins, supporting the theory of trade connections with India.

**Solomon's throne** (verses 17-19), made of ivory covered with pure gold, with six steps and twelve lions, is described as unique – "nothing like it had ever been made in any kingdom." The throne symbolized both wealth and royal authority.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries at Marib in Yemen have confirmed the existence of a wealthy Sabaean kingdom during this period, supporting the biblical portrayal of the queen's wealth and resources.

The chapter concludes with a summary of Solomon's reign (40 years) and his death (verses 30-31), as well as the transition to his son Rehoboam – preparing the reader for the division of the kingdom in the next chapter.
