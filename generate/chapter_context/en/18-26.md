# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 26

## Key Terms

### Rephaim (הָרְפָאִים)
In verse 5, the Hebrew word *refaim* is used, translated as "the dead" or "shades." In the ancient Near East, this was a term for the spirits of the deceased in the realm of the dead. Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra also refer to *rp'um* as a designation for deceased kings and heroes. The idea that the dead live a shadowy existence "under the waters" reflects ancient Near Eastern cosmology, where the underworld was placed beneath the waters of the primeval sea.

### Sheol and Abaddon (שְׁאוֹל / אֲבַדּוֹן)
Verse 6 mentions both *Sheol* (the realm of the dead) and *Abaddon* (destruction/ruin). These two Hebrew terms describe the underworld from different angles: *Sheol* denotes the dwelling place of the dead, while *Abaddon* (from the root *'bd*, "to perish") emphasizes the aspect of destruction. That these places are "naked" before God means that nothing is hidden from Him—even the realm of the dead is open to His gaze.

### Rahab (רָהַב)
In verse 12, *Rahab* is mentioned, a mythical sea monster symbolizing chaotic forces. In Babylonian and Canaanite mythology, there were similar ideas about an original battle between the creator god and the chaos monster (cf. Marduk and Tiamat). In Hebrew poetry, *Rahab* became an image of God's victory over chaos, without the text necessarily adopting the polytheistic mythology.

### The Fleeing Serpent (נָחָשׁ בָּרִיחַ)
Verse 13 refers to "the fleeing serpent" (or "the twisting serpent"). Ugaritic texts from the 13th century BCE mention *bṯn brḥ* ("the fleeing serpent") as a chaos monster. This parallel shows that Job uses familiar imagery from surrounding cultures to describe God's sovereign power over all chaotic forces.

## Events and Customs

### Ancient Near Eastern Cosmology
The chapter contains several cosmological images common in the ancient Near East. Verse 7 describes how God "hangs the earth on nothing" and "stretches out the north over empty space" (*tohu*). While many ancient cultures imagined the earth resting on pillars, water, or an animal, this verse presents a more abstract understanding. The "pillars of heaven" in verse 11 may refer to mountains at the horizon that were thought to support the sky—a common image in Egyptian and Mesopotamian art and literature.
