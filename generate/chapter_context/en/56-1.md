# Historical and Cultural Context for Titus 1

## People

**Titus** is referred to as Paul's "own true child in the common faith" (v. 4). Unlike Timothy, Titus was a Gentile believer. His Gentile background made him an important example in the debate over circumcision (Gal 2:1-3). The fact that Paul now left him on Crete to organize the churches shows that he had considerable authority and experience.

**Epimenides** is the poet quoted in verse 12 as the Cretans' "own prophet." He was a semi-legendary Cretan sage from the 6th century BC, known in Greek tradition as a poet and religious expert. Some ancient lists included him among the Seven Sages. The saying "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" was famous in antiquity and illustrates the bad reputation the Cretans had. That Paul calls him "prophet" (προφήτης) may be ironic or reflect that Epimenides was credited with religious insight in Greek culture.

## Places

**Crete** was a large island in the eastern Mediterranean, strategically located on trade routes between east and west. In New Testament times, the island was under Roman rule as part of the province of Creta et Cyrenaica. Crete had many cities scattered across the island, which explains Paul's instruction to appoint elders "in every town" (κατὰ πόλιν). Archaeological excavations have uncovered several cities from this period, including Gortyna, which was the provincial capital. Jews had been settled on Crete for centuries, a fact mentioned already in 1 Maccabees 15:23 and confirmed by Acts 2:11, where "Cretans" are listed among the listeners on the day of Pentecost.

## Events and Customs

**The Circumcision Group** (οἱ ἐκ τῆς περιτομῆς, v. 10) refers to Jewish believers who insisted that Gentile believers must follow Jewish law, including circumcision. This was a known issue in the early church that had been addressed at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15), but which continued to create tensions in the congregations. On Crete, these people taught "Jewish myths" (Ἰουδαϊκοῖς μύθοις, v. 14), likely speculative interpretations of genealogies and details of the law combined with extra-biblical traditions.

**Household Teaching** is mentioned as problematic because false teachers "ruin whole households" (ὅλους οἴκους ἀνατρέπουσιν, v. 11). In the Greco-Roman world, the household (οἶκος) was the basic social unit, and house churches were the norm for Christian gatherings. Therefore, false teachers gaining influence over entire households represented a serious threat to the stability of the church.

## Key Terms

**Presbyteros and episkopos** appear to refer to the same office in this chapter. In verse 5, Titus is told to appoint "elders" (πρεσβυτέρους), while verse 7 describes the qualifications for an "overseer" (ἐπίσκοπον). This suggests that at this time the two titles described the same function from different angles: "elder" emphasized dignity and maturity, while "overseer" emphasized the role of leader and guardian. The later development toward a clearer distinction between bishop and presbyters is seen only in writings from the second century.

**Theou oikonomos** (θεοῦ οἰκονόμον, "God's steward," v. 7) draws on the image of household stewards in the Greco-Roman world. An oikonomos was a trusted person, often a slave or freedman, who managed the owner's property and household. The title emphasized both responsibility and accountability—the steward acted on behalf of another.

**Hygiainousa didaskalia** (ὑγιαινούσῃ διδασκαλίᾳ, "sound teaching," v. 9) is an expression characteristic of the Pastoral Epistles. The adjective "sound" (ὑγιαίνω) was drawn from medical terminology and implied that false teaching was like a disease that spread and destroyed the body—in this case, the church's "body."
