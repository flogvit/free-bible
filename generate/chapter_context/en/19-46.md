## Places

### God’s City and the Holy Dwelling
Verse 5 mentions "the city of God" (עִיר־אֱלֹהִים) and "the holy dwelling of the Most High" (קְדֹשׁ מִשְׁכְּנֵי עֶלְיוֹן). This refers to Jerusalem and Mount Zion, where the temple stood. The image of a river with streams that bring joy to the city is remarkable, since Jerusalem had no major river. The Gihon spring was the city’s main water source. Therefore, the river motif likely carries theological significance, pointing to God’s life-giving presence, possibly echoing the rivers of paradise (Genesis 2:10) and future prophecies about life-giving water flowing from the temple (Ezekiel 47:1–12).

## Events and Customs

### Possible Historical Background
Many scholars link this psalm to the failed siege of Jerusalem by the Assyrian king Sennacherib in 701 B.C. during King Hezekiah’s reign. According to 2 Kings 19:35, the angel of the Lord struck down the Assyrian army at night, and the enemy withdrew without capturing the city. The phrase "God helps her when morning dawns" (verse 6) may reflect this dramatic deliverance at daybreak. The destruction of weapons of war in verse 10 also fits this scenario.

## Key Terms

### Alamot (עַל־עֲלָמוֹת)
The heading contains the musical term "alamot," which likely means "for soprano voices" or "in a high register." The word is related to the Hebrew word for "young woman" (עַלְמָה) and may indicate that the psalm was intended to be sung by female singers or in a higher pitch.

### YHWH Sabaoth (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת)
The divine name "the Lord, God of hosts" appears twice in the refrain (verses 8 and 12). This name highlights God as the commander of heavenly armies and earthly forces, emphasizing his sovereign power over all nations and powers.

### Selah (סֶלָה)
This liturgical term occurs three times in the psalm (verses 4, 8, and 12) and likely marks pauses in the performance, perhaps for instrumental interludes or to allow time for meditation on the text.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

### Sennacherib’s Prism
The so-called "Taylor Prism" or "Sennacherib’s Prism" (now in the British Museum) describes the Assyrian king’s campaign against Judah. Interestingly, the inscription boasts of having trapped Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem but never mentions that the city was captured. This absence confirms the biblical testimony that Jerusalem was miraculously spared, which may have inspired this psalm’s declarations of trust.
