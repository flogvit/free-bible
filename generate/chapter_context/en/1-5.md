# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 5

## People

The chapter presents ten generations from Adam to Noah in a systematic genealogy. Several names deserve special attention: **Seth** (שֵׁת) means "appointed" or "substitute," pointing back to Genesis 4:25 where Eve sees him as a substitute for Abel. **Enoch** (חֲנוֹךְ) means "dedicated" and stands out due to his unique fate – he did not die, but "God took him" (v. 24). **Methuselah** (מְתוּשֶׁלַח) has the longest lifespan in the Bible (969 years), and his name may mean "his death shall bring" – according to traditional chronological calculation, he died the same year the flood came. **Lamech** (לֶמֶךְ) names his son **Noah** (נֹחַ) with a prophetic explanation linked to "comfort" (נחם) and rest from the curse upon the earth (v. 29).

## Events and Customs

The genealogy's formula follows a fixed pattern: years lived before the first son, years after the first son, total lifespan, and "then he died." This structured format has parallels in Mesopotamian king lists, particularly the **Sumerian King List**, which contains eight (or in some versions ten) kings before the flood with extremely high lifespans (up to 43,200 years). The biblical list is significantly more modest in its numbers but shares the literary convention of marking a pre-flood era with unusually long life spans. The formula is remarkably broken at Enoch, where "then he died" is replaced by "God took him" – a unique deviation that highlights his special relationship with God.

## Key Terms

The expression **"This is the book of the generations of Adam"** (סֵפֶר תּוֹלְדֹת אָדָם) uses the important Hebrew word *toledot*, which here marks a new section in the narrative. The description that Seth was born **"in Adam's likeness, after his image"** (בִּדְמוּתוֹ כְּצַלְמוֹ) mirrors the wording of 1:26 regarding humanity's creation in God's image, showing that the divine image is carried forward through generations. Regarding Enoch it is said that he **"walked with God"** (וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים), an expression which in Hebrew (*hithallek*) suggests a continuing, intimate relationship – the same expression is later used only of Noah (6:9).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Sumerian King List**, preserved on several cuneiform tablets (including the Weld-Blundell prism from around 1800 BC), shows striking structural similarities with Genesis 5. The list contains kings who reigned before the flood, followed by the notice "the flood swept over the land." Then the list continues with significantly shorter reigns. Although the numbers in the Sumerian list are astronomically higher, both texts share the idea of a dramatic transition at the flood and a gradual reduction of human lifespans afterward. This suggests a shared cultural memory in the ancient Near East about a pre-flood time with exceptional conditions.
