## People

**Ahasuerus (Xerxes I)** is the Persian king who stands central in chapter 1. The Hebrew name אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ (*Aḥashverosh*) corresponds to the Persian *Khshayarsha*, which became Xerxes in Greek. In this chapter, he is presented as ruler over a vast empire, and his character is revealed through the magnificent banquet and his fierce reaction to Vashti's disobedience.

**Vashti** (וַשְׁתִּי) is the queen who refuses to obey the king's command. Her name may be derived from the Persian *vahišta* ("the best"). In the chapter, she appears as an independent figure who holds her own banquet for the women and refuses to display her beauty before the king's male guests.

**The seven eunuchs** are named (verse 10): Mehuman, Biztha, Harbona, Bigtha, Abagtha, Zethar, and Karkas. **The seven princes** (verse 14) – Carshena, Shethar, Admatha, Tarshish, Meres, Marsena, and Memucan – form the king's innermost circle of advisors. The number seven was significant in Persian administration. It is Memucan who proposes the judgment against Vashti (verses 16-20).

## Places

**Shushan (Susa)** is referred to as שׁוּשַׁן הַבִּירָה (*Shushan habirah*), "the fortress of Susa" (verses 2, 5), indicating the fortified royal residence where the events of the chapter take place.

**The extent of the empire** is described as "from India to Cush" with 127 provinces (מְדִינָה, verse 1). Cush corresponds to the region south of Egypt, while India refers to the areas around the Indus River – this establishes the scope of Ahasuerus' power as the framework for the chapter.

**The garden court of the king's palace** (חֲצַר גִּנַּת בִּיתַן הַמֶּלֶךְ, verse 5) was the location of the second banquet, lasting seven days, for all the people in Susa.

## Events and Customs

**The 180-day banquet** (verses 3-4) in the king's third year of reign was not a continuous drinking party, but rather an extended display of the empire's splendor for officials, military commanders, and nobles from all provinces. This was followed by a seven-day banquet for everyone in Susa (verse 5).

**The drinking custom** is described with the expression כַדָּת אֵין אֹנֵס ("according to the law, without compulsion," verse 8). In Persian and Greek banquets, it was customary that all had to drink equally. The king's suspension of this rule is presented as a special favor.

**The queen's separate banquet** (verse 9) reflects Persian court etiquette where women and men often celebrated separately. That Vashti held a banquet for the women in "the royal house" (בֵּית הַמַּלְכוּת) demonstrates her high rank.

**Vashti's disobedience and judgment** (verses 10-22): On the seventh day, when the king was "glad from wine", he sent his seven eunuchs to bring Vashti wearing the royal crown. Her refusal led to the king's anger and a formal consultation with the princes regarding what punishment the law required.

## Key Terms

**פַּרְתְּמִים** (*partemim*, verse 3) is a Persian loanword designating the Persian nobility or aristocracy.

**דָּת** (*dat*, verses 8, 13, 15, 19) means "law" or "decree" and is a Persian loanword. The expression "the law of the Persians and Medes that cannot be altered" (verse 19) reflects a well-known concept regarding Persian legal tradition.

**סָרִיס** (*saris*, verses 10, 12, 15) is often translated as "court official" or "eunuch." In Persian courts, eunuchs held important roles as intermediaries and trusted servants with access to the king's private chambers.

**כְּטוֹב לֵב־הַמֶּלֶךְ בַּיָּיִן** ("when the king's heart was glad from wine," verse 10) is a euphemism for drunkenness that sets the stage for the king's unwise command.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

Verse 6 gives an unusually detailed description of the palace's splendor with חוּר כַּרְפַּס וּתְכֵלֶת (white linen and blue purple), גְּלִילֵי כֶסֶף (silver rings), עַמּוּדֵי שֵׁשׁ (marble pillars) and floors of בַּהַט וָשֵׁשׁ וְדַר וְסֹחָרֶת (alabaster, marble, mother-of-pearl, and colored stone). The many Persian loanwords in the description testify to the author's knowledge of Persian court culture.

The chapter contains a striking number of Persian terms and names, giving the text an authentic Persian character and placing the reader directly into this foreign court environment.
