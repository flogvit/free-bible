# Historical and Cultural Context for Galatians 6

## Events and Customs

Verse 6 describes a practice in which the one being instructed in the word (ὁ κατηχούμενος) should share all good things with their teacher (τῷ κατηχοῦντι). This reflects an established Christian teaching custom that had already developed during the first decades. The verb "katecheo" (κατηχέω) means to teach orally and gave rise to our word "catechesis." In the Greco-Roman world, it was common for students to support their philosophical teachers financially. Paul transfers this custom into church life, where those teaching in the faith were to receive material support from those they taught.

The circumcision controversy in verses 12–13 partly concerns avoiding persecution. By requiring circumcision, Christians could appear to belong to Judaism, which had a protected status within the Roman Empire. Paul reveals that part of the motivation behind the demand for circumcision was to "make a good impression in the flesh" (εὐπροσωπῆσαι ἐν σαρκί)—that is, to avoid social and political persecution linked to the scandal of the cross.

## Key Terms

The expression "the law of Christ" (τὸν νόμον τοῦ Χριστοῦ) in verse 2 is unique in the New Testament. In the context of Galatians, it stands in contrast to the law discussed in the preceding chapters. Paul presents an alternative "law"—the commandment of love fulfilled through mutual care and bearing one another's burdens.

The word "stigmata" (στίγματα) in verse 17 refers to brand marks or tattoos used on slaves, soldiers, or temple devotees to show ownership or allegiance. In a religious context, followers of certain cults bore physical marks demonstrating their devotion to a deity. Paul uses this image provocatively: while his opponents want to place the mark of circumcision upon the Galatians, he himself bears the scars from persecution (whipping, stoning) as his own "ownership marks" belonging to Christ.

The expression "new creation" (καινὴ κτίσις) in verse 15 has roots in prophetic and Jewish expectations of God's renewal of creation (cf. Isa 65:17). Paul declares that this eschatological reality has already broken in, making the distinction between circumcised and uncircumcised irrelevant.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verse 11 notes that Paul writes with "large letters" (πηλίκοις γράμμασιν) in his own hand. Papyrus finds from antiquity show it was common practice for a secretary to write the main body of a letter, while the sender added the conclusion personally to authenticate the document. The "large letters" may be due to Paul not being a professional scribe, possibly having eye problems (a possible interpretation of 4:13–15), or writing in large script to emphasize the importance of the conclusion. Some scholars view this as an authenticity marker, where Paul indicates the transition from the secretary's handwriting to his own.
