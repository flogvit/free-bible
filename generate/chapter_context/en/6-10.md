# Historical and Cultural Context for Joshua 10

## People

**Adoni-Zedek** (אֲדֹנִי־צֶדֶק, "my lord is righteousness") was the king of Jerusalem and leader of a coalition of five Amorite kings. His name follows a pattern known from earlier rulers of Jerusalem—in Genesis 14:18 we encounter Melchizedek ("king of righteousness"). The Amarna letters from the 14th century BC mention Jerusalem rulers such as Abdi-Heba, confirming the city's significance as a political center during this period. The other four kings—Hoham (הוֹהָם) of Hebron, Japhia (יָפִיעַ) of Lachish, Piram (פִּרְאָם) of Jarmuth, and Debir (דְּבִיר), king of Eglon—represented important city-states in the Shephelah (lowlands) and the southern highlands.

## Places

The chapter covers a large geographical area from Gibeon in the north to Kadesh-Barnea in the south. **Gibeon** (modern el-Jib) lies about 9–10 km north-northwest of Jerusalem and was archaeologically a significant city in the Bronze Age. The **Beth-Horon pass** was the strategically important main route between the coastal plain and the highlands—a natural location for pursuing fleeing enemies. **Lachish** (Tell ed-Duweir) has been thoroughly excavated and shows destruction layers from the late Bronze Age, although the dating of these layers is debated. The **cave at Makkedah**, where the five kings hid, has never been securely identified, but the area around the Aijalon Valley contains many natural caves in the limestone.

## Events and Customs

The ritual of placing a foot on the necks of defeated kings (v. 24) is well documented in ancient Near Eastern iconography. Egyptian and Assyrian reliefs depict victors trampling their enemies, symbolizing total submission and dominance. **Hanging on trees** (v. 26) was not an execution method but a public display of already killed enemies to demonstrate victory and bring shame. Joshua's command to take down the bodies before sunset (v. 27) follows the instruction in Deuteronomy 21:22–23 and shows that even in warfare, the laws of the Torah applied.

## Key Terms

**Herem** (חֵרֶם, "ban/devotion to destruction") is used repeatedly in the chapter regarding the conquered cities. This was not arbitrary destruction but a religious act in which everything was "given over" to God—nothing could be kept as war booty. The term **"Book of Jashar"** (סֵפֶר הַיָּשָׁר, "the Book of the Upright," v. 13) refers to a lost poetic collection also mentioned in 2 Samuel 1:18, which evidently contained ancient heroic songs from Israel's early history.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Excavations at Lachish have revealed massive destruction layers testifying to violent conquests in the late Bronze Age, illustrating the city's military significance throughout history. At Gibeon, archaeologists have found a large water reservoir and industrial winepress installations, confirming the city's description as "great" and important (v. 2). The hailstorm described in verse 11 cannot be verified archaeologically, but such catastrophic storms are climatologically possible in the region and are also mentioned in other ancient Near Eastern texts as divine intervention in war.
