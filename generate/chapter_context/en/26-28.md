# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 28

## People

The chapter addresses two figures in Tyre: **נְגִיד** (*nagid*, "prince/ruler") in verses 1–10 and **מֶלֶךְ** (*melek*, "king") in verses 11–19. This distinction may reflect that Tyre had both a political ruler and a sacred royal tradition. The historical ruler at Ezekiel's time was likely **Ittobaal III** (Ethbaal III), who reigned during the Babylonian siege. Phoenician kings traditionally had close ties to the temple and priesthood, and royal self-exaltation to divine status was known in the eastern Mediterranean region.

The reference to **Daniel** (verse 3) shows a textual variant: דָּנִאֵל (*ketiv*) / דָּנִיֵּאל (*qere*). The shorter form without the yod may point toward the legendary wisdom tradition known from Ugaritic texts (the Dan'el story), rather than the biblical prophet Daniel. This was a mythical figure of wisdom known in Canaanite tradition.

## Places

**Tyre** is described as located "in the heart of the seas" (בְּלֵב יַמִּים), an accurate description of the island city situated about 800 meters from the mainland. This location gave the city exceptional defensive capabilities and contributed to the ruler's arrogance. **Sidon** (verses 21–24), Tyre's neighboring city and occasional rival, was the oldest of the Phoenician city-states and the mother city of Tyre. At Ezekiel's time, Sidon was politically subordinate to Tyre but remained an important religious and commercial center.

## Events and Customs

The description of "God's garden" (עֵדֶן גַּן־אֱלֹהִים) with precious stones and a cherub walking among "fiery stones" reflects Phoenician and Canaanite temple iconography. The nine precious stones mentioned in verse 13 partially overlap with the twelve stones on the high priest's breastplate (Exodus 28), suggesting a royal-priestly connection. "Fiery stones" may refer to sparkling gemstones or divine spheres in mythological cosmology. The anointed cherub (כְּרוּב מִמְשַׁח) reflects concepts of divine guardian beings known from both Israelite and Phoenician tradition.

The expression "the death of the uncircumcised" (מוֹתֵי עֲרֵלִים, verse 10) was a shameful death in Israelite thinking, since circumcision was the sign of covenant fellowship. The Phoenicians generally did not practice circumcision, so the judgment places Tyre's ruler among the despised nations.

## Key Terms

The word **רְכֻלָּה** (*rekullah*, "trade/merchandise") appears several times (verses 5, 16, 18) and is a key term linking Tyre's wealth with its moral downfall. The trade filled the city with **חָמָס** (*chamas*, "violence/wrongdoing"). **חוֹתֵם תָּכְנִית** (*chotem toknit*, verse 12) is a rare expression – תָּכְנִית is a *hapax legomenon* – which can be translated "seal of perfection" or "pattern of perfection," a title expressing the highest claims of royal ideology.

**סִלּוֹן מַמְאִיר** and **קוֹץ מַכְאִב** (verse 24) – "pricking thorn" and "painful brier" – are metaphors for the hostile influence of neighboring peoples on Israel, imagery known from other prophetic texts.
