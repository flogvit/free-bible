# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 3

## People

**Moses (Μωϋσῆς)** is the central Old Testament figure in this chapter, mentioned by name three times (vv. 7, 13, 15). Paul specifically refers to the account in Exodus 34:29–35, where Moses' face shone after he received the law on Mount Sinai. In Jewish tradition during Paul's time, Moses was the most highly honored figure after the patriarchs, and his role as lawgiver was undisputed. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE–50 CE) described Moses as the perfect prophet and lawgiver. Paul's argument would have been bold for Jewish readers, since he places the ministry of the new covenant above that of Moses.

## Events and Customs

**Letters of recommendation (συστατικῶν ἐπιστολῶν)** were an established practice in the Greco-Roman world. Such letters were used by traveling teachers, philosophers, and merchants to confirm their identity and credibility. In a church context, we see this practice also in Acts 18:27 (Apollos) and Romans 16:1–2 (Phoebe). Paul implies that some opponents in Corinth had brought such letters, while he himself did not need them because the congregation itself was his "letter."

**Synagogue reading** is mentioned indirectly in verses 14–15, where Paul refers to the reading of "the old covenant" (τῆς παλαιᾶς διαθήκης). In synagogues, the Torah was read aloud every Sabbath according to a fixed lectionary. The expression "when Moses is read" (ἀναγινώσκηται Μωϋσῆς) was a common way to describe this worship practice.

## Key Terms

**Διαθήκη (covenant/testament)** is used in this chapter to contrast the old and new covenants (vv. 6, 14). The word could mean both "covenant" (the Hebrew *berit* concept) and "testament" in Greek legal usage. Here Paul draws on the prophet Jeremiah's promise of a "new covenant" (Jer 31:31–34) as background for his argument concerning the ministry of the Spirit versus the ministry of the letter.

**Γράμμα versus πνεῦμα (letter versus spirit)** in verse 6 does not represent an allegorical method of reading, but rather contrasts the arrangements of the old covenant (the law written on stone tablets) with the reality of the new covenant (the Spirit writing on hearts). This builds upon Ezekiel 36:26–27 concerning the transformation of the heart.

**Κάλυμμα (veil/covering)** is used four times in the chapter (vv. 13, 14, 15, 16). The word denotes a piece of cloth that covers something. Paul gives Moses' veil a new interpretation: it covered not only Moses' face but also symbolizes the spiritual blindness that prevents understanding of the Scriptures apart from Christ.

**Δόξα (glory/radiance)** appears ten times in the chapter and is a key word in Paul's argument. The word renders the Hebrew *kavod*, which denotes God's visible presence and majesty. The glory on the stone tablets was temporary (τὸ καταργούμενον), while the glory of the Spirit is permanent and surpasses the former.

**Παρρησία (boldness/freedom of speech)** in verse 12 was an important concept in Greek culture, originally tied to the right of a free citizen to speak openly in the assembly. In a religious context, it denotes the open and fearless access to God that the new covenant provides.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of papyrus letters from Egypt, including letters of recommendation from the first and second centuries CE, confirm the widespread use of such letters as Paul mentions in verse 1. These letters often followed standardized formulas and were kept as documentation of a person's credibility and connection to reliable individuals.

Inscriptions from ancient synagogues and literary sources such as Philo confirm the central place Torah reading held in Jewish devotional life, providing background for Paul's image of the veil lying over the heart "when Moses is read" (v. 15).
