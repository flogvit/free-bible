# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 2

## People

**Hannah** appears in this chapter as the author of a song of praise (vv. 1–10) that bears remarkable similarity to Mary’s song (the Magnificat) in Luke 1. Her prayer represents an important poetic text in the Old Testament expressing God’s care for the lowly and oppressed.

**Eli** was the high priest at the sanctuary in Shiloh. Tradition (cf. 1 Chron. 24:3) traces his lineage to the line of Ithamar among Aaron’s descendants. His role as high priest is emphasized, but the chapter also reveals his weakness as a father. **Hophni and Phinehas**, Eli’s sons, are described as "בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל" (bené belijja'al – "sons of Belial"), a Hebrew expression meaning "wicked men" or "worthless men." This term is otherwise used in the Old Testament to refer to the worst lawbreakers.

## Places

**Shiloh** (v. 14) was Israel’s central sanctuary during the period of the judges, where the tabernacle was located after the conquest of the land (Josh. 18:1). Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Seilun have uncovered a significant cultic site with remains from the Iron Age. The site lies about 30 km north of Jerusalem in the hill country of Ephraim. The chapter shows that Shiloh functioned as a pilgrimage destination where Israelites came annually to offer sacrifices.

## Events and Customs

**Sacrificial practices and priestly rights**: According to the Mosaic Law, priests were entitled to receive the breast and the right thigh from peace offerings (Lev. 7:31–34). The chapter describes how Eli’s sons violated these rules by taking arbitrary portions with a three-pronged fork and demanding raw meat before the fat had been burned for the Lord. This was doubly offensive: they took the wrong portions, and they took them before God’s portion had been offered.

**The women serving at the entrance of the tent of meeting** (v. 22) are referred to as "הַצֹּבְאוֹת" (hatsove'ot), meaning "those who serve." This may refer to women who performed certain religious duties at the sanctuary, possibly also mentioned in Exod. 38:8. Hophni and Phinehas’ sexual abuse of these women represented a gross violation of the holiness of the sanctuary.

**The linen ephod** (v. 18) that Samuel wore was a simple priestly garment made of linen (אֵפוֹד בָּד, 'efod bad), marking his service to the Lord.

## Key Terms

**"Horn"** (קֶרֶן, qeren) in verses 1 and 10 is a central symbolic term. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the horn represented strength, power, and dignity—an image drawn from oxen and wild animals. When Hannah says "my horn is exalted," she expresses that her honor and strength have been restored.

**"Messiah"** (מָשִׁיחַ, masjíach) appears in verse 10, the first time in the Bible used to refer to a future king. The term means "the anointed one" and points prophetically forward to the kingdom that would soon be established. This makes Hannah’s song an early messianic text.

**"Man of God"** (אִישׁ־אֱלֹהִים, 'isj-'elohim) in verse 27 designates a prophet who brings the Lord’s message. The judgment pronounced by this anonymous prophet against Eli’s house anticipates the message that Samuel himself will deliver in chapter 3.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Seilun have confirmed a significant Iron Age cultic site, supporting the description of Shiloh as a central pilgrimage destination in this chapter. The discovery of cultic objects and storage buildings at Shiloh illustrates the site’s importance as a religious center before the temple in Jerusalem was built.
