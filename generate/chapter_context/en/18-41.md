## Key Terms

### Leviathan (לִוְיָתָן)
The chapter continues the description of Leviathan introduced in 40:25 (Hebrew numbering). The Hebrew word is possibly related to the root *lwy* ("to twist, coil"), although its etymology is debated. In Ugaritic literature from Ras Shamra, there appears a similar creature called *Lōtān* – a seven-headed sea monster defeated by the god Baal. In Canaanite mythology, this represented chaotic powers. In the book of Job, Leviathan is not used as an enemy God must fight, but as a creation that demonstrates God's sovereign power over even the most fearsome beings.

### "King over all proud beasts" (מֶלֶךְ עַל־כָּל־בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ)
The expression in verse 26 ("all the sons of pride") uses the Hebrew *bĕnê-šāḥaṣ*, which denotes wild, unruly creatures. This royal image connects Leviathan to the Ancient Near Eastern concept of chaotic forces that only the highest deity can control.

## Events and Customs

### Fire-breathing Creature
The description of Leviathan breathing fire and smoke (verses 10–13) has parallels in Mesopotamian and Canaanite iconography, where dragons and sea monsters are often depicted with fire. This was an established mythological motif in the ancient Near East. The text uses poetic images such as "torches" (*lappîdîm*), "sparks" (*kîdôdê ʾēš*) and "smoke from a boiling pot" to portray the creature's fearsome character.

### Impenetrable Armor
The detailed description of the scales (verses 7–9) as tightly fitted "shields" (*māginnîm*) reflects knowledge of military armor. In ancient times, scale armor was well known in Egypt and Mesopotamia, and the image of overlapping scales "sealed" together communicated absolute invincibility to contemporary readers.

## Chapter-Specific Findings
Ugaritic texts (ca. 1400–1200 B.C.) from Ras Shamra in Syria describe the Baal cycle where the god Baal defeats *Lōtān*, "the fleeing serpent" and "the twisting monster." These texts show that the Leviathan motif was widespread in Canaanite culture. However, the book of Job radically reinterprets this motif: instead of a divine battle, Leviathan is presented as God's creation, completely under His control – a point that emphasizes God's absolute sovereignty.
