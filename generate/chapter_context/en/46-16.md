# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 16

## People

The chapter mentions several important figures in the early church. **Timothy** was Paul’s young coworker. In chapter 16, Paul writes about a possible visit from Timothy and urges the congregation to welcome him without fear, since he "does the work of the Lord" just as Paul does. Paul’s concern that Timothy might be "despised" may reflect his youth and perhaps a reserved personality. **Apollos** is mentioned as someone Paul had encouraged to visit Corinth along with other brothers, but Apollos did not wish to go at that time—he would come "when it is convenient" (v. 12). His hesitation may be connected to the divisions in the church, where some said, "I belong to Apollos" (1:12).

**Stephanas and his household** are described as "the firstfruits of Achaia"—the first converts in the province—who have devoted themselves to serving the saints. The three names Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus likely represent the delegation that brought the Corinthians’ letter to Paul. Paul says they have "made up for your absence" (v. 17). **Aquila and Priscilla** (Prisca) were the Jewish couple who had fled Rome after Emperor Claudius expelled the Jews around AD 49. They ran a tentmaking workshop with Paul in Corinth and were now established in Ephesus, where they hosted a house church that sends its greetings.

## Places

**Ephesus**, from which Paul writes, was the capital of the Roman province of Asia and an important religious and commercial center. Paul plans to stay there until Pentecost because "a wide door for effective work" has opened to him, even though there are also "many adversaries" (vv. 8–9). **Macedonia** is mentioned as a place Paul intends to travel through on his way to Corinth, where he hopes to spend the winter.

The **churches in Galatia** are noted as part of a larger network of churches participating in the collection for Jerusalem. **Jerusalem** was the destination of this collection—a relief offering for the poor in the mother church, which also served to demonstrate unity between Gentile and Jewish Christians. The province of **Asia** also sends greetings through its churches (v. 19).

## Events and Customs

The **collection for the saints** (λογεία/logeia) in Jerusalem was a major project Paul organized among Gentile Christian churches (cf. Rom 15:25–27; 2 Cor 8–9). The practice of setting aside money "on the first day of every week" (κατὰ μίαν σαββάτου, literally "on the first [day] of the Sabbath/week") shows that Christians were already gathering on Sundays, the resurrection day, rather than on the Sabbath. This is early evidence of Christian Sunday practice.

The **holy kiss** (φίλημα ἅγιον) was a common greeting in the early church expressing spiritual fellowship and family belonging. The custom was known in both Jewish and Greco-Roman cultures but took on special significance as a sign of Christian unity and reconciliation.

## Key Terms

The expression **"Marana tha"** (Μαράνα θά) in verse 22 is Aramaic and means "Our Lord, come!" This is one of the earliest preserved Christian prayers, and the fact that it remains untranslated in a Greek letter to a predominantly Gentile church shows how central it was in early liturgical practice. It reflects the strong expectation of Christ’s return in the apostolic church.

The word **"anathema"** (ἀνάθεμα) in the same verse refers to something handed over to God’s judgment—cursed or excluded from fellowship. The combination of this serious warning with the Aramaic prayer has led some scholars to suggest that it may have been part of the liturgy at the Lord’s Supper, where those who did not love the Lord were warned before the communal meal.

**"Firstfruits"** (ἀπαρχή) regarding Stephanas’ household uses imagery from Old Testament sacrificial practice, in which the first portion of the harvest was dedicated to God as a sign that the entire harvest belonged to Him.
