## People

The chapter focuses on the four members of Isaac's family in a critical conflict situation. **Isaac** is presented as old and blind (Hebrew *כִּי־זָקֵן* and *וַתִּכְהֶיןָ עֵינָיו*), which makes him vulnerable to deception. **Rebekah** appears as the driving force behind the deception, taking the initiative to secure the blessing for her younger son. **Esau** is described as "hairy" (*אִישׁ שָׂעִר*), a hunter who lives from game, while **Jacob** is "smooth-skinned" (*אִישׁ חָלָק*) – a contrast that becomes central to the deception.

## Events and Customs

The **father's blessing** (*בְּרָכָה*) described in this chapter was a legally binding act of great significance in the ancient Near East. When a patriarch gave his blessing to a son, especially on his deathbed, it was considered irrevocable – as Isaac himself confirms in verse 33: "I have blessed him, and he shall be blessed." The **meal before the blessing** was also culturally significant; eating together created a covenant bond and marked the solemnity of the act.

The **disguise with goat skins** on the hands and the smooth part of the neck (verse 16) was intended to imitate Esau's hairy skin. This detailed deception shows how physically close the blessing ritual was – it included touch (verses 21-22) and kissing (verses 26-27). The smell of the clothes also played a role, as Esau's "good clothes" (*בִּגְדֵי... הַחֲמֻדֹת*) probably carried with them the scent of the fields and nature that Isaac associated with his eldest son.

## Key Terms

The word **מַטְעַמִּים** (*mat'ammim*), translated as "tasty food" or "delicious dishes," appears several times in the chapter (verses 4, 7, 9, 14, 17, 31). The word comes from the root ט-ע-ם meaning "to taste," and refers to specially prepared food for a solemn occasion. This was not everyday food, but festive food that marked the significance of the blessing.

**בְּכֹרָה** (*bekhorah*, birthright) and **בְּרָכָה** (*berakhah*, blessing) are two distinct but related concepts in the chapter. Esau had already sold his birthright (ch. 25), but the blessing was a separate act. His complaint in verse 36 – "he has deceived me twice" – shows that he regarded these as two separate losses. The wordplay on Jacob's name (*יַעֲקֹב*) and the verb "to deceive/supplant" (*וַיַּעְקְבֵנִי*) underscores the meaning of the name: "he who grasps the heel" or "he who supplants."

## Places

**Haran** (Hebrew *חָרָן*) is mentioned in verse 43 as the place of refuge Rebekah suggests for Jacob. There lives her brother Laban, and Jacob's flight there becomes necessary because of Esau's hatred and threats to kill him.
