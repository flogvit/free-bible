## Places

Mark 10 describes Jesus’ journey from Galilee toward Jerusalem, with several geographical stops. "The borders of Judea and the country beyond the Jordan" (v. 1) refers to Perea, the Transjordan region east of the Jordan River that was under Herod Antipas’s rule. This was a common detour for Jewish pilgrims wishing to avoid hostile Samaria on their way to Jerusalem. The route went south along the Jordan Valley to Jericho.

Jericho (v. 46) was a prosperous oasis city in the Jordan Valley, about 250 meters below sea level and approximately 25 kilometers northeast of Jerusalem. In Jesus’ time, there were two Jerichos: the old Canaanite/Jewish town and Herod the Great’s winter palace complex slightly south of the ancient city. Archaeological excavations have uncovered Herodian Jericho with impressive palace ruins, a hippodrome, and a large reservoir. The road from Jericho up to Jerusalem rose more than 1,000 meters over a distance of about 27 kilometers—the final and steepest leg of the pilgrimage journey.

## People

Bartimaeus (vv. 46–52) is unusually presented in detail with both his name and his father’s name ("son of Timaeus"), which is rare in the gospel healing narratives. This may indicate that he was known in the early Christian community. As a blind beggar, he represented society’s most vulnerable—dependent on alms along the busy road where pilgrims passed. His messianic confession "Son of David" is theologically charged and reflects popular expectation of a Davidic deliverer.

James and John, sons of Zebedee (vv. 35–45), were fishermen from Galilee and belonged to Jesus’ inner circle along with Peter. Their request for places of honor reveals typical messianic expectations of an earthly kingdom where fellow believers would hold positions of power.

## Events and Customs

The divorce practice discussed (vv. 2–12) was a hotly debated topic in contemporary Judaism. Two rabbinic schools stood opposed: Shammai allowed divorce only in cases of adultery, while Hillel permitted it for almost any reason. The background was Deuteronomy 24:1 and the phrase "something indecent." That Jesus also addresses women who initiate divorce (v. 12) likely reflects Roman law, under which women had this right—a practice known among Hellenized Jews such as Herodias.

The blessing of children by laying on of hands (vv. 13–16) was an established Jewish custom. Parents sought blessings from respected rabbis and devout men. The Greek word "παιδία" (paidia) refers to young children, not infants. In antiquity, children had no legal status and were often overlooked—the disciples’ reaction reflects this cultural norm.

## Key Terms

The expression "βιβλίον ἀποστασίου" (biblion apostasiou, "certificate of divorce," v. 4) was a legal document that formally dissolved the marriage and freed the woman to remarry. Without this document, a woman who left her husband would be in a very vulnerable position without rights.

The image of "the camel going through the eye of a needle" (v. 25) uses "κάμηλος" (kamēlos) and "τρυμαλιὰ ῥαφίδος" (eye of a needle) to create an absurd contrast between the largest pack animal in the region and the smallest everyday opening. This was typical Oriental hyperbole, not a reference to a supposed "needle gate" in Jerusalem, which is a later legend without historical basis.

The term "λύτρον" (lytron, "ransom," v. 45) was a technical term for the price paid to redeem a slave or prisoner of war. In the Septuagint, the word is used concerning ransom and redemption, giving Jewish listeners rich associations with the Exodus and God’s deliverance of Israel.
