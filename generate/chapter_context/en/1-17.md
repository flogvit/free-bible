## People

In this chapter, a name change marks a new phase in the patriarch’s life. **Abram** (אַבְרָם, "exalted father") receives the name **Abraham** (אַבְרָהָם), explained as meaning "father of a multitude of nations" (אַב־הֲמוֹן גּוֹיִם, verse 5). Likewise, **Sarai** (שָׂרַי) is changed to **Sarah** (שָׂרָה); both names likely mean "princess" or "ruler" (verse 15). In the ancient Near East, changing one’s name at important life transitions was common, and when a deity or ruler gave a new name, it signaled a new identity and destiny. **Ishmael** (יִשְׁמָעֵאל), Abraham’s son with Hagar, was thirteen years old at his circumcision (verse 25). He receives the blessing of becoming the father of twelve princes and a great nation (verse 20), but the covenant is explicitly confirmed with Isaac (verses 19, 21).

## Events and Customs

**Circumcision** (מִילָה) is introduced here as the sign of the covenant between God and Abraham and his descendants. The ritual was to be performed on the eighth day after birth and applied to all male members of the household, including slaves (verses 12–13). Circumcision was widespread in the ancient Near East, often serving as a puberty rite. What is distinctive in Genesis 17 is that it becomes a sign of the covenant administered in infancy and is linked to the promise of land and descendants. The penalty for failing to be circumcised was being "cut off from his people" (verse 14), demonstrating the ritual’s fundamental importance for belonging to the covenant community.

## Key Terms

**El Shaddai** (אֵל שַׁדַּי) is the divine name used when God reveals Himself to Abraham in verse 1. This name appears for the first time in the Bible here. Its meaning is debated; traditionally translated "God Almighty," it may also be connected to the Akkadian *šadû* (mountain) or the Hebrew *šad* (breast), conveying connotations of fertility and nourishment. The name occurs especially in the patriarchal narratives and emphasizes God’s power to fulfill promises concerning descendants.

**Berit olam** (בְּרִית עוֹלָם), "everlasting covenant," is used repeatedly throughout the chapter (verses 7, 13, 19) and underscores that the covenant is not limited by time but applies to all generations. The term **tamim** (תָמִים) in verse 1, often translated as "whole," "blameless," or "perfect," does not refer to moral perfection but to undivided devotion and integrity in one’s relationship with God.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Reliefs from Saqqara in Egypt (circa 2400 BCE) depict circumcision rituals and confirm that the practice was widespread in the region long before Israel’s time. Texts from Mari and other Mesopotamian sources testify that name changes upon entering a covenant or assuming new office were common practice. Assyrian vassal treaties from the second and first millennia BCE show similar covenant formulas containing promises, obligations, and signs—a structure reflected in the covenant between God and Abraham in this chapter.
