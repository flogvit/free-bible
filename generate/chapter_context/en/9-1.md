# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 1

## Characters

**Elkanah** is introduced with a detailed genealogy tracing his lineage back four generations. He is described as an "Ephraimite" (אֶפְרָתִי), indicating his affiliation with the tribe of Ephraim, although 1 Chronicles 6:18-23 places him among the Levites. This can be explained by the fact that Levites lived scattered throughout various tribal territories. The name Elkanah means "God has created" or "God has acquired."

**Hannah** (חַנָּה, "grace" or "favor") and **Peninnah** (פְּנִנָּה, possibly "pearl" or "coral") represent the two wives in a polygamous marriage. Hannah's barrenness places her within the same narrative tradition as Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel—all matriarchs whose infertility was overcome by God's intervention. **Eli** served as the high priest at Shiloh and ministered at the sanctuary there. His two sons, **Hophni** and **Phinehas**, are mentioned here already as priests, preparing the reader for the coming conflict.

## Places

**Ramathaim-Zophim** (הָרָמָתַיִם צוֹפִים, "the two heights of Zophim") was Elkanah's hometown in the hill country of Ephraim. The term "Ramathaim" (a double form) may indicate a twin city or a place with two hills.

**Shiloh** (שִׁלֹה) was Israel's central sanctuary during the period of the judges. The text refers to the sanctuary as "the house of the Lord" (הֵיכַל יְהוָה), suggesting a more permanent structure than the portable tabernacle.

## Events and Customs

**Annual Pilgrimage**: Elkanah's family traveled "year by year" (מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה) to worship and offer sacrifices. This reflects the practice of the three annual pilgrimage festivals commanded in the books of Moses, likely the Feast of Tabernacles, which was the most popular.

**Portions of the Sacrifice (מָנוֹת)**: After a peace offering (זֶבַח), the meat was divided into portions for family members to share in a communal meal. The fact that Elkanah gave Hannah a special portion (מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם – possibly "one double portion" or "one choice portion") demonstrated his love for her despite her barrenness.

**The Nazirite Vow**: Hannah's vow that "no razor shall touch his head" (וּמוֹרָה לֹא־יַעֲלֶה עַל־רֹאשׁוֹ) links Samuel to the Nazirite tradition (Numbers 6:1-21). Unlike temporary Nazirite vows, Samuel's was to last "all the days of his life"—a lifelong consecration similar to Samson's.

**Weaning and Dedication**: In the ancient Near East, children were usually breastfed until they were two or three years old. The offerings Hannah brought—three bulls (or one three-year-old bull, depending on textual tradition; the Septuagint and verse 25 support one bull), an ephah of flour (about 22 liters), and a skin of wine (נֵבֶל יַיִן)—represented a significant sacrifice that underscored the solemnity of dedicating her son to the Lord's service.

## Key Terms

**"The Lord of Hosts"** (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת): This is the first occurrence of this divine name in the Bible. It means "Lord of armies" and emphasizes God's sovereignty over both heavenly and earthly hosts. The name becomes central in the books of Samuel and Kings.

**"The Lord had closed her womb"** (יְהוָה סָגַר רַחְמָהּ): This expression reflects the biblical understanding that fertility is a gift directly from God. The word for "womb" (רֶחֶם) is related to the word for "mercy" or "compassion" (רַחֲמִים), creating a theological connection between God's grace and the gift of life.

**"Worthless woman"** (בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל): Hannah uses this expression to reject Eli's accusation. "Beliyal" means "worthlessness" or "wickedness" and is used for morally reprehensible people. This expression occurs frequently in the books of Samuel.

**שָׁאַל/שְׁמוּאֵל**: The name Samuel (שְׁמוּאֵל) is explained by a wordplay on the verb "to ask" (שָׁאַל). Hannah says, "I asked him of the Lord" (מֵיְהוָה שְׁאִלְתִּיו). Interestingly, this etymology fits the name Saul (שָׁאוּל, "asked for") even better, which may be a deliberate literary connection between these two figures.
