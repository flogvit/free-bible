# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezra 4

## People

The chapter introduces several key figures. **"The adversaries of Judah and Benjamin"** (v. 1) refers to the peoples settled in the former Northern Kingdom by the Assyrians. They claim they have worshipped the God of Israel since **Esarhaddon** (681–669 B.C.) brought them there. This aligns with 2 Kings 17:24–41, which describes the mixed religious practices among these peoples.

**Rehum**, holding the title "beʿel ṭeʿem" (בְּעֵל־טְעֵם, "lord of decrees"), was a high-ranking Persian official, likely a regional governor or commander. **Shimsbai** was a "sofer" (סָפְרָא), a scribe or secretary—a crucial position in the Persian administration. **Ashnappar** (v. 10) is most probably the great Assyrian king Ashurbanipal (668–627 B.C.), who continued the deportation policies of his predecessors.

## Places

**Samaria** (שָׁמְרָיִן) served as the Persian provincial capital for the region. The city had been the Northern Kingdom's capital before 722 B.C. and was now the center of the mixed population opposing the Jews. **"Beyond the River"** (עֲבַר נַהֲרָא) was the official name of the Persian satrapy encompassing the entire area west of the Euphrates, including Syria, Phoenicia, and Judah.

The list of peoples in verses 9–10 mentions groups from various parts of the Assyrian and Babylonian empires: Babylonians, people from Susa (the Elamite capital), and several others. This illustrates the scope of the Assyrian mass deportations.

## Events and Customs

The offer of cooperation in verse 2 and its rejection in verse 3 reflect a fundamental tension in the post-exilic period: Who belonged to true Israel? The returnees insisted that only those who had been in exile and now held Cyrus's mandate had the right to build the temple. This excluded those who had remained in the land or were descendants of imported peoples.

**"Hiring advisors"** (v. 5) was a common practice within the Persian system. Local populations could engage spokespeople at court who could influence royal decisions. This shows that the opponents used official channels rather than merely local harassment.

## Key Terms

The chapter shifts from Hebrew to **Aramaic** in verse 8 and continues in Aramaic until Ezra 6:18. Aramaic was the administrative language of the Persian Empire, so the letters are quoted in their original language. The expression "written in Aramaic and translated" (v. 7) may mean that the letter was written in the Aramaic script and language, or that it was translated for the king.

The terms for taxes in verse 13—**"minda"** (tribute tax), **"bēlō"** (consumption tax), and **"ḥalāḵ"** (road toll)—are Persian loanwords reflecting the empire's complex taxation system. The letter writers argued economically: Jerusalem would threaten the king's revenue.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Persian administrative system described in the chapter—with regional officials, formal letters, and royal archives—is confirmed by many discoveries. **The Persepolis tablets** show how correspondence functioned within the Persian bureaucracy. The expression "book of records" (v. 15) refers to the royal archives kept in the capitals; such archives are known from both Assyrian and Persian sources.

The chronology in the chapter is complex. Verses 6–23 form an insertion describing opposition under later kings (Ahasuerus/Xerxes I and Artaxerxes I), before the text in verse 24 returns to the time of Darius. This literary device gathers various examples of opposition thematically, even though they span nearly a hundred years chronologically.
