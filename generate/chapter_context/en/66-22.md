# Chapter Context for Revelation 22

## Characters

### John
In verse 8, the author identifies himself explicitly: "Κἀγὼ Ἰωάννης ὁ ἀκούων καὶ βλέπων ταῦτα" (And I, John, am the one who hears and sees these things). He falls down to worship the angel but is rebuked and told to worship God alone (vv. 8–9).

### The Angel
The angel who has shown John the visions (vv. 8–9) refuses worship and calls himself "σύνδουλός" (fellow servant)—on the same level as the prophets and those who hold fast to the words of this book. This emphasizes that even heavenly messengers are not to be worshipped.

### Jesus
In verse 16, Jesus speaks directly and identifies himself: "Ἐγὼ Ἰησοῦς" (I, Jesus). He confirms that he has sent his angel to testify to the churches and applies messianic titles to himself.

## Key Terms

### The Water of Life and the Tree of Life
The expression "ποταμὸν ὕδατος ζωῆς" (river of the water of life, v. 1) and "ξύλον ζωῆς" (tree of life, vv. 2, 14, 19) link this chapter directly back to the creation account in Genesis 2–3. In the ancient world, rivers were literally life-giving—civilizations such as Egypt and Mesopotamia depended entirely on their rivers. The image of a crystal-clear river flowing from God’s throne combines this with the temple tradition from Ezekiel 47, where water flows out from the temple and gives life to everything it touches. The tree that bears fruit twelve times a year—once each month—surpasses nature’s usual cycle and signals a new created order in which the leaves serve for "θεραπείαν τῶν ἐθνῶν" (the healing of the nations).

### Alpha and Omega
The expression "τὸ Ἄλφα καὶ τὸ Ὦ" (v. 13) uses the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet as a picture of totality and eternity. This was a known rhetorical device in antiquity—in Hebrew, the equivalent is "aleph and tav". When Christ applies this to himself together with "ὁ πρῶτος καὶ ὁ ἔσχατος" (the first and the last), it is a direct echo of God’s self-designation in Isaiah 44:6, expressing full divine identity.

### The Root and Offspring of David and the Bright Morning Star
The title "ἡ ῥίζα καὶ τὸ γένος Δαυίδ" (the root and offspring of David, v. 16) combines two perspectives: Christ is both the source of David’s lineage (the root) and a descendant of David (the offspring). "Ὁ ἀστὴρ ὁ λαμπρός, ὁ πρωϊνός" (the bright morning star) alludes to Balaam’s prophecy in Numbers 24:17 concerning a star from Jacob and was in Jewish tradition a messianic image. The morning star (Venus) was associated in antiquity with royal power and new hope, since it heralded the dawn.

## Events and Customs

### Sealing versus Openness
In verse 10, John is told: "Μὴ σφραγίσῃς" (Do not seal up) the words of this prophecy. This stands in direct contrast to Daniel 12:4, where the prophet was instructed to seal his scroll because the time was far off. The difference marks an eschatological nearness—"ὁ καιρὸς γὰρ ἐγγύς ἐστιν" (for the time is near). Sealing documents was a common practice in antiquity, both for preservation and to keep the contents secret until the proper time.

### The Warning Against Altering the Text
Verses 18–19 contain a stern warning against adding to or taking away from the words of this book. Such colophons (concluding warnings) were common in ancient texts, both in legal documents and religious writings. We find similar formulations in Deuteronomy 4:2 and in ancient Near Eastern law codes. The purpose was to protect the integrity of the text against changes by copyists and to underscore its authority.

### The Bride and the Spirit’s Invitation
The liturgical cry "Ἔρχου" (Come!) in verse 17 likely reflects early Christian worship practice. The combination of the Spirit and the bride together calling for the coming of the bridegroom mirrors the early church’s "Maranatha" prayer (1 Corinthians 16:22)—an Aramaic expression meaning "Our Lord, come!" This shows that Revelation was intended to be read in the congregation’s assembly.
