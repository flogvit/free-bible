## People

**Manasseh** (מְנַשֶּׁה) reigned for 55 years (c. 687–642 B.C.) – the longest reign of any Judean king. He was the son of the righteous Hezekiah, but he reversed his father’s reforms. Assyrian sources mention Manasseh ("Manasi of Judah") in inscriptions from Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal as a vassal king who paid tribute. His captivity in Babylon (v. 11) aligns with the fact that Assyrian kings used Babylon as a secondary capital and imprisoned rebellious vassals there.

**Amon** (אָמוֹן) reigned only two years (c. 642–640 B.C.) before he was killed in a palace conspiracy. His short reign was marked by idolatry without the repentance his father experienced. "The people of the land" (עַם־הָאָרֶץ, v. 25) – likely Judean citizens who owned land – executed the murderers and installed eight-year-old Josiah as king.

## Places

**Hinnom Valley** (גֵּי בֶן־הִנֹּם, v. 6) was a valley south of Jerusalem where Manasseh practiced child sacrifice. This place became associated with Molek worship and later became so despised that "Gehenna" (גֵּיהִנֹּם, from גֵּי בֶן־הִנֹּם) became a symbol for hell in later Jewish tradition.

**Ophel** (עֹפֶל, v. 14) was the southern extension of the temple hill in Jerusalem. Archaeological excavations have uncovered massive fortifications from the Iron Age in this area, confirming the chapter’s description of Manasseh’s building activities after his return.

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** ("made his sons pass through the fire," v. 6) was a Canaanite practice associated with the god Molek. This custom, known from Phoenician and Punic sources, involved sacrificing children by burning them. Archaeological finds from Carthage (tophet sanctuaries) show the widespread nature of this practice in the Semitic world.

**The host of heaven** (צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם, v. 3, 5) refers to astral worship of the sun, moon, stars, and planets. This cult was especially popular in the Neo-Assyrian Empire and spread to its vassal states. Building altars for this cult in the temple courts (v. 5) represented a direct introduction of Assyrian religious practice into the temple of Yahweh.

**Divination practices** (v. 6) – the text mentions several occult practices: עוֹנֵן (cloud observation/omen-taking), נִחֵשׁ (sign interpretation/augury), כִּשֵּׁף (sorcery), אוֹב (necromancy), and יִדְּעוֹנִי (familiar spirit). These were common in Mesopotamian culture but strictly forbidden under Mosaic law.

## Key Concepts

**Hooks** (בַּחֹחִים, v. 11) – the Hebrew word has the basic meaning of "thorns" and here likely describes a nose ring or hook that was inserted through the nose or lips of captives. Assyrian reliefs show war prisoners being led away with ropes attached to such hooks – a humiliating treatment reserved for rebellious vassals.

**Repentance** – the verb "to humble oneself" (נִכְנַע, v. 12, 19, 23) is central to this chapter. The theology of Chronicles emphasizes that even the worst sinner can find forgiveness through sincere repentance. This theme is unique to Chronicles – the parallel account in 2 Kings does not mention Manasseh’s repentance.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian annals from Esarhaddon (681–669 B.C.) and Ashurbanipal (669–627 B.C.) mention "Manasi, king of Judah" among the vassal kings who delivered building materials for Assyrian construction projects. This confirms Manasseh’s historicity and his status as an Assyrian vassal. A prism list from Nineveh includes his name along with 21 other western kings who paid tribute to Assyria.
