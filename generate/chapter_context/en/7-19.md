# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 19

## People

**The Levite from the Hill Country of Ephraim**: This unnamed man belonged to the tribe of Levi, which held a special religious function in Israel. The Levites did not have their own tribal territory but lived scattered in cities throughout the land. That he resided "in the remotest parts" of the hill country of Ephraim suggests an isolated existence, underscoring the fragmented social structure of that period.

**The Concubine (פִּילֶגֶשׁ, *pilegesh*)**: A concubine had a lower legal status than a primary wife but was still an acknowledged part of the household. She had fewer rights regarding inheritance and bride-price. That she left her husband and returned to her father’s house, where she stayed for four months (v. 2), shows her attachment to her original family. Her fate illustrates women’s vulnerable position during this period.

## Places

**Bethlehem in Judah**: The town from which the concubine came, located about 8 km south of Jerusalem. This city is mentioned several times in Judges (also in the story of Ruth) and held significance as a village within the territory of the tribe of Judah.

**Jebus (Jerusalem)**: The text explains that Jebus "is Jerusalem" (v. 10), indicating that at this time the city was still inhabited by Jebusites and not under Israelite control. The Levite refused to stay there because it was "a foreign city" – a decision with tragic consequences.

**Gibeah in Benjamin**: A town north of Jerusalem within Benjaminite territory. That the town lay on Israelite soil makes the crime all the more shocking – evil came from Israelites, not from the "foreign" Jebusites the Levite had avoided.

## Events and Customs

**The Sacred Duty of Hospitality**: This chapter illustrates both the importance of hospitality and its collapse. The father-in-law’s generous hospitality in Bethlehem (they stayed there for several days, vv. 4–9) stands in sharp contrast to Gibeah’s failure to receive them. That no one "took them into their house" (v. 15) was a serious breach of ancient Near Eastern norms. The old man’s warning "do not spend the night on the square" (v. 20) suggests awareness of the city’s dangers.

**"Men of Belial" (בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל)**: This expression literally means "sons of worthlessness/destruction" and refers to lawless, wicked people. Their demand to "know" (יָדַע) the guest clearly carries sexual meaning, parallel to the story of Sodom in Genesis 19. The offer of women instead of the male guest reflects a tragic prioritization where hospitality toward men was considered more important than women’s safety.

**Dismemberment and Sending Out the Body**: The Levite’s act of cutting his concubine into twelve pieces and sending them to all the tribes of Israel was a dramatic call for united action. Similar practices are known from other Old Testament texts (1 Samuel 11:7, where Saul cut up oxen as a mobilization signal). The twelve pieces corresponded to the twelve tribes of Israel and constituted an unprecedented appeal for national response to a national shame.

## Key Terms

**"There was no king in Israel" (v. 1)**: This repeated phrase in Judges 17–21 explains the moral chaos of that period and points forward to the need for centralized leadership. The expression functions both as a historical description of the situation and as a theological assessment – without unifying authority, society deteriorated into lawlessness.

**"Nebalah" (נְבָלָה) – "Shameful Deed"** (vv. 23–24): This strong word denotes actions that fundamentally break with Israel’s social order and covenant with God. It is used for particularly serious sexual crimes and dishonor to one’s family (cf. Genesis 34:7; 2 Samuel 13:12). That such evil occurred among the Israelites themselves, and not among the Canaanites, intensifies the story’s shock.
