## Events and Customs

Job 21 contains several references to burial customs in the ancient Near East. In verses 32–33, it describes how the wicked are "brought to the grave" and how "the clods of the valley are sweet to him"—a poetic depiction of burial. The expression that "all people follow after him" reflects the ancient custom of a funeral procession, in which the entire local community participated. That there is also "watch kept over the mound" (גָּדִישׁ) points to the tradition of guarding or gathering at the grave in the period following burial, which was common in Mesopotamian and Canaanite cultures to protect the deceased and show respect.

## Key Terms

The phrase "שֵׁבֶט אֱלוֹהַּ" (God's rod/discipline) in verse 9 is a central expression that combines the image of the shepherd's staff with divine discipline. In wisdom literature, the "rod" represented both protection and correction. Job observes that the wicked apparently escape this divine discipline.

In verse 17, the image "נֵר רְשָׁעִים" (the lamp of the wicked) is used—a common wisdom motif symbolizing life, prosperity, and family line. For a lamp to be "extinguished" meant not only death but also the obliteration of one's legacy and descendants. Here Job directly challenges his friends' claim (compare Bildad's speech in 18:5–6) by asking: "How often does this actually happen?"

The expression "חֲבָלִים יְחַלֵּק בְּאַפּוֹ" (he apportions calamities in his anger) in verse 17 uses the word "חֲבָלִים", which can mean both "ropes/bands," "birth pains," and "calamity/destruction"—a deliberately ambiguous term that strengthens the image of divine judgment.
