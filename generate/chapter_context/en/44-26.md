# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 26

## People

**King Agrippa II** (Herod Agrippa II) was the son of Herod Agrippa I (who died in Acts 12) and the great-great-grandson of Herod the Great. He ruled over territories northeast of Galilee from about 50 AD and had a special responsibility from the Romans to appoint high priests and oversee the temple in Jerusalem. This explains Paul’s comment that Agrippa was "familiar with" Jewish customs and disputed questions (v. 3). Josephus confirms Agrippa’s deep knowledge of Jewish religion and his attempts to mediate during the lead-up to the Jewish War.

**Berenice** was Agrippa’s sister. Their relationship was the subject of gossip in antiquity—Josephus hints at an incestuous affair. She later became the mistress of the future emperor Titus. Her presence at this hearing underscores the ceremonial nature of the occasion.

**Festus** was the Roman procurator of Judea (about 59–62 AD). His outburst, "You are out of your mind, Paul!" (v. 24), reflects a typical Roman skepticism toward Eastern religious claims, especially the doctrine of resurrection.

## Events and Customs

**The Pharisees’ "strictest sect"** (v. 5): Paul uses the word *akribéstatos* (the most accurate/strict) to describe Pharisaism. This was the Jewish group known for its thorough interpretation of the Torah and belief in resurrection—a point Paul strategically highlights for Agrippa.

**"The twelve tribes"** (v. 7): The expression *dōdekáphylon* ("twelve-tribe people") refers to the entire people of Israel, not just the two tribes that returned from exile. The idea of reuniting the ten lost tribes was connected with messianic expectations.

**Voting on capital punishment** (v. 10): Paul’s reference to having "cast his vote" (*katēnenka psēphon*) can be understood literally (that he was a member of the Sanhedrin) or figuratively (that he gave his approval to death sentences). The expression is debated among scholars.

## Key Terms

**"Kicking against the goads"** (v. 14): The expression *pros kentra laktizein* was a well-known Greek proverb describing futile resistance against superior power—like an ox kicking against the goad that drives it. This appears in Greek authors such as Euripides and Pindar, showing that Jesus spoke in culturally recognizable images, here rendered in Hebrew/Aramaic (*tē Hebraïdi dialektō*).

**"Christian"** (v. 28): The word *Christianos* occurs only three times in the NT. Agrippa’s reply is ambiguous in Greek (*en oligō*) and could mean "with little" (short time/few arguments) or be ironic: "Do you think you can make me a Christian so easily?"

**"Not done in a corner"** (v. 26): Paul’s assurance that the events did not happen "in a corner" (*en gōnia*) plays on Greek philosophical language. Publicity and verifiability were important values in Greco-Roman culture, and Paul claims that Jesus’ death and resurrection were public events known to Agrippa himself.
