# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 9

## Places

**Goshen** (verse 26) was the region in the eastern Nile Delta where the Israelites lived. Archaeological investigations have confirmed that this area was well-suited for livestock raising and had a different microclimatic character than the rest of Egypt. The fact that Goshen was spared from the hail can partly be explained by the general observation that the delta experiences less extreme weather than Upper Egypt, but the text primarily highlights this as God's protection.

## Events and Customs

**The Livestock Plague** (דֶּבֶר, *dever*) described in verses 1–7 struck horses, donkeys, camels, cattle, and sheep. Cattle raising was central to the Egyptian economy and religion—several deities were associated with cattle, including Hathor (the cow goddess) and the Apis bull. A plague that wiped out Egypt's livestock would therefore have been both an economic catastrophe and a blow to their religious symbols.

**Boils and Rashes** (שְׁחִין, *shechin*, verses 8–12) were brought about when Moses threw soot (פִּיחַ כִּבְשָׁן, *piach kivshan*) from a furnace toward the sky. Furnaces were used in Egyptian metallurgy, and soot from such furnaces was considered unclean. The fact that even the magicians (חַרְטֻמִּים, *chartummim*) were struck and could not stand before Moses marks a turning point—the Egyptian sorcerers are now completely powerless.

## Key Terms

**"God of the Hebrews"** (אֱלֹהֵי הָעִבְרִים, *Elohei ha-Ivrim*) is used twice in this chapter (verses 1, 13). This expression serves as an identifier before Pharaoh and places Israel's God in contrast to Egypt's many gods. The term "Hebrew" may be related to *habiru/apiru*, a designation known from Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts for semi-nomadic groups.

**"Hardened Heart"** is described with two different Hebrew verbs in this chapter: כָּבֵד (*kaved*, "to make heavy/hard", verses 7, 34) and חָזַק (*chazaq*, "to strengthen/harden", verses 12, 35). Egyptian religion placed great emphasis on the heart—in the Book of the Dead, the deceased's heart was weighed against the feather of truth. Pharaoh's hardened heart is therefore a motif with deep cultural resonance.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Agricultural Details** in verses 31–32 are remarkably precise: flax and barley were destroyed because the barley had headed out (אָבִיב, *aviv*) and the flax was in bloom (גִּבְעֹל, *giv'ol*), while wheat and spelt survived because they mature later. This matches exactly with the Egyptian growing season, where barley and flax are harvested in February–March, while wheat and spelt are harvested in April–May. Such details suggest good knowledge of Egyptian agricultural conditions and place the hailstorm in late winter.

**The Combination of Hail and Fire** (verse 24: וְאֵשׁ מִתְלַקַּחַת בְּתוֹךְ הַבָּרָד, "fire that flamed up within the hail") likely describes a violent thunderstorm with lightning. Severe storms with hail are rare but not unknown in Egypt, especially in the delta region during winter. Egyptian texts refer to such storms as signs of the gods' wrath.
