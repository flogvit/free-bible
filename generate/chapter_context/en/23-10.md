# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 10

## People

**The king of Assyria** appears as the main character in this chapter, although he is not named directly. The context points to several possible kings: Tiglath-Pileser III (745–727 B.C.), Shalmaneser V (727–722 B.C.) who besieged Samaria, or Sennacherib (705–681 B.C.) who threatened Jerusalem. The boast of the Assyrian king in verses 8–14 reflects typical Assyrian royal rhetoric known from inscriptions, where kings portrayed themselves as invincible rulers of the world. The phrase "my princes are all kings" (v. 8) reflects the Assyrian practice of installing vassal kings over conquered territories.

## Places

The chapter mentions several cities that Assyria had conquered: **Carchemish** (by the Euphrates, conquered by Assyria around 717 B.C. under Sargon II), **Calno** (in northern Syria), **Arpad** (near Aleppo, fell in 740 B.C.), **Hamath** (by the Orontes River, conquered in 720 B.C.), **Damascus** (fell in 732 B.C.) and **Samaria** (fell in 722 B.C.). This list represents the boastful record of the Assyrian king and shows the empire’s expansion southward toward Judah.

Verses 28–32 poetically describe an invasion route toward Jerusalem through **Aiath** (עַיַּת, identified with Ai), **Migron**, **Michmash**, **Geba**, **Ramah**, **Gibeah** (Saul’s hometown), **Gallim**, **Laishah**, **Anathoth** (Jeremiah’s later hometown), **Madmenah**, **Gebim** and **Nob**. This route runs from the north through the tribe of Benjamin toward Jerusalem and may reflect either an actual or expected Assyrian advance.

## Events and Customs

The chapter opens with a judgment against those who write "unjust laws" (חִקְקֵי־אָוֶן). This refers to corrupt judges and scribes in Judah who manipulated the legal system. In ancient Israel, scribes were responsible for documenting legal decisions and property transfers. Misusing this power to deprive widows and orphans of their rights was a serious violation of covenant laws (cf. Exod 22:21–24).

The image of Assyria as God’s "rod" (שֵׁבֶט) and "staff" (מַטֶּה) (v. 5) reflects a common Old Testament understanding in which God uses pagan nations as instruments of judgment, without those nations themselves understanding their role in God’s plan.

## Key Terms

**שְׁאָר יָשׁוּב** (*she'ar yashub*, "a remnant shall return") in verses 21–22 is a central theological concept in Isaiah. This was also the name of Isaiah’s son (7:3) and represents both judgment (only a remnant survives) and hope (a remnant will turn back to God). The term "remnant" (שְׁאָר) appears several times in the chapter and expresses the tension between near-total destruction and preserved hope.

**אֵל גִּבּוֹר** (*El Gibbor*, "Mighty God") in verse 21 is the same title used for the Messiah in Isaiah 9:5, thus creating a theological connection between the two chapters.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian annals confirm the conquests mentioned in verses 9–11. Tiglath-Pileser III’s inscriptions describe the capture of Arpad and Damascus, while Sargon II’s annals document the fall of Samaria in 722 B.C. Particularly interesting are Assyrian reliefs showing the removal of idols from conquered cities, which corresponds with the Assyrian king’s boast about having "found kingdoms with their gods" (v. 10). The Assyrian king equated Jerusalem’s God with the idols of other nations—a theological error that Isaiah strongly refutes.
