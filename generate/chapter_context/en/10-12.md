# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 12

## People

**Nathan** was one of David’s court prophets who held a trusted position at the king’s court. His role in this chapter illustrates the unique standing prophets had in Israelite society—they could confront even the king with God’s word. The prophet’s use of a parable to expose David’s sin was a rhetorical technique known from the wisdom tradition of the ancient Near East.

**Bathsheba** is referred to here as "Uriah’s wife" (vv. 9–10, 15), which underscores the seriousness of David’s sin. Later she is called "his wife" (v. 24), and she becomes the mother of Solomon. The name **Jedidiah** (יְדִידְיָהּ, "beloved of the Lord"), which the Lord gives to Solomon through the prophet Nathan (v. 25), is a theophoric name that confirms God’s grace toward the child despite the circumstances surrounding his parents’ relationship.

## Places

**Rabbah** (rabbat benei ammon, "the capital of the Ammonites") was the capital city of Ammon, today’s Amman in Jordan. The city was strategically located near water sources, which explains the reference to "the water city" (v. 27). Archaeological excavations at the Amman citadel have uncovered fortifications from the Bronze Age. The conquest of Rabbah marked the end of the war that began in chapters 10–11, and Joab’s tactical wisdom is shown in his allowing David to take credit for the final victory.

## Events and Customs

**Fourfold restitution** (v. 6) corresponds with the Mosaic Law regarding theft of small livestock (Exodus 21:37 [22:1 in some Bibles]). Thus, David’s judgment against the man in the parable became a judgment against himself.

**The fast and mourning ritual** that David practiced while the child was ill (vv. 16–17) included lying on the ground, refusing food, and neglecting personal care. That he ended his mourning when the child died was unusual—normally the period of grief began at death. David’s explanation (vv. 22–23) reveals a pragmatic theology concerning the limits of prayer and the irreversible nature of death.

**The king’s war spoils** included the crown of the Ammonite king (v. 30). Its weight of one talent of gold (about 34 kg) has led some scholars to suggest that the crown was worn only on special occasions or that the text includes precious stones in its total weight.

## Key Terms

The expression **"you are the man"** (*attah ha'ish*, אַתָּה הָאִישׁ, v. 7) is one of the Bible’s most dramatic turning points. Nathan uses a legal form in which the king, as supreme judge, pronounces judgment in a case, only to reveal that he has judged himself.

The verb **"despise"** (*bazah*, בָּזָה, vv. 9–10) is used concerning David’s attitude toward the Lord’s word. This is a covenantal term denoting breach of the relationship between God and humanity.

**"The Lord has taken away your sin"** (*gam-YHWH he'evir chattatekha*, v. 13) shows an important theological principle: repentance and confession lead to forgiveness, yet the consequences of sin may still fall. Psalm 51 is traditionally linked to this event.
