# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 9

## People

**Herod Antipas** (vv. 7-9) was tetrarch of Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39. The title "tetrarch" (τετραάρχης) originally meant "ruler over a fourth," but during this period it referred to a subordinate prince. He was the son of Herod the Great and the Herod who executed John the Baptist. His confusion about Jesus and his desire to see him foreshadow the trial in Luke 23:6-12.

**Moses and Elijah** (vv. 30-31) represent, respectively, the Law and the Prophets in Jewish tradition. The fact that these two specifically appear is significant: both met God on a mountain, both had unusual endings to their lives (Moses' unknown grave, Elijah's ascension), and both were connected with messianic expectations. Their conversation about Jesus' "exodus" (ἔξοδος) in Jerusalem creates a deliberate parallel to Israel's departure from Egypt.

## Places

**Bethsaida** (v. 10) was a fishing village at the northern end of the Sea of Galilee that Philip (another son of Herod the Great) upgraded to city status and renamed Julias in honor of Livia Julia, the wife of Emperor Augustus. Archaeological excavations at et-Tell have uncovered a city from this period with fishing gear, anchors, and other artifacts confirming the site's character. The town lay on the border between Galilee and Philip's territory.

**The mountain** of transfiguration (v. 28) is traditionally identified with Tabor, but many scholars consider Hermon (2,814 m) more likely, given its proximity to Caesarea Philippi mentioned in parallel passages (Matt 16:13; Mark 8:27). The Greek expression "τὸ ὄρος" (the mountain) evokes associations with Sinai/Horeb, where both Moses and Elijah had their encounters with God.

**A Samaritan village** (vv. 52-53) illustrates the deep hostility between Jews and Samaritans. The conflict had roots going back to the divided kingdom and the Assyrian resettlement of Samaria with foreign populations (2 Kings 17:24-41). The Samaritans accepted only the books of Moses and had their own temple on Mount Gerizim. Their refusal to welcome Jesus because he was heading to Jerusalem reflects this religious rivalry.

## Events and Customs

**The sending out of the Twelve** (vv. 1-6) followed a pattern known from traveling Jewish teachers. The instructions not to take a staff, bag, bread, or money emphasized radical dependence on God's provision and the hospitality of recipients. Shaking the dust off one's feet (v. 5) was a symbolic action Jews typically performed when leaving pagan territory—doing this toward a Jewish town was a strong testimony of rejection.

**The blessing over the meal** (v. 16) followed the standard form of Jewish table blessing (berakah): One took the bread, looked up to heaven, blessed God, broke the bread, and distributed it. That Jesus organized the crowd into groups of fifty (v. 14) may have evoked memories of Israel's organization in the wilderness under Moses (Exod 18:21).

**Peter's suggestion about three shelters** (v. 33) may reflect the practice of the Feast of Tabernacles, where Israelites built temporary dwellings. This feast was also connected with messianic expectations concerning God's presence among his people.

## Key Terms

**"God's Messiah"** (τὸν χριστὸν τοῦ θεοῦ, v. 20) is Peter's confession and combines the Greek translation of "the anointed one" with a genitive construction that emphasizes that this Messiah is chosen and sent by God himself. This differs from political messianic expectations of the time.

**"The Son of Man"** (ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, vv. 22, 44, 58) is Jesus' preferred self-designation. The expression alludes to Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" receives eternal dominion. By using this ambiguous term, Jesus could hint at his identity without triggering the political associations linked with "Messiah."

**"Exodus"** (ἔξοδος, v. 31) is used concerning Jesus' impending death in Jerusalem. The word means "departure" and creates a deliberate theological parallel to Israel's deliverance from Egypt. Jesus' death is presented as a new and final liberation.

**"Analempsis"** (ἀνάλημψις, v. 51) is often translated as "taking up" and refers to Jesus' coming ascension. This rare word marks a turning point in Luke's narrative—from here on, Jesus is firmly determined to go to Jerusalem.
