## People

**Caleb son of Jephunneh** appears as a central figure in this chapter (vv. 13–19). The text emphasizes that he received Hebron "according to the Lord’s word" (cf. Josh 14:6–15), linking his inheritance directly to divine promise. Caleb was a Kenizzite (cf. v. 17, where Othniel is called "son of Kenaz"), from a clan that was incorporated into the tribe of Judah, reflecting the ethnic complexity of early Israelite society.

**Othniel’s conquest of Debir** (vv. 15–17) introduces him as Caleb’s nephew or younger brother (Hebrew אֲחִי can mean either). The story of Achsah, Caleb’s daughter, who requests water springs as a dowry (vv. 18–19), offers a rare glimpse into women’s agency in property matters in ancient Israel.

## Places

**Hebron** (קִרְיַת אַרְבַּע, "Kirjath-Arba," vv. 13, 54) was one of the oldest and most important cities in the region. The name "Kirjath-Arba" means "city of four" or "Arbah’s city," and Arbah is described as the ancestor of the Anakim (v. 13). Archaeological excavations at Tel Rumeida have uncovered settlement layers from the Bronze Age, confirming the city’s long history.

**The boundary description** (vv. 1–12) outlines Judah’s territory with precision: from the Desert of Zin in the south, along the Salt Sea (Dead Sea) to the east, through the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem, and westward to the Mediterranean Sea. Several mentioned places have archaeological confirmation: **Lachish** (v. 39) has been thoroughly excavated and shows significant Canaanite and later Israelite settlement. **Ekron** (vv. 45–46), **Ashdod**, and **Gaza** (v. 47) were Philistine cities that Judah claimed, but likely never fully controlled.

**En Gedi** (עֵין גֶּדִי, v. 62) in the desert region by the Dead Sea is archaeologically documented with settlements from early periods. The name means "spring of the young goat" and refers to the life-giving springs in an otherwise barren landscape.

## Events and Customs

**Casting lots** (גּוֹרָל, *goral*, v. 1) was the method used for distributing the land. This was understood as a way for God to determine the allocation. The practice ensured that no one could accuse Joshua of favoritism and gave the distribution divine legitimacy.

**The dowry custom** described in vv. 16–19 illustrates marriage arrangements in the ancient Near East. Caleb’s offer of his daughter to whoever conquered Debir reflects the connection between military prowess and marital alliances. Achsah’s subsequent request for "gulot mayim" (גֻּלֹּת מָיִם, water springs/pools) shows that women could negotiate property rights, and that water resources were crucial in the dry southern landscape.

## Key Terms

**Anakim** (עֲנָק, vv. 13–14) were a people known for their large stature, often translated as "giants." The name may be linked to the Hebrew word for "necklace" or "long neck." The three named Anakites—Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai—who were driven out by Caleb, perhaps represent clans rather than individuals.

**Jebus/Jebusites** (vv. 8, 63) were the pre-Israelite population of Jerusalem. The chapter’s closing remark that "the Jebusites live with the people of Judah in Jerusalem to this day" (v. 63) reflects the situation at the time this part of the text was written.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The list of places in Joshua 15 contains approximately 115 place names. Many can be identified with modern locations. The geographical organization—Negev (vv. 21–32), Shephelah/lowland (vv. 33–47), hill country (vv. 48–60), and wilderness (vv. 61–62)—reflects distinct ecological zones that still characterize the region.

Archaeological investigations at several of the mentioned sites, such as Beth Shemesh (v. 10), Azekah (v. 35), Lachish (v. 39), and Makkedah (v. 41), have uncovered remains from the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age.
