# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 7

## Events and Customs

Paul opens the chapter with an illustration drawn from marriage law (verses 1–3). In both Jewish and Roman law, a marriage was binding as long as both spouses were alive, and death released the surviving spouse from the marital bond. The Greek term *hypandros* (ὕπανδρος, "under a husband") describes the legal status of a married woman. Under Roman law, a woman was often subject to *manus* (the authority of her husband), while Jewish tradition recommended that widows wait for a period (usually three months) before remarrying. Paul uses this universally understood legal reality to illustrate how death nullifies the binding power of the law.

The command "You shall not covet" (Οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις) in verse 7 quotes from the Mosaic Law (Exodus 20:17; Deuteronomy 5:21). This command was especially central to Jewish ethical teaching because it addressed not only outward actions but also inner attitudes. Rabbinic tradition regarded coveting as the root of other sins, which explains why Paul chooses this particular command to illustrate the law's function as a revealer of sin.

## Key Terms

The word *nomos* (νόμος, "law") is used in several ways in this chapter: referring to the Mosaic Law, to a general principle, and to the power of sin operating as a law within human members. This ambiguous usage was common in Jewish-Hellenistic literature and requires the reader to follow the context carefully.

The expression *sarx* (σάρξ, "flesh") in verses 14–25 refers not primarily to the physical body itself, but to the human condition under the power of sin—that which is weak and susceptible to temptation. This is a key Pauline term that differs from Greek philosophical dualism, where the body itself was considered evil.

The concept *ho esō anthrōpos* (ὁ ἔσω ἄνθρωπος, "the inner person") in verse 22 has parallels in Greek philosophy, particularly in Plato's Republic (589a), but Paul uses it within a distinct biblical framework. For Paul, it describes that part of the human being which recognizes and rejoices in God's will, in contrast to the flesh, which is subject to the power of sin.
