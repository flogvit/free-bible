# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 47

## People

**The Sons of Korah** (בְנֵי־קֹרַח) are mentioned in the superscription (v. 1) as the authors of this psalm. They were a Levitical family of singers responsible for temple music.

## Events and Customs

This psalm likely reflects a liturgical procession in which the ark of the covenant was carried up to the temple, an idea suggested by verse 6 ("God has gone up with a shout"). The language recalls 2 Samuel 6:15, where David brought the ark to Jerusalem "with shouting and the sound of the horn". Clapping (תִּקְעוּ־כָף, "clap your hands") and shouting (הָרִיעוּ) were common elements in royal enthronement ceremonies in the ancient Near East. The blowing of the shofar marked solemn occasions, especially the New Year festival (Rosh Hashanah), when God’s kingship was proclaimed.

## Key Terms

**Teruah** (תְּרוּעָה) in verse 6 refers to a loud, piercing shout or blast of the horn that signaled royal presence or military victory. **Maskil** (מַשְׂכִּיל) in verse 8 calls for a "skillful" or "wise" song of praise. The exact meaning of this term is debated. **Elyon** (עֶלְיוֹן, "Most High") in verse 3 was an ancient Semitic divine title that the Israelites used to express Yahweh’s sovereignty over all gods.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The psalm’s depiction of God’s ascension to his throne (vv. 6, 9) shares thematic parallels with ancient Near Eastern coronation rituals but applies them to Yahweh’s universal reign. The reference to "the God of Abraham" (v. 10) and the gathering of the nations reflects the covenant promise that all families of the earth would be blessed through Abraham (Genesis 12:3). The verb זַמְּרוּ ("sing praises") is repeated four times in verses 7–8, emphasizing the psalm’s urgent call to worship.
