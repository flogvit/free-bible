# Historical and Cultural Context for Ruth 2

## People

**Boaz** is introduced in this chapter as a central figure. He is described as an אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל (*ish gibbor chayil*), an expression denoting a man of significant social status, wealth, and standing in the community. The term can also imply moral integrity and capability. Boaz belonged to Elimelech's family line, making him a potential kinsman-redeemer (*go'el*) for Naomi and Ruth. His generous treatment of Ruth, a foreign woman, portrays him as a man of exemplary piety and social awareness.

## Events and Customs

**The Right to Glean** (*leket*) was a central welfare provision in Israelite society, rooted in the Mosaic Law (Lev 19:9-10; 23:22; Deut 24:19-22). The law commanded landowners to allow the poor, widows, fatherless, and foreigners to gather what was left behind by the harvesters. This was not charity but a legally established right for the marginalized. Ruth made use of this provision as both a widow and a foreigner. That Boaz instructed his workers to deliberately let stalks fall from the sheaves (*tsebatim*) for her went far beyond the minimum requirements of the law.

**Harvest Work** was carried out collectively, with clear hierarchies. The foreman (*hanna'ar hannitsav*) supervised the harvesters. Women and men often worked in separate groups. A meal consisting of bread, vinegar (*chomets*) for dipping, and roasted grain (*qali*) was typical food for laborers during harvest time.

## Key Terms

**Go'el** (גֹּאֵל) – "the kinsman-redeemer" – is mentioned for the first time in verse 20. This was a relative with both legal obligation and the right to protect family interests, including buying back sold land, redeeming relatives from slavery, and in certain cases marrying a childless widow to raise up offspring for the deceased. Naomi's identification of Boaz as "one of our kinsman-redeemers" hints at possibilities that unfold later in the story.

**To seek refuge under the wings of the Lord** (verse 12: לַחֲסוֹת תַּחַת־כְּנָפָיו) is a poetic image comparing God's protection to a bird sheltering its young. This imagery appears in several places in the Psalms and gains particular resonance later in the narrative.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The ephah** that Ruth gathered (about 22 liters of barley) represents an unusually large amount for one day's work – enough to feed two women for approximately two weeks. This underscores both Ruth's diligence and Boaz's generosity. Archaeological discoveries of measuring vessels from the Iron Age confirm standardized units of measurement in ancient Israel.
