# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 10

## People

**Cornelius** was a centurion (ἑκατοντάρχης) in the Italian cohort (σπείρης τῆς καλουμένης Ἰταλικῆς). A centurion typically commanded around 80 soldiers (a centuria) and formed the backbone of the Roman army. Roman cohorts bearing the designation 'Italica' are historically attested through inscriptions, including "Cohors II Italica civium Romanorum" known from the Syria region, although the exact chronology of their stationing is debated. These units consisted of Roman citizens recruited from Italy, which gave them higher status than auxiliary troops. Cornelius is described as "God-fearing" (φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν), a technical term for Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel, followed moral commandments, and gave alms, but had not undergone full conversion including circumcision.

**Simon the tanner** (Σίμωνι βυρσεῖ) with whom Peter stayed in Joppa is an interesting detail. The tanning trade was considered unclean in Jewish tradition due to constant contact with dead animals. The fact that Peter already lived with a tanner suggests some flexibility regarding purity issues even before the vision.

## Places

**Caesarea Maritima** was the headquarters of Roman administration in Judea, built by Herod the Great between 25–13 BC. The city had an impressive artificial harbor, an amphitheater, a hippodrome, and a temple dedicated to Emperor Augustus. Archaeological excavations have revealed the city's magnificent Roman architecture, including the famous Pilate inscription from 1961. As an administrative center, the city was heavily influenced by Roman and Hellenistic culture, with a significant non-Jewish population.

**Joppa** (Ἰόππην), modern Jaffa/Tel Aviv, was an ancient port city with a substantial Jewish population. The distance between Joppa and Caesarea was about 50 kilometers, roughly a day's journey on foot. Peter's prayer on the roof (τὸ δῶμα) at the sixth hour (around noon) occurred between traditional Jewish prayer times, while Cornelius' vision at the ninth hour (around 3 PM) corresponded to the afternoon sacrifice in the temple and the traditional afternoon prayer.

## Events and Customs

**Purity laws and food regulations** are central to Peter's vision. The sheet containing "all kinds of four-footed animals, reptiles, and birds" (v. 12) included animals forbidden in Leviticus 11. The words κοινὸν καὶ ἀκάθαρτον ("unclean and profane") refer to the complex Jewish system where something could be ritually unclean either by nature or through contact. Peter's reaction, "I have never eaten" (οὐδέποτε ἔφαγον), shows how deeply internalized these regulations were.

**Table fellowship between Jews and Gentiles** was a serious cultural taboo that went far beyond food laws. Peter expresses this explicitly: "You know it is unlawful (ἀθέμιτον) for a Jew to associate with or visit anyone from another nation" (v. 28). This was not direct Torah law but had developed as an "oral law" during the Second Temple period to preserve Jewish identity under foreign rule.

## Key Terms

**"God-fearing"** (φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν) was a semi-technical designation for Gentiles who sympathized with Judaism. These people occupied a middle position: they worshipped the God of Israel, participated in synagogue services, and followed ethical commandments, but were not full proselytes. Inscriptions from Aphrodisias in Asia Minor (from around the 2nd century AD, though assumed to reflect earlier practice) explicitly mention θεοσεβεῖς as a distinct category alongside Jews and proselytes.

**"No one should be called unclean"** (μηδένα κοινὸν... λέγειν ἄνθρωπον, v. 28) marks Peter's interpretation of the vision: it was not primarily about food but about people. The shift from animals to humans as objects of purity thinking represents a radical rethinking of the category "unclean" in light of faith in the Messiah.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Caesarea have uncovered structures from the Herodian and Roman periods that bring the narrative to life. The ruins of the harbor, aqueduct, and administrative buildings demonstrate the city's significance as a center of Roman power. Inscriptions confirm the presence of military units in the area. A particularly relevant parallel is the finding from the Aphrodisias synagogue documenting "God-fearers" (θεοσεβεῖς) as a distinct group on the fringe of the Jewish diaspora—exactly the category to which Cornelius belonged.
