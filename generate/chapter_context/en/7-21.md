# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 21

## Places

**Mizpah** (הַמִּצְפָּה) serves as the gathering place for the tribes in this chapter. The site is likely located in the territory of Benjamin and was an important cultic and political center in pre-monarchic times. Archaeological excavations at Tell en-Nasbeh, often identified with Mizpah, have uncovered remains from the Iron Age that confirm the site's significance as a regional hub.

**Jabesh-gilead** (יָבֵשׁ גִּלְעָד) lay east of the Jordan River, in the Gilead region. The city clearly had close ties to the tribe of Benjamin, which is also reflected later in 1 Samuel 11 and 31. Its location is debated, but Tell el-Maqlub or Tell Abu Kharaz have been proposed as candidates.

**Shiloh** (שִׁלוֹ) is described in verse 19 with precise geographical details: north of Bethel, east of the main road from Bethel to Shechem, and south of Lebonah. Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Seilun have uncovered a large cult center from the Bronze and Iron Ages, with signs of destruction around 1050 BCE.

## Events and Customs

**The Oath in Mizpah** (vv. 1, 7, 18) not to give daughters to Benjamin illustrates how binding collective oaths were in Israelite culture. Such oaths were sworn in God's name and could not be broken without serious consequences. The Hebrew word שְׁבוּעָה (shevu'ah) denotes an oath that binds the speaker under a curse if violated.

**The Annual Festival at Shiloh** (חַג־יְהוָה, v. 19), with dancing in the vineyards, reflects a harvest festival, likely the Feast of Tabernacles or a similar annual celebration. Ritual dances performed by young women were known in several ancient Near Eastern cultures. The Hebrew term מְחֹלוֹת (meholot) describes circle dancing or processional dance, often associated with religious celebration.

**The Seizure of Wives** (vv. 20–23) was not an accepted practice in Israel, but the chapter presents it as a legal solution: since the women were "taken" (גָּזָלוּ, gazalu – "seized") and not "given," the fathers technically did not break their oath. Similar stories of bride seizure appear in Roman tradition (the Sabine women) and other cultures, suggesting a broader cultural pattern.

## Key Terms

**פֶּרֶץ** (perets, v. 15) – "breach" or "gap" – is used concerning the missing tribe. The word describes a rift in Israel's unity, as if the tribal confederation has received a wound that must be healed to preserve national integrity.

**יְרֻשַּׁת פְּלֵיטָה** (yerushat peletah, v. 17) – "the inheritance of the survivors" – expresses concern that Benjamin's tribal territory and inheritance rights should endure. The concept of נַחֲלָה (inheritance) was central to Israelite thought; the extinction of a tribe would create a permanent void in the promised land.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The detailed geographical description of Shiloh's location (v. 19) is remarkably precise and may suggest that the text was written when the site was less well known – possibly after its destruction around 1050 BCE. Archaeological findings at Khirbet Seilun confirm a significant cult site with large storage buildings capable of accommodating pilgrims during festivals. Pottery finds and remains of a possible temple complex support the picture of Shiloh as an important religious center before the temple in Jerusalem was built.
