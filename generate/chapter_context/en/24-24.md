# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 24

## People

The chapter mentions several key historical figures. **Jeconiah** (יְכָנְיָהוּ, also called Jehoiachin), the son of Jehoiakim, was king of Judah for only three months in 597 B.C.E. before he was taken into exile to Babylon. **Nebuchadnezzar** (נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר) was the king of Babylon who carried out this deportation. **Zedekiah** (צִדְקִיָּהוּ), mentioned in verse 8, was the king installed by Nebuchadnezzar over what remained of Judah after 597 B.C.E.

## Places

**The temple of the Lord** (הֵיכַל יְהוָה) in Jerusalem is the setting for Jeremiah's vision, where the two baskets of figs were placed before the temple. **The land of the Chaldeans** (אֶרֶץ כַּשְׂדִּים, Babylon) is where the exiles were sent—and paradoxically, the place where God promises to do good for them. **Egypt** (מִצְרָיִם) is mentioned in verse 8 as a refuge for some who fled there—a group that shares in the judgment over the "bad figs."

## Events and Customs

The chapter is dated "after Nebuchadnezzar had taken Jeconiah into exile"—the deportation of 597 B.C.E. On this occasion, the king, his court, **the craftsmen** (הֶחָרָשׁ), and **the smiths/metalworkers** (הַמַּסְגֵּר) were taken to Babylon. The Babylonians deliberately deported skilled workers to weaken the military capacity of the provinces.

## Key Terms

The word **דּוּדָאֵי** (duda'e, "baskets") describes the containers in which the fruit was presented—likely woven from reeds or palm leaves, typically used to carry fruit to the temple. **תְּאֵנֵי הַבַּכֻּרוֹת** (te'ene habbikkurot, "early figs") refers to the first ripening of figs in June, which was especially valued for its sweetness and quality. These first-ripe figs also had cultic significance as offerings.

The expression **לֵב לָדַעַת** (lev lada'at, "a heart to know") in verse 7 expresses the Hebrew understanding of the heart as the seat of will and insight—to "know" God involves both intellectual recognition and relational faithfulness.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verse 9 contains a textual variant: the ketiv (the written text) has **לזועה** while the qere (the read text) has **לְזַעֲוָה**—both mean "to horror/dread." The vision with the two baskets forms a clear structural pattern: good figs = the exiles = blessing (vv. 5-7); bad figs = those remaining and those in Egypt = judgment (vv. 8-10).
