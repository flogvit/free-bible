# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 72

## People

The psalm is attributed to Solomon (לִשְׁלֹמֹה), either as its author or as the king for whom it was written. The heading can mean both "of Solomon" or "for Solomon." Verse 20 serves as a colophon marking the end of Book II of the Psalter with the words, "The prayers of David son of Jesse are ended." This may suggest that David wrote this as a prayer for his son Solomon at the time of the royal succession, or it may simply mark the conclusion of a Davidic collection.

## Places

The psalm mentions several geographical areas that highlight the scope of the ideal king's rule. **Tarshish** (v. 10) was likely a distant port city in the west, possibly in Spain or Sardinia, known for trade in metals. **Sheba** (שְׁבָא) refers to southern Arabia (modern-day Yemen), famous for its trade in gold, frankincense, and spices. The visit of the queen of Sheba to Solomon (1 Kings 10) illustrates exactly such connections. **Seba** (סְבָא) was probably an African kingdom, possibly in Nubia or Ethiopia. The expression "from sea to sea and from the River to the ends of the earth" (v. 8) describes universal dominion—from the Mediterranean Sea to the Persian Gulf, from the Euphrates to the farthest reaches of the world.

## Events and Customs

The psalm reflects ancient Near Eastern coronation rituals and royal ideology. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, the king was responsible for upholding **justice** (מִשְׁפָּט) and **righteousness** (צְדָקָה), especially for the weak in society. The expression "lick the dust" (v. 9) was a well-known gesture of submission in antiquity, attested in Egyptian and Assyrian inscriptions. Bringing **tribute** (מִנְחָה) and **gifts** (אֶשְׁכָּר) was standard practice for vassal states and trading partners.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **יִנּוֹן** (yinnon, keri reading; ketiv: ינין) in verse 17 is rare and significant. It means "to sprout" or "to multiply" and was later understood in Jewish tradition as a name for the Messiah. The term **גֵּז** (v. 6) refers to freshly cut grass or mown meadow, an image of how the king's rule brings refreshing blessing like rain over dry land. **Shalom** (שָׁלוֹם) in verses 3 and 7 encompasses more than the absence of war—it implies comprehensive well-being, harmony, and perfection in society.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions from the same period contain similar idealized descriptions of kings as protectors of the poor and guarantors of cosmic order. Particularly interesting is the parallel to Hammurabi's law code, where the king is portrayed as one who ensures that "the strong do not oppress the weak." Archaeological finds from Sheba, including inscriptions mentioning trade with northern kingdoms, confirm the historical reality behind the verses about distant kings bringing gifts.
