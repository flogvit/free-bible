# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 23

## People

**Balaam, son of Beor** is mentioned in verses 5–6 as the prophet hired by the king of Moab to curse Israel. Balaam came from **Pethor in Aram-Naharaim** (Mesopotamia), an area known for its diviners and interpreters of omens. The account of Balaam is found in Numbers 22–24, and his name is also confirmed by an extra-biblical inscription from Tell Deir 'Alla in Jordan (circa 840–800 BCE), which refers to "Balaam, son of Beor, seer of the gods". This shows that Balaam was a historically known figure in the region.

## Places

**Ammon and Moab** were two peoples and kingdoms east of the Jordan River and the Dead Sea. According to Genesis 19, both descended from Lot's daughters. Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, while Ammon was located further north, with its capital in what is now Amman. Archaeological findings indicate that both kingdoms had developed state structures by the end of the second millennium BCE. **Edom** lay south of the Dead Sea and was regarded as a "brother" because the Edomites descended from Esau, Jacob's brother. **Egypt** is mentioned with the reminder that the Israelites were "foreigners" there—an experience that should shape their attitude toward outsiders.

## Events and Customs

**Qehal YHWH** ("the assembly of the Lord") refers to the cultic and social community in Israel. Exclusion from this assembly meant restrictions on participation in worship and marriage rights, not necessarily total exclusion from society. Similar boundaries between "inside" and "outside" are found in other ancient cultures.

**Cultic purity in the military camp** (vv. 10–15) had parallels in other ancient cultures where warfare was viewed as a sacred activity. Hittite and Assyrian texts contain similar rules regarding purity during campaigns. In Israel, the rationale was that God himself "walks in the midst of the camp"—God's holy presence required physical and ritual purity.

**Cultic prostitution** (vv. 18–19)—the terms *qedeshah* (קְדֵשָׁה, female) and *qadesh* (קָדֵשׁ, male) are debated among scholars. Traditionally understood as cultic prostitution linked to Canaanite fertility worship, but recent research questions whether this was as widespread as once assumed. The prohibition against bringing "the wages of a prostitute" (אֶתְנַן זוֹנָה) and "the price of a dog" (מְחִיר כֶּלֶב) to the temple—the latter possibly referring to a male prostitute—clearly marked distance from such practices.

## Key Terms

**Mamzer** (מַמְזֵר, v. 3) is a debated term. Traditionally translated as "illegitimate child" or "child of forbidden union", but its precise meaning remains uncertain. Some scholars suggest it may refer to children from mixed marriages with certain ethnic groups.

**Neshekh** (נֶשֶׁךְ, vv. 20–21) literally means "bite" and is used for interest. The word's etymology suggests how interest "bites" into the borrower's economy. The prohibition against taking interest from "brothers" (fellow Israelites) was intended to protect the poor from debt slavery, while foreigners engaged in trade did not have the same protection.

**Ervat davar** (עֶרְוַת דָּבָר, v. 15) is often translated as "something indecent" or "shameful thing". The expression links physical nakedness to moral impurity and explains why God would "turn away" from an unclean camp.
