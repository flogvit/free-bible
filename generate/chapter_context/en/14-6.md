# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 6

## People

**Solomon** appears in this chapter in his role as king-priest, a function that was unusual in Israel. At the temple dedication, he assumes a leading liturgical role by blessing the people and leading the great dedicatory prayer. This reflects the close connection between kingship and cult in ancient Israel, even though priestly functions normally belonged to the priests (the sons of Aaron), with the Levites as their assistants. The special position of the Davidic dynasty is emphasized through repeated references to the promises given to David (verses 4–17).

## Places

**The Temple in Jerusalem** stands central as the newly completed sanctuary. The chapter describes the inner court (עֲזָרָה, *azarah*) where the bronze platform stood (verse 13). Archaeological investigations have not been able to locate Solomon’s temple directly due to later structures on the Temple Mount, but the descriptions correspond with other known temple structures from the Iron Age in the Levant.

**The Bronze Platform** (כִּיּוֹר נְחֹשֶׁת, *kijor nechoshet*) on which Solomon stood during prayer is described as five cubits long and wide, and three cubits high (about 2.5 x 2.5 x 1.5 meters). This detail appears only in Chronicles, not in the parallel text of 1 Kings 8, and shows the Chronicler’s interest in cultic details.

## Events and Customs

**The Dedicatory Prayer** represents one of the longest prayers in the Old Testament. The prayer’s structure follows a pattern with seven specific prayer themes: oath/curse at the altar (verses 22–23), military defeat (verses 24–25), drought (verses 26–27), famine, pestilence, and blight (verses 28–31), the foreigner’s prayer (verses 32–33), war (verses 34–35), and captivity/exile (verses 36–39). This sevenfold pattern reflects completeness in Hebrew thought.

**The Prayer Posture** described—kneeling with outstretched hands toward heaven (verse 13)—was common in the ancient Near East. Egyptian and Mesopotamian depictions show similar postures in worship. The fact that the king kneels publicly emphasizes that even the most powerful in the kingdom bows before God.

## Key Terms

**"To dwell in thick darkness"** (לִשְׁכּוֹן בָּעֲרָפֶל, *lishkon ba'arafel*) in verse 1 refers to the theophany tradition where God reveals himself in cloud and darkness (cf. Exodus 20:21; Deuteronomy 4:11). The term עֲרָפֶל (*arafel*) denotes the dense darkness that surrounds God’s presence and protects people from direct contact with the holy.

**"To set your name there"** (לָשׂוּם שִׁמְךָ שָׁם, *lasum shimkha sham*, verse 20) expresses a theology that balances God’s transcendence with his presence. God’s "name" represents his accessible presence without limiting him to the building. This concept is also found in Assyrian royal inscriptions where kings "set their name" on conquered territories to mark authority.

**"The heavens and the heaven of heavens"** (שָׁמַיִם וּשְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם, *shamajim ushemei hashamajim*) in verse 18 reflects the ancient Near Eastern idea of multiple heavenly spheres. The expression emphasizes God’s absolute transcendence—even the highest heaven cannot contain him.
