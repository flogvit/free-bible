# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 6

## Events and Customs

### Alms (ἐλεημοσύνη)
Giving alms was one of the three pillars of Jewish piety, along with prayer and fasting. In first-century Jewish society, it was common to give alms publicly, often in connection with synagogue services or at the city gate. Rabbinic literature testifies that givers were sometimes announced aloud, and there were "alms boxes" (קופה) in synagogues. Jesus’ criticism of blowing a trumpet (σαλπίσῃς) is likely a hyperbolic description of the attention being sought, although some scholars have suggested it may refer to the sound of coins falling into metal containers.

### Prayer Practices
Jewish devotional practice included set times for prayer (morning, afternoon, and evening), and many devout Jews would stand up to pray wherever they were when the time came. Standing on street corners and in synagogues was therefore not unusual. The Greek word ταμεῖόν (v. 6) refers to an inner room or storage chamber—often the only room in a simple house that had a door and could be closed.

### Pagan Prayer Practices
The criticism of "vain repetition" (βατταλογέω) points to pagan beliefs that gods had to be persuaded through long incantations and repetitions. This stands in contrast to the short, trusting prayer Jesus teaches.

### Fasting
Pharisees voluntarily fasted twice a week (Monday and Thursday), beyond the required fasting days. The practice of "disfiguring one’s face" (ἀφανίζουσιν τὰ πρόσωπα) could include sprinkling ashes on the head, refraining from washing, and going about with unkempt hair. Anointing the head and washing the face (v. 17), however, were signs of normal daily hygiene and festive occasions.

## Key Terms

### Ὑποκριταί (hypocrites)
The word originally meant "actor"—one who wears a mask on the theater stage. In the Jewish context of Jesus’ time, it had acquired the meaning of someone playing a role, displaying outward piety without inner sincerity. Jesus consistently uses it in this chapter to refer to those who practice their religion for human approval.

### Μαμωνᾶς (Mammon)
An Aramaic loanword (ממון) meaning wealth or property. The word was personified in Jewish tradition as a power competing for human loyalty. By setting Mammon against God as two "lords" (κύριοι), Jesus suggests that wealth can become an idolatrous power demanding worship.

### Ἐπιούσιος (daily)
This adjective in the Lord’s Prayer (v. 11) is found almost nowhere else in Greek literature and has been the subject of much discussion. It may mean "for the coming day," "necessary for existence," or "supernatural." Church fathers interpreted it both literally (food for the day) and sacramentally (Eucharistic bread).

### Ὀφειλήματα (debts/sins)
In v. 12, the metaphor of debt is used for sin, which was common in Aramaic. Luke’s parallel uses "sins" (ἁμαρτίας), while Matthew retains the Semitic metaphor where sin is understood as a debt owed to God.

### Ὀφθαλμὸς ἁπλοῦς / πονηρός (good/evil eye)
The "evil eye" (עין רעה) was a Hebrew idiom for stinginess, while the "good eye" meant generosity. This explains the connection with the passage on wealth—it is not primarily about sight, but about the heart’s attitude toward money and other people.
