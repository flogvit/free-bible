# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 37

## People

**Sennacherib** (Hebrew: סַנְחֵרִיב) was king of Assyria from 705–681 B.C. He is one of the best-documented kings of antiquity through his own annals and reliefs. His campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. is described in detail in Assyrian sources, where he boasts of having besieged 46 fortified cities in Judah and imprisoned Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem.

**Tirhakah** (תִּרְהָקָה, v. 9), king of Cush, belonged to the 25th Egyptian dynasty. He formally reigned from about 690 B.C., but was likely a military leader as early as 701 B.C. His involvement shows that the conflict had international dimensions, with Egypt serving as a counterweight to Assyrian expansion.

**Rabshakeh** (רַב־שָׁקֵה) is not a personal name but an Assyrian title meaning "chief cupbearer" or "commander." This was a high-ranking military or diplomatic official in the Assyrian court.

## Places

**Lachish** and **Libnah** (v. 8) were strategic cities in Judah. Lachish was Judah's second most important city and controlled the road to Egypt. Assyrian reliefs from Nineveh show in dramatic detail the siege and capture of Lachish. Libnah lay northwest of Lachish and was a fortified city near the Philistine region.

**Gozan, Haran, Reseph, and Eden in Telassar** (v. 12) were regions in upper Mesopotamia that Assyria had conquered. Gozan (Tell Halaf) and Haran (חָרָן) are well-known archaeological sites confirming Assyrian control over these areas. The people of Eden (בְנֵי־עֶדֶן) lived in Telassar.

## Events and Customs

**Tearing clothes and putting on sackcloth** (v. 1) was a standard expression of deep grief, humility, and repentance in ancient Israel. Sackcloth (שַׂק) was a coarse, dark fabric made from goat hair. The fact that the king himself did this and entered the temple shows that he sought God's help in a desperate situation.

**Spreading out the letter before the Lord** (v. 14) was a symbolic act in which Hezekiah laid the Assyrian threat directly before God, as if God Himself were to read the enemy's words. This expresses a concrete, physical understanding of prayer and God's presence in the temple.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the Holy One of Israel"** (קְדוֹשׁ יִשְׂרָאֵל, v. 23) is used here of God whom Sennacherib has mocked. Isaiah's prophecy confronts the Assyrian king's arrogance against the living God.

**"Hook in the nose and bit in the lips"** (v. 29) refers to the Assyrian practice of leading captives by means of hooks or rings through the nose or lips. Assyrian reliefs depict this treatment of prisoners. Here the image is used ironically: the conqueror himself will be treated like a captured animal.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Sennacherib Prism** (Taylor Prism), found in Nineveh and now in the British Museum, describes the same campaign from an Assyrian perspective. Remarkably, Sennacherib never mentions capturing Jerusalem—he only boasts of the tribute Hezekiah paid. This agrees with the biblical account that the city was spared.

**The murder of Sennacherib** (v. 38) is confirmed by Assyrian sources. Babylonian chronicles report that he was killed by his son, and Esarhaddon (אֵסַר־חַדֹּן) took the throne after a brief civil war. The god **Nisroch** has not been identified with certainty but may be a Hebrew rendering of Marduk or Nusku.
