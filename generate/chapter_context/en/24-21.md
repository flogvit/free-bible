## People

The chapter introduces several individuals who are central to the narrative. **King Zedekiah** (Heb. צִדְקִיָּהוּ, Tsidqiyyahu, "my righteousness is YHWH") sends envoys to Jeremiah to seek a favorable oracle from God during the Babylonian siege. His hope for divine intervention stands in sharp contrast to the judgment that Jeremiah proclaims.

**Pashhur son of Malkijah** (Heb. פַּשְׁחוּר בֶּן־מַלְכִּיָּה) is a different person from Pashhur son of Immer in chapter 20. **Sephaniah son of Maaseiah** (Heb. צְפַנְיָה בֶן־מַעֲשֵׂיָה) was a priest who served as an intermediary between the king and Jeremiah. Both belonged to influential families in Jerusalem, and their mission shows that the king took the Babylonian threat seriously.

## Events and Customs

The expression "inquire of the Lord for us" (Heb. דְּרָשׁ־נָא בַעֲדֵנוּ, v. 2) reflects a formal request for an oracle. Zedekiah’s hope that God would perform "wonderful deeds" (Heb. נִפְלְאֹתָיו) likely alludes to the tradition of Jerusalem’s miraculous deliverance from the Assyrian king Sennacherib in 701 BC (2 Kgs 19:35–36). The king expected God to intervene again, but Jeremiah proclaims the opposite.

The choice between "the way of life" and "the way of death" (v. 8, Heb. דֶּרֶךְ הַחַיִּים / דֶּרֶךְ הַמָּוֶת) echoes covenant formulations such as those in Deut 30:15–19, but here it is given radically new content: surrender to the enemy is the way of life. The call to desert was regarded as treason. The expression "his life shall be his spoil" (v. 9, Heb. וְהָיְתָה־לּוֹ נַפְשׁוֹ לְשָׁלָל) was a military idiom meaning to escape with one’s life intact.

## Key Terms

The expression "outstretched hand and strong arm" (v. 5, Heb. בְּיָד נְטוּיָה וּבִזְרוֹעַ חֲזָקָה) is a deliberate reversal of Exodus language. These words are normally used to describe God’s deliverance of Israel from Egypt (Deut 4:34; 5:15), but here they are turned against God’s own people. This shocking image emphasizes that God now fights against Jerusalem, not for it.

The title "house of David" (v. 12, Heb. בֵּית דָּוִד) recalls the covenant obligations associated with the Davidic monarchy. The command to "judge rightly every morning" (Heb. דִּינוּ לַבֹּקֶר מִשְׁפָּט) refers to the king’s legal duty to hold court at daybreak.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Lachish letters (ostraca found in Lachish, dated to about 588–586 BC) provide invaluable insight into the atmosphere during the Babylonian siege. Letter IV describes how the signal fires from the cities were extinguished one by one, testifying to the same desperation that characterizes Zedekiah’s appeal to Jeremiah. Babylonian chronicles confirm Nebuchadnezzar’s campaign against Judah in this period.
