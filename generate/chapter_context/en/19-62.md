# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 62

## People

The heading mentions **Jeduthun** (יְדוּתוּן), one of the three main leaders of temple music during the time of David and Solomon, along with Asaph and Heman (1 Chron 16:41–42; 25:1–6). Jeduthun was a Levite appointed to lead praise singing with harps and lyres. The phrase "to the choirmaster: according to Jeduthun" may indicate that the psalm was to be performed in the musical tradition established by Jeduthun, or by his descendants who continued this service. His name is connected to the root ידה (yadah, "to praise/thank") and also appears in the headings of Psalms 39 and 77.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **דּוּמִיָּה** (dumiyyah) in verse 2 and **דּוֹמִּי** (dommi) in verse 6 are central to the psalm. Both derive from the root דמם, which expresses silence and calm. This implies more than just the absence of sound—it describes a state of trusting waiting and dependence, where the soul rests in God without anxiety or protest. This stands in contrast to the common response of people in ancient times toward enemies, which was lamentation and cries for vengeance.

The word **אַךְ** (akh) is repeated six times in the psalm (verses 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 10) and serves as a powerful stylistic device. It means "only," "truly," or "surely" and creates an exclusive focus—either on God as the only refuge or on the emptiness of human beings.

The expressions **בְּנֵי־אָדָם** and **בְּנֵי אִישׁ** in verse 10 represent a wordplay distinguishing between ordinary people and those of high rank. Both groups are weighed on the scales and found lighter than a breath (הֶבֶל, hevel)—the same word used throughout Ecclesiastes to describe the transience of life.

## Events and Customs

The image of **scales** (מֹאזְנַיִם) in verse 10 alludes to a well-known commercial practice in ancient times. Scales were used to measure precious metals and goods, and honesty in weighing was an important ethical theme in ancient Israel (Lev 19:36; Prov 11:1). The psalmist uses this image to show that people, regardless of social status, lack substance when weighed against God's standard.
