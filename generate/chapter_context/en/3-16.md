# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 16

## People

The chapter opens with a direct reference to the death of Aaron’s two sons, Nadab and Abihu, as described in Leviticus 10. This tragedy forms the backdrop for the strict regulations that follow. Aaron, Israel’s first high priest, is the central figure in the ritual. His unique role as the only one permitted to enter the Most Holy Place underscores the hierarchical structure of the priesthood in ancient Israel. The chapter also mentions "a man who is appointed" (אִישׁ עִתִּי, *ish itti*), a person prepared to lead the scapegoat out into the wilderness.

## Places

The Most Holy Place (הַקֹּדֶשׁ, *ha-qodesh*), the room behind the veil (פָּרֹכֶת, *parokhet*) in the tabernacle, is the central location for the rituals described in this chapter. Here stood the ark of the covenant with its atonement cover (כַּפֹּרֶת, *kapporet*). The wilderness (מִדְבָּר, *midbar*) is mentioned as the destination for the scapegoat, described as "a desolate land" (אֶרֶץ גְּזֵרָה, *erets gezerah*). Later Jewish tradition identified a specific place called "the rock of Azazel" in the wilderness of Judea, where the goat was led.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the Yom Kippur ritual (יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים), the Day of Atonement. This was the only day of the year when the high priest could enter the Most Holy Place. The ritual includes casting lots between two goats—one for the Lord and one for Azazel. The practice of placing sins upon an animal and sending it away (the scapegoat ritual) has parallels in Hittite rituals where animals were used as carriers of impurity and evil, sent out from the community. The command to "afflict/humble yourselves" (תְּעַנּוּ אֶת־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם) was understood as fasting and self-denial. The day is called "a sabbath of solemn rest" (שַׁבַּת שַׁבָּתוֹן), the most holy day of rest.

## Key Terms

**Azazel** (עֲזָאזֵל): This enigmatic term has been the subject of extensive discussion. Possible interpretations include: a name for a wilderness demon, a place name, or a description meaning "the goat that goes away." Later Jewish literature (such as 1 Enoch) developed Azazel into a fallen angel. The word itself may derive from עֵז (*ez*, goat) and אָזַל (*azal*, to go away/disappear).

**Kapporet** (כַּפֹּרֶת): Often translated as "mercy seat" or "atonement cover," this was the gold plate with cherub figures that covered the ark of the covenant. It was here that God’s presence manifested and where blood was sprinkled. The word comes from the root כפר (*kpr*), meaning "to cover over" or "to make atonement."

**Kipper** (כִּפֶּר): The verb for "atonement" or "make atonement" appears 15 times in this chapter, emphasizing its central theme. The concept involves both cleansing from sin and restoring the relationship between humanity and God.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, fragments were found that mention the figure of Azazel (particularly in 1 Enoch and 4Q180), showing how this term was understood and developed in early Jewish tradition. The Mishnah tractate Yoma (edited around 200 CE) provides detailed descriptions of how the Yom Kippur ritual was performed in the Second Temple period, including that a scarlet thread (לשון של זהורית) was tied to the scapegoat’s horn and that the goat was led to a cliff about 12 kilometers from Jerusalem and pushed off.
