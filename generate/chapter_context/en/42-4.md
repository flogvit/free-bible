# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 4

## Places

**The Wilderness Area near the Jordan** – After his baptism, Jesus was led into "the wilderness" (ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ), which likely refers to the Judean Desert, the barren mountainous region between the Jewish highlands and the Jordan Valley/Dead Sea. This rugged landscape held strong symbolic meaning in Jewish tradition as the place of Israel’s 40 years of wandering and was associated with both testing and divine closeness.

**Nazareth** (Ναζαρά) was a small, relatively insignificant village in Galilee, not mentioned in the Old Testament. Archaeological excavations have revealed a poor farming village with around 200–400 inhabitants during Jesus’ time, featuring terraces, wine presses, and simple dwellings carved into the hillside. The town was located in a mountainous area, and verse 29 describes how the people took Jesus to "the edge of the cliff" (ὀφρύος τοῦ ὄρους) upon which the city was built – consistent with Nazareth’s location on a mountainside with steep drops nearby.

**Capernaum** (Καφαρναούμ), meaning "Village of Nahum", was a larger town on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Unlike remote Nazareth, Capernaum was strategically situated along the trade route Via Maris and served as a customs station. Archaeologists have uncovered remains of a synagogue that can be dated to the first century, as well as what is traditionally identified as Peter’s house.

## Events and Customs

**The Synagogue Service** is described in detail in verses 16–21 and provides valuable insight into first-century practices. Jesus "stood up to read" (ἀνέστη ἀναγνῶναι), which was customary – people stood when reading from Scripture. He was then given a scroll (βιβλίον) and he "unrolled it" (ἀναπτύξας) to find the passage. After reading, he "rolled it up" (πτύξας), "handed it back to the attendant" (ἀποδοὺς τῷ ὑπηρέτῃ) and "sat down" (ἐκάθισεν) to teach – the seated position was the traditional posture of a teacher. The attendant (ὑπηρέτης) was the synagogue’s servant, called *hazzan* in Hebrew, who was responsible for caring for the scrolls and assisting during the service.

**The 40 Days of Temptation** (ἡμέρας τεσσεράκοντα) directly connect to key Old Testament themes: Israel’s 40 years in the wilderness, Moses’ 40 days on Mount Sinai (Exodus 34:28), and Elijah’s 40-day journey to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). Jesus’ fasting and resistance to temptation are presented as a contrast to Israel’s failure under testing in the wilderness.

**Healing after Sunset** (verse 40: δύνοντος τοῦ ἡλίου) reflects Sabbath law. People waited until sunset to bring their sick to Jesus because the Sabbath had then ended, allowing them to carry their sick without breaking the prohibition against work on the Sabbath.

## Persons

**The Widow of Zarephath** and **Naaman the Syrian** (verses 25–27) are Old Testament figures whom Jesus uses to provoke his listeners. Zarephath was a Phoenician town between Tyre and Sidon – pagan territory – where the prophet Elijah was sent to a non-Jewish widow during a famine (1 Kings 17). Naaman was the commander of the Aramean king, an enemy of Israel, who was cleansed of leprosy by Elisha while Israelite lepers remained sick (2 Kings 5). By highlighting God’s blessing upon Gentiles rather than Israelites, Jesus challenged Jewish exclusivism and foreshadowed the spread of the gospel to the nations.

## Key Terms

**"The Year of the Lord’s Favor"** (ἐνιαυτὸν κυρίου δεκτόν) in verse 19 is quoted from Isaiah 61:1–2 and alludes to the Jubilee year (Leviticus 25), which was to occur every 50th year. In the Jubilee year, slaves were to be freed, debts forgiven, and land returned to its original owners. Jesus applies this as a picture of the messianic liberation he brings, but significantly omits Isaiah’s next phrase about "the day of vengeance" – an omission that underscores the focus on grace in his ministry.

**"The Holy One of God"** (ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ) in verse 34 is an unusual title used by the unclean spirit to refer to Jesus. In the Old Testament, this expression is applied only to Aaron (Psalm 106:16) and possibly to the prophet Elisha (2 Kings 4:9). The fact that demons recognize Jesus’ identity while people remain ignorant or doubtful is a remarkable feature of this chapter.

**The Pinnacle of the Temple** (τὸ πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ) in verse 9 likely refers to the southeastern corner of the temple wall, where there was a steep drop down into the Kidron Valley. Josephus describes this height as dizzying. In Luke’s version of the temptations, the temple scene is placed last (unlike Matthew’s order), giving Jerusalem and the temple a climactic position in the temptation narrative.
