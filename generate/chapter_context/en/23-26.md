# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 26

## Places

The chapter opens with a song to be sung "in the land of Judah" and describes a "strong city" (עִיר עָז, *ir oz*) in contrast to "the lofty city" (קִרְיָה נִשְׂגָּבָה, *qirya nisgava*) that will be destroyed (vv. 1, 5). The strong city represents Jerusalem or the restored Zion, while the lofty city likely points to a hostile world power. Many scholars identify this with Babylon, Nineveh, or another oppressive city symbolizing human arrogance. The contrast between the exalted city brought low "into the dust" and God’s city that stands firm reflects a recurring theme in Isaiah’s apocalyptic section (chapters 24–27).

## Key Terms

The expression שָׁלוֹם שָׁלוֹם (*shalom shalom*, "peace, peace") in verse 3 is an intensified form expressing perfect and enduring peace. This doubling is characteristic of Hebrew poetry and emphasizes the completeness of the peace God gives to those who trust in Him. The name of God יָהּ יְהוָה (*Yah YHVH*) in verse 4 combines the short form and full form of God’s name, which is unusual and underscores God’s eternal and unchanging nature as "an everlasting rock" (צוּר עוֹלָמִים, *tsur olamim*).

The term רְפָאִים (*refaim*) in verses 14 and 19 refers to the dead in the underworld (Sheol). In Canaanite culture, the refaim were associated with deified ancestors, but in ancient Israel it simply denoted the deceased who live a shadowy existence. Verse 19 contains a remarkable resurrection text where "טַל אוֹרֹת" (*tal orot*, "the dew of light") will awaken the dead—an image that links God’s life-giving power to nature’s renewal.

## Events and Customs

The image of the woman in labor in verses 17–18 was a well-known metaphor in the ancient Near East for crisis and distress. The people describe their own helplessness: they have had pains, but "have given birth only to wind"—they were unable to bring salvation into the world. This contrasts with God’s promise of resurrection in verse 19. The exhortation in verse 20 to go into rooms and shut the doors recalls the Passover account in Exodus 12, where the Israelites were to stay inside while the Lord’s judgment passed over Egypt. This allusion reinforces the idea of divine protection during the time of wrath.
