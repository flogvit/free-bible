## Key Concepts

The chapter contains several Hebrew words and expressions that require explanation. The word **מִשְׁפָּט** (mishpat) occurs repeatedly (vv. 4, 5, 6, 12, 17, 23) and denotes both "judgment" and "justice" – a central concept in Elihu’s argument concerning God’s character. He asserts that God cannot pervert mishpat because he is the ultimate Judge.

The expression **בְּלִיַּעַל** (beliyya'al) in verse 18, translated as "worthless" or "scoundrel", was in an ancient Near Eastern context a serious insult against a king. Elihu uses this rhetorically to show that although people dare not call an earthly king such a name, Job indirectly accuses God of something similar. The term **צַלְמָוֶת** (tsalmawet) in verse 22, often translated "shadow of death", describes the deepest darkness where no one can hide from God – a poetic image of God’s all-seeing nature.

## Events and Customs

Elihu’s appeal to "wise men" and "understanding ones" (vv. 2, 10, 34) reflects the Old Testament wisdom tradition in which elders and scholars gathered to discuss theological and ethical questions. This form of public debate with witnesses was known in the ancient Near East, and Elihu calls upon his listeners as a kind of jury to judge between his arguments and Job’s.

The idea that God can remove powerful rulers "in an instant" without human intervention (vv. 20, 24) reflects a widespread ancient Near Eastern experience with sudden regime changes. In contrast to surrounding cultures that often attributed such events to fate or competing gods, Elihu asserts that the God of Israel alone has sovereign control over all earthly powers.
