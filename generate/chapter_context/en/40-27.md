## People

**Pontius Pilate** (Greek: Πιλάτῳ τῷ ἡγεμόνι) was the Roman prefect of Judea. In this chapter, he interrogates Jesus, attempts to release him through the Passover custom, but yields to the crowd's demands. His washing of hands (v. 24) and declaration of innocence are unique to Matthew's account. His wife is mentioned only here in the New Testament (v. 19), warning him after a dream not to have anything to do with "this righteous man".

**Barabbas** (Greek: Βαραββᾶν) is an interesting figure because his name means "son of the father" in Aramaic (bar-abba). Some early manuscripts give him the first name "Jesus" (Ἰησοῦν Βαραββᾶν), creating a dramatic contrast: the crowd chooses between two "Jesuses" – one called "son of the father" and one called "Christ".

**Joseph of Arimathea** (v. 57–60) is described as a rich man and disciple. He asked Pilate for Jesus' body and laid it in his own new tomb hewn out of rock. The fact that he had a rock-cut tomb near Jerusalem indicates that he was wealthy.

**Simon of Cyrene** (v. 32) was forced to carry Jesus' cross. Cyrene was a Greek city in Libya with a significant Jewish diaspora. The verb ἠγγάρευσαν describes the Roman right to compel civilians to perform services for the military.

**The women at the cross** (v. 55–56) included Mary Magdalene, Mary (mother of James and Joseph), and the mother of Zebedee's sons. They had followed Jesus from Galilee and served him. Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" were also witnesses at the tomb (v. 61).

**The centurion** (ἑκατόνταρχος, v. 54) and the soldiers guarding Jesus confessed at his death: "Truly, this was God's Son" – the first Gentile confession in Matthew after the crucifixion.

## Places

**Golgotha** (Greek: Γολγοθᾶ, v. 33) is Aramaic and translated into Greek as Κρανίου Τόπος – "The Place of a Skull". The location was outside the city walls, and the traditional site is under the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem.

**The Praetorium** (τὸ πραιτώριον, v. 27) was the Roman governor's headquarters in Jerusalem, either Herod's palace or the Antonia Fortress. Here the soldiers gathered "the whole cohort" (ὅλην τὴν σπεῖραν) to mock Jesus.

**The Potter's Field/Blood Field** (Ἀγρὸς τοῦ Κεραμέως / Ἀγρὸς Αἵματος, v. 7–8) was purchased with the thirty silver pieces Judas returned. Matthew sees this as fulfillment of prophecy (v. 9–10). Tradition places the site in the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

**The hand-washing ritual** (v. 24), where Pilate washes his hands "before the crowd" (ἀπέναντι τοῦ ὄχλου), was known in both Jewish and Greco-Roman culture as a symbol of innocence. In Deuteronomy 5:6–7, elders wash their hands over a heifer to declare themselves innocent in an unsolved murder. Pilate thus uses a ritual his Jewish audience would understand.

**Flogging before crucifixion** (φραγελλώσας, v. 26) was standard Roman procedure. The Roman *flagellum* was a whip with leather thongs embedded with bone or metal pieces, which weakened the victim before crucifixion.

**Crucifixion** followed a fixed pattern: the victim was stripped, clothes were divided among the executioners (v. 35), and a tablet (*titulus*) with the charge was placed above the head (v. 37). Matthew's version "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" corresponds to the political charge of rebellion against Rome.

**Wine mixed with gall** (οἶνον μετὰ χολῆς, v. 34) was likely a numbing drink. Mark mentions "myrrh", while Matthew uses "gall" (χολή), possibly to create an allusion to Psalm 69:22.

**Passover pardon** (v. 15–17) was a custom where the governor released a prisoner chosen by the crowd. Pilate offered the choice between Barabbas and Jesus.

## Key Terms

**Korban** (κορβανᾶν, v. 6) is a Hebrew/Aramaic loanword denoting the temple treasury or gifts dedicated to God. The chief priests' scrupulosity – they refused to put "blood money" into the korban, yet had no qualms about paying it out – illustrates an ironic moral blindness.

**"Eli, Eli, lema sabaktani"** (Ἠλὶ ἠλὶ λεμὰ σαβαχθάνι, v. 46) is Aramaic (with Hebrew "Eli" for "my God"), a direct quote from Psalm 22:2. The misunderstanding that Jesus was calling on Elijah (Ἠλίαν) is phonetically plausible in Aramaic/Hebrew.

**The temple curtain** (τὸ καταπέτασμα τοῦ ναοῦ, v. 51) was torn from top to bottom when Jesus died. This likely refers to the inner curtain separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place. The tearing symbolizes open access to God's presence.

**Signs at Jesus' death** (v. 51–53): The curtain tore, the earth shook, rocks split, tombs opened, and holy people stood up. These cosmic signs underscore the significance of Jesus' death.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Excavations under the Church of the Holy Sepulchre have uncovered first-century rock-cut tombs, supporting the area's identification with Golgotha and Joseph of Arimathea's new tomb (v. 60).

Skeletal finds from Giv'at ha-Mivtar (1968) show that crucified individuals could be buried in Jewish graves, confirming that Joseph's action (v. 57–60) was possible under certain circumstances.
