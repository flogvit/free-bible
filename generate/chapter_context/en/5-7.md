# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 7

## Peoples

The chapter opens by mentioning seven nations that Israel would encounter in Canaan: the Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites (verse 1). These are described as "greater and mightier" than Israel, emphasizing that the conquest will be God's work, not Israel's military strength. The list serves as a representative summary of the land's population and their religious practices from which Israel must distance itself.

## Events and Customs

The term *ḥerem* (חֵרֶם, "ban" or "devoted to destruction") in verses 2, 25, and 26 describes a practice where war spoils were dedicated to the deity through total destruction. Similar practices are known from other ancient Near Eastern cultures, including the Mesha Stele from Moab. The prohibition against intermarriage (verses 3–4) and the command to destroy cult objects—altars, *masseboth* (stone pillars), *asherahs* (cult poles), and carved images (verses 5, 25)—reflect a radical rejection of Canaanite religious syncretism.

## Key Terms

The expression *am segullah* (עַם סְגֻלָּה, "treasured possession" or "special people") in verse 6 denotes a particularly valuable possession. The word *segullah* is used in Akkadian texts to refer to royal treasures and private property set apart from the state's wealth. This term expresses Israel's unique status as God's personal, precious possession among all the peoples of the earth.

The word *ḥesed* (חֶסֶד) in verses 9 and 12, often translated "mercy" or "steadfast love," describes covenant loyalty that goes beyond legal obligations. It encompasses both God's faithful love toward His people and the mutual loyalty expected within the covenant relationship.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Canaan have uncovered numerous cult objects of the type the chapter commands to be destroyed. Discoveries of *asherahs* (cult poles dedicated to the fertility goddess Asherah), stone pillars (*masseboth*), and female figurines at sites such as Hazor, Megiddo, and Lachish confirm how widespread Canaanite cultic practices were. At several Israelite settlements from the Iron Age, remains of destroyed cult objects have been found, which may indicate attempts to comply with these commands. At the same time, finds of Asherah figurines even within Israelite contexts show that syncretism remained a persistent challenge, just as the text's warnings suggest.
