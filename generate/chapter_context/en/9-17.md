## Places

The chapter provides an unusually precise geographical description of the battlefield. The Philistines gathered at Socoh (שֹׂכֹה) and camped between Socoh and Azekah at Ephes Dammim (v. 1), while the Israelites were encamped in the Elah Valley (עֵמֶק הָאֵלָה). The Elah Valley is identified with Wadi es-Sant, a broad valley leading from the coastal plain up toward the highlands of Judah. Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Qeiyafa, located on the southern slope of the valley, have uncovered a fortified city from the time of King David (c. 1000 BC) with two gates – something that has strengthened its identification with biblical Shaaraim ("two gates"), mentioned in v. 52 as a place along the Philistines' flight path. Socoh and Azekah are also archaeologically confirmed and lie strategically at the entrance to the valley from Philistine territory.

Goliath came from Gath (מִגַּת), one of the five main cities of the Philistines. Gath is usually identified with Tell es-Safi, where extensive excavations have revealed massive fortifications and Philistine culture from the Iron Age.

## People

Goliath (גָּלְיָת) is described as "איש הבנים" – "a man between the lines" or "champion" (v. 4), a technical term for one who challenges to a representative duel. His height is given as "six cubits and a span" (about 2.9 meters in the Masoretic Text; the Septuagint and a Qumran fragment have "four cubits and a span," about 2 meters). He wore full bronze armor: a helmet, scale armor weighing five thousand shekels (about 57 kg), greaves, and a javelin – equipment reflecting heavily armed warriors of the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age. The detailed description of his armor (vv. 5-7) has parallels with Mycenaean and Aegean warrior gear, which aligns with the Philistines' origin from the Aegean region.

David (דָּוִד) is introduced as the son of an Ephrathite named Jesse from Bethlehem (v. 12), the youngest of eight sons. His three oldest brothers – Eliab, Abinadab, and Shammah – served in the army (vv. 13-14). David's description as "נַעַר" (young man/youth) and "אַדְמֹנִי עִם־יְפֵה מַרְאֶה" (ruddy with a handsome appearance, v. 42) repeats the characterization from 16:12.

## Events and Customs

The chapter depicts the practice of representative single combat (μονομαχία), where one warrior from each side fought on behalf of the entire army, and the outcome decided the war without full-scale battle (vv. 8-9). This custom is attested in several ancient cultures, including Homeric Greece and ancient Mesopotamia, and served to limit loss of life. The Philistines' challenge that the loser should become the winner's "עֲבָדִים" (slaves/servants) was a formal war convention.

The supplies David brought – roasted grain (קָלִיא), bread, and cheese (חֲרִצֵי הֶחָלָב) for the "commander of a thousand" (vv. 17-18) – illustrate how Israelite families supported their soldiers in the field, since there was no centralized military logistics. "ערובה" (pledge/security, v. 18) likely refers to a sign that his brothers were alive and well.

## Key Terms

The expression "הֶעָרֵל" (the uncircumcised, vv. 26, 36) is repeatedly used by David concerning Goliath. This was more than an anatomical description – it marked the Philistines as standing outside the covenant community with Israel's God and was a strong insult in Israelite culture. The Philistines were unique among Israel's neighbors in not practicing circumcision.

David's sling (קֶלַע) was not a toy, but a serious military weapon. Professional slingers could achieve speeds of over 100 km/h with stone projectiles, and Assyrian reliefs show slingers as distinct military units. The five "glatte steinene" (smooth stones) (חֲלֻּקֵי אֲבָנִים) from the stream were ideal projectiles – water-worn stones flew more accurately than uneven ones.

"יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת" (the Lord, God of hosts, v. 45) is a title that emphasizes God's role as commander-in-chief of the heavenly army – in sharp contrast to the Philistines' trust in human military power.
