# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 10

## Key Concepts

Chapter 10 contains several Hebrew words and expressions that reflect ancient Near Eastern thinking about human nature and creation. The phrase **נָקְטָה נַפְשִׁי** (*nåqetåh nafshí*, v. 1) literally means "my soul is weary" or "my soul loathes life," expressing deep existential aversion to living. The word **נֶפֶשׁ** (*néfesh*) refers not merely to the "soul" in the modern sense, but to the entire life force and essence of a person.

In verse 9, Job uses the image **כַחֹמֶר** (*kachómer*) – "like clay" – to describe God's creation of humanity. This connects with the creation account in Genesis 2:7 and was a widespread metaphor throughout the ancient Near East, where gods were depicted as potters shaping humans. Akkadian myths such as the Atrahasis epic likewise describe human creation from clay.

## Events and Customs

Job's description of human formation in verses 10–11 – with images of milk being "poured out" and cheese "solidifying," followed by skin, flesh, bones, and sinews – reflects ancient embryology. In the ancient Near East and in early Greco-Roman medicine (as seen in Aristotle), it was believed that the fetus formed when male semen coagulated with female menstrual blood, much like milk turning into cheese. This was a common understanding and not a uniquely Jewish concept.

The chapter's concluding description of the realm of the dead (vv. 21–22) using the terms **חֹשֶׁךְ** (*chóshekh* – darkness), **צַלְמָוֶת** (*tsalmávet* – shadow of death), **עֵיפָתָה** (*efatah* – thick darkness), and **אֹפֶל** (*ófel* – utter darkness) paints a picture of Sheol as a place without order or light. This conception of the realm of the dead as a dark, chaotic place has parallels in Mesopotamian literature, especially in "Ishtar's Descent to the Underworld."
