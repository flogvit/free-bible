# Psalm 90 – Chapter Context

## People

The psalm is attributed to Moses (מֹשֶׁה), who in the superscription is called "the man of God" (אִישׁ־הָאֱלֹהִים). This title is also used for Moses in Deuteronomy 33:1 and Joshua 14:6, marking him as a unique mediator between God and the people. Whether Moses actually wrote the psalm or whether it was attributed to him due to its thematic connection with the wilderness wandering period is debated. The psalm’s reflections on human frailty and God’s eternity fit well with Moses’ experience of watching an entire generation die in the wilderness over forty years (Numbers 14:29–35).

## Key Terms

**מָעוֹן (maon)** – "dwelling" or "refuge" (v. 1). This image of God as a place of refuge is especially powerful in the context of the wilderness, where the people had no permanent home.

**דַּכָּא (dakka)** – "crushing" or "that which is crushed" (v. 3). The word points back to the creation account where humanity was formed from the dust of the ground (Genesis 2:7; 3:19) and expresses human frailty and return to the earth.

**אַשְׁמוּרָה (ashmura)** – "night watch" (v. 4). The Israelites divided the night into three watches of about four hours each, which underscores how short even a thousand years appear before God.

**הֶגֶה (hege)** – "sighing" or "murmuring" (v. 9). The word can also mean a faint sound or a breath, illustrating the transience of life.

## Events and Customs

The psalm’s mention of "days of affliction" and "years of trouble" (v. 15) likely reflects the trials of the wilderness wandering period. The repeated emphasis on God’s anger (vv. 7, 9, 11) corresponds with the judgment upon the wilderness generation that refused to enter the land. The prayer "turn back, O Lord" (v. 13) uses the same Hebrew verb (שׁוּב) that God uses when commanding humanity to "return" to dust (v. 3), creating a poetic tension between judgment and grace.

The lifespan of seventy to eighty years (v. 10) is interesting in light of the fact that the Pentateuch attributes much greater ages to the patriarchs. This likely reflects a realistic assessment of normal life expectancy in ancient Israel.
