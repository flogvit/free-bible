# Historical and Cultural Context for Daniel 9

## People

**Darius the son of Ahasuerus** (v. 1) is a debated figure. He is described as "of Median descent" and as the one who "became king over the kingdom of the Chaldeans." His identification remains uncertain—some scholars link him to the Persian general Gubaru, who ruled Babylon after its conquest in 539 BC; others identify him with Cyrus himself under an alternate name, or with a Median vassal king. This historical difficulty underscores the text's focus on theology rather than strict chronology.

**Gabriel** (v. 21) is the angel Daniel previously saw in the vision of chapter 8. His name means "God is my strength" or "Man of God." Gabriel is one of only two angels named in the Hebrew Bible (along with Michael in Daniel 10:13, 21; 12:1). His role as the messenger of divine revelation and interpreter of prophetic visions is central to this chapter.

## Places

**Jerusalem and the ruined temple** stand at the heart of Daniel's prayer (vv. 16–19). The city had lain in ruins since its destruction by the Babylonians in 586 BC. Daniel, living in exile, prays with his face turned toward "your holy mountain" (v. 20)—a practice reflecting Jewish tradition of praying toward Jerusalem (cf. 1 Kings 8:48; Dan 6:11). Jerusalem's status as "the city called by your name" (vv. 18–19) expresses the theological conviction that God's name and glory are bound to this place.

## Events and Customs

**Daniel's penitential practices** (v. 3)—fasting, wearing sackcloth, and sitting in ashes—represent traditional expressions of grief, humility, and repentance in ancient Israelite culture. Sackcloth (שַׂק, *saq*) was a coarse fabric made from goat hair, worn directly against the skin as a sign of deep mourning. Ashes on the head symbolized human frailty and humility before God. This combination appears during national crises (Neh 9:1; Esth 4:1–3; Jonah 3:5–6).

**The prayer of confession** (vv. 4–19) follows a pattern known from other exilic and post-exilic texts (Ezra 9; Neh 9; Baruch 1–3). The collective confession of sin—"we have sinned"—is characteristic. Daniel includes himself in the people's guilt, even though he personally appears righteous. The prayer repeatedly refers to "the law of Moses" and the curses written there (vv. 11, 13), pointing to the covenant blessings and curses found in Deuteronomy 28–30.

**The time of the evening sacrifice** (v. 21)—מִנְחַת־עָרֶב (*minchat-erev*)—was the daily afternoon offering time, traditionally between about 3:00 PM and sunset. Although the temple lay in ruins, Jews in exile continued to observe these times for prayer (cf. Ps 141:2). Gabriel's arrival at this moment emphasizes that God still hears His people's prayers even without temple sacrifices.

## Key Terms

**The seventy years** (v. 2) refer to Jeremiah's prophecy concerning the duration of the exile (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10). Daniel studies "the writings" (בַּסְּפָרִים, *bassefarim*)—an early witness that prophetic texts were collected and treated as authoritative scriptures worthy of study and interpretation.

**Seventy weeks** (v. 24)—שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעִים (*shavu'im shiv'im*)—literally means "seventy sevens." The Hebrew word שָׁבוּעַ (*shavua*) means a "period of seven," usually referring to weeks, but it can also denote a cycle of seven years (cf. Lev 25:8 regarding the sabbatical year). The prophecy is structured into periods of 7 + 62 + 1 such units.

**Mashiach nagid** (vv. 25–26)—מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד—means "an anointed prince" or "an anointed leader." The term "mashiach" (anointed) was used for kings, high priests, and even the Persian king Cyrus (Isa 45:1). At this point, the term had not yet acquired the specific messianic meaning it later developed.

**The abomination that causes desolation** (v. 27)—שִׁקּוּצִים מְשֹׁמֵם (*shiqqusim meshomem*)—is an expression combining "abominable things" (often used concerning idolatry) with desolation. Historically, this term has been linked to Antiochus IV Epiphanes' desecration of the temple in 167 BC, and it is also referenced in the New Testament (Matt 24:15; Mark 13:14).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Dead Sea Scrolls contain several fragments showing early Jewish interpretation of Daniel 9. The document 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) applies the seventy weeks to end-time calculations and connects them with the jubilee cycle. This demonstrates that an apocalyptic interpretation of the prophecy was widespread in late Second Temple Judaism.

Babylonian chronicles confirm the Persian Empire's conquest of Babylon in 539 BC, which forms the historical framework for the chapter's introduction. The Cyrus Cylinder, discovered in Babylon in 1879, documents Cyrus's policy allowing deported peoples to return to their homelands—a policy that made possible the Jewish return Daniel here prays for.
