# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 29

## Events and Customs

This chapter describes the offerings for three important harvest festivals in the seventh month (Tishri): the Feast of Trumpets (vv. 1–6), the Day of Atonement (vv. 7–11), and the Feast of Tabernacles with its concluding solemn assembly (vv. 12–38).

**Feast of Trumpets** (יוֹם תְּרוּעָה, "day of shouting") marked the beginning of the seventh month. The text prescribes that this should be a day of תְּרוּעָה – a loud blast or alarm signal. In later Jewish tradition, this day became Rosh Hashanah (New Year), but this chapter focuses exclusively on the sacrificial regulations.

**Day of Atonement** (Yom Kippur) was the only annual fast day explicitly commanded in the books of Moses. The expression "you shall humble your souls" (וְעִנִּיתֶם אֶת־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם) meant fasting and self-examination. The offerings here supplemented the special service of the high priest described in Leviticus 16.

**Feast of Tabernacles** (Sukkot) was the most extensive sacrificial festival in the Israelite calendar. The descending sequence of bulls (from thirteen down to seven) over seven days, totaling seventy bulls, was interpreted in Jewish tradition as representing all the nations of the world (based on the seventy nations listed in Genesis 10).

## Key Terms

**עֲצֶרֶת** (atseret, "solemn assembly") in verse 35 designates the eighth day as a concluding festival gathering. The word comes from the root עצר meaning "to hold back" or "gather", marking a day when the people were to remain assembled for a final worship service. This day later came to be called Shemini Atzeret and was celebrated as an independent festival.

**רֵיחַ נִיחֹחַ** ("a pleasing aroma") is an anthropomorphic expression describing God’s acceptance of the offering. It does not mean that God literally smells, but rather that He receives worship with favor.

**מִקְרָא־קֹדֶשׁ** ("holy convocation") indicates days when the people were called together for collective worship, as opposed to individual sacrifices.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The detailed lists of offerings in this chapter have parallels in other ancient Near Eastern cultures, where major public festivals required extensive state-sponsored sacrifices. Mesopotamian temple archives from Mari and Emar document similar practices with recorded quantities of animals and food gifts for specific festivals. The systematic and detailed enumeration of sacrificial animals, along with amounts of grain and oil, reflects an administrative practice known throughout the ancient Near East, where priests kept precise records of cultic obligations.
