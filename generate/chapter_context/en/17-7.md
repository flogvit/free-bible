# Historical and Cultural Context for Esther 7

## People

**Harbonah** (חַרְבוֹנָה) is mentioned here as one of the eunuchs who served before the king. He was previously named in 1:10 as one of the seven court officials serving King Ahasuerus. His role in this chapter is crucial—he draws the king’s attention to the gallows Haman had prepared for Mordecai. As a court official, Harbonah had direct access to the king and could influence events at a decisive moment.

## Places

**The palace garden** (גִּנַּת הַבִּיתָן) is mentioned as the place where the king went in his anger. Persian palaces were known for their elaborate gardens, called *pairidaeza* (the origin of our word "paradise"). Archaeological excavations at Persepolis and Susa have uncovered remains of such garden complexes with water channels and plantings. The king’s retreat to this place was likely both to find space to think and to preserve his dignity—displaying open rage in the presence of guests would have been inappropriate for a Persian great king.

## Events and Customs

**Falling on the couch** where the queen lay (verse 8) was interpreted by the king as an assault against the queen. In Persian court etiquette, any physical closeness to the king’s women was strictly forbidden, and even seemingly innocent actions could be punished with death. Haman probably threw himself down to beg for mercy, but his action was—consciously or unconsciously—misinterpreted. **Covering the face** (וּפְנֵי הָמָן חָפוּ) was done by servants as a sign that he was condemned to death. Greek sources describe how Persians covered the faces of those sentenced to die.

## Key Terms

The expression **"sold"** (נִמְכַּרְנוּ) in verse 4 refers back to the ten thousand talents of silver Haman offered the king (3:9). Esther deliberately uses economic language to appeal to the king’s self-interest—the enemy harms not only the Jews but also the king’s wealth. The verb **לִכְבּוֹשׁ** ("to subdue/abuse") in verse 8 carries strong connotations of sexual assault in biblical Hebrew (cf. Est 7:8; Neh 5:5), which explains the king’s violent reaction.

**The tree/gallows** (הָעֵץ) fifty cubits high (about 23 meters) was likely a stake for impalement or public display of the body, not hanging in the Western sense. Persians often used impalement as an execution method, and the enormous height was intended to make the shame maximally visible.
