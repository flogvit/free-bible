# Historical and Cultural Context for Colossians 3

## Key Concepts

The chapter contains several Greek words and expressions with special significance. The expression **τὰ ἄνω** ("that which is above," vv. 1-2) stands in contrast to **τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς** ("that which is on the earth"). This vertical cosmology was well known in both Jewish and Greco-Roman thought, but Paul gives it a Christ-centered content by linking "the above" to Christ's position at God's right hand.

The imagery of **stripping off** (ἀπεκδυσάμενοι) and **putting on** (ἐνδυσάμενοι) in verses 9-12 likely refers to baptismal practices in the early church, where the baptized literally stripped off their clothes before baptism and were clothed in new, white garments afterward. The metaphor of "the old self" and "the new self" thus gains a concrete liturgical resonance.

The verb **βραβευέτω** (v. 15, "let ... rule/judge") is drawn from athletic language and refers to the role of the umpire in the Greek games. The image of an umpire deciding the contest was easily recognizable to readers in the Greco-Roman world.

## Events and Customs

The **Household Codes** (vv. 18-25) represent a specific literary form that gives exhortations to various groups within the household. Such lists were well known in Greco-Roman moral philosophy, especially in the Stoic tradition, where duties between spouses, parents and children, as well as masters and slaves were discussed. What distinguishes the Christian version is the grounding "in the Lord" (ἐν κυρίῳ) and the mutual obligation placed on the superior party (husbands must love, fathers must not provoke, masters must treat [slaves] justly).

**Slavery** was a fundamental social institution in the Roman Empire, and a significant portion of the population were slaves. Paul's exhortation to slaves to work "from the heart, as for the Lord" (v. 23) gave them a dignity that was unusual in his day, even though the letter does not directly challenge the institution itself.

## People and Groups

The list of social and ethnic categories abolished in verse 11 is remarkable: **Ἕλλην καὶ Ἰουδαῖος** (Greek and Jew), **περιτομὴ καὶ ἀκροβυστία** (circumcised and uncircumcised), **βάρβαρος, Σκύθης** (barbarian, Scythian), **δοῦλος, ἐλεύθερος** (slave, free). In antiquity, the Scythians were regarded as the most primitive and wild among the barbarians – they represented the extreme edge of civilization's boundaries. By including them, Paul emphasizes the universal scope of the gospel. This list is more comprehensive than the parallel in Galatians 3:28 and reflects the gospel's radical dismantling of social and ethnic dividing lines.
