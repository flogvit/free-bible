# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 17

## People

**Jason (Ἰάσων)** was Paul’s host in Thessalonica and had to post bail for his guests when the riot broke out. The name Jason was common among Hellenistic Jews as a Greek form of the Hebrew Joshua/Jeshua. He took significant personal risk by sheltering the missionaries.

**Dionysius the Areopagite (Διονύσιος ὁ Ἀρεοπαγίτης)** was a member of the Areopagus council in Athens, indicating high social status. Council members were usually former officials from the city’s elite. The fact that such a person came to faith shows that the gospel reached all levels of society. **Damaris (Δάμαρις)** is mentioned by name as a believer in Athens. Her mention alongside Dionysius the Areopagite suggests she held some social standing or significance in the early church.

## Places

**Thessalonica** was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia and an important trading city along the Via Egnatia, the main road between Rome and the East. The city had a substantial Jewish population with its own synagogue. Luke’s use of the title "politarchs" (πολιτάρχας) for the city’s rulers is confirmed by archaeological finds and demonstrates his accuracy, since this title was specific to Macedonian cities.

**Berea** (modern-day Veria) lay about 80 km southwest of Thessalonica, away from the main road. The Jewish community there is described as "more noble" (εὐγενέστεροι) because they examined the Scriptures daily to test Paul’s message.

**Athens** was no longer a political superpower at this time but remained the undisputed center of Greek philosophy and culture. The city was known for its countless temples, altars, and statues. The Roman author Petronius joked that it was easier to find a god than a person in Athens. **Agora** (ἀγορά) was the central marketplace where philosophers traditionally debated – Socrates had done the same at this spot over 400 years earlier.

**Areopagus (Ἄρειος Πάγος)**, "the Hill of Ares", was both a geographical location (a rock northwest of the Acropolis) and the name of Athens’ venerable council that met there. The council had jurisdiction over religious and moral matters, including the introduction of new deities into the city. It was therefore natural that Paul was brought there to explain his "new teaching".

## Events and Customs

**The accusation in Thessalonica** had political explosive power: proclaiming "another king than Caesar" (βασιλέα ἕτερον... Ἰησοῦν) was potentially high treason (*maiestas*). Under Emperor Claudius (41–54 AD), there was particular sensitivity regarding Jewish disturbances – Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome around the year 49. **The bail (τὸ ἱκανὸν)** that Jason had to pay was a financial guarantee that his guests would not cause further trouble.

**The debate culture in Athens** was legendary. Luke’s remark that Athenians and visitors "spent their time doing nothing but telling or hearing something new" (v. 21) matches contemporary descriptions. Demosthenes had criticized the same thing centuries earlier, and Roman authors like Cicero confirmed this characterization.

## Key Terms

**Spermologos (σπερμολόγος)** – literally "seed-picker" – was a derogatory Athenian slang term. Originally it described birds picking up seeds; figuratively it meant a person who gathered and scattered random fragments of ideas without real understanding. The Epicureans and Stoics regarded Paul as such an intellectual amateur.

**"Strange gods" (ξένων δαιμονίων)** – the Athenians thought Paul was preaching new deities. Interestingly, this was the same accusation that had been brought against Socrates in 399 BC. Some scholars suggest the listeners misunderstood "Jesus" (Ἰησοῦς) and "Anastasis" (ἀνάστασις/resurrection) as the names of a divine pair – a male and a female deity.

**Deisidaimonesterous (δεισιδαιμονεστέρους)** in verse 22 can mean both "very religious" (positive) and "superstitious" (negative). Paul likely used the word with diplomatic ambiguity to win goodwill from his audience.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Several **inscriptions bearing the title "politarch"** have been found in the Thessalonica area, including a famous inscription from the Vardar Gate (now in the British Museum) that mentions this title. This confirms Luke’s accuracy, since "politarch" was an unusual title that hardly appears in other ancient literature.

**Altars to unknown gods** are attested by several ancient authors. Pausanias (1st–2nd century AD) describes altars "to unknown gods" along the road from the port of Phaleron to Athens. Diogenes Laertius reports that Epimenides of Crete recommended erecting such altars to ward off a plague in Athens. The philosopher Apollonius of Tyana is also said to have seen them.

**The quotation in verse 28** – "For we are also his offspring" – comes from the Stoic poet Aratus of Soli (ca. 315–240 BC) in his astronomical poem *Phaenomena*. A similar formulation is found in Cleanthes’ *Hymn to Zeus*. By quoting Greek poets, Paul demonstrated familiarity with his audience’s own tradition and built a bridge between Greek philosophy and biblical creation theology.
