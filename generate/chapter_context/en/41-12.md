# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 12

## People

**Herodians** were a political group that supported the Herodian dynasty and, indirectly, Roman rule in Judea. Their collaboration with the Pharisees (v. 13) was unusual, since the Pharisees generally opposed Roman influence. This alliance shows how threatened the religious and political leaders felt by Jesus.

**Sadducees** were an aristocratic religious-political group closely connected to the temple priesthood and high priests. They accepted only the five books of Moses as authoritative and rejected belief in resurrection, angels, and spirits. Their hypothetical question about a woman with seven husbands (vv. 18–23) was a standard argument they used against the Pharisees' teaching on resurrection.

## Places

**The Temple Treasury** (γαζοφυλάκιον, v. 41) was located in the Court of Women in the temple complex. According to the Mishnah, there were thirteen trumpet-shaped chests (shofar boxes) where people could place their gifts for various purposes. The area was publicly accessible, and it was common for people to observe others' offerings.

## Events and Customs

**Levirate Marriage** (v. 19) was an ancient custom described in Deuteronomy 25:5–6. When a man died without children, his brother was to marry the widow to secure the deceased's family line and inheritance. This practice was still known in Jesus' time, although it was probably less common than in earlier periods.

**Vineyard Cultivation and Tenancy** (vv. 1–12) reflects common practice in Palestine. Absentee landowners leased their land to tenants in exchange for a share of the harvest. Tensions between tenants and landowners were frequent, and historical sources document several cases of tenant uprisings during this period.

## Key Terms

**Denarius** (δηνάριον, v. 15) was a Roman silver coin equivalent to a day's wage for a laborer. The coin bore the emperor's portrait and an inscription that often included divine titles for the emperor—something many Jews regarded as idolatry.

**Kensos** (κῆνσος, v. 14) was a direct tax imposed on Judea by Rome in AD 6 when it became a Roman province. This tax was deeply unpopular and had sparked the revolt led by Judas of Galilee. The question about taxes was therefore politically charged.

**Lepton** (λεπτά, v. 42) was the smallest Jewish bronze coin. Two lepta equaled one kodrantes (Roman quadrans), which was the smallest Roman coin—worth approximately 1/64 of a denarius.

**Shema** (vv. 29–30) is the central Jewish confession of faith from Deuteronomy 6:4–5, which devout Jews recited morning and evening. The name comes from the Hebrew opening word "Hear" (שְׁמַע, shema).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of denarii from the reign of Emperor Tiberius (AD 14–37) confirm details in the discussion about the tax coin. These coins bear the inscription "Ti[berius] Caesar Divi Aug[usti] F[ilius] Augustus" (Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus)—exactly the kind of "image and inscription" Jesus referred to. Such coins have been found in large numbers throughout Israel.
