# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 21

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographically significant locations. **Negev** (נֶגֶב), **Temanah** (תֵּימָנָה), and **Darom** (דָּרוֹם) are all Hebrew words for south or a southern direction, and here they figuratively refer to Judah and Jerusalem as seen from Babylon. The prophecy concerning the fire of "the forest of the Negev" (יַעַר הַשָּׂדֶה נֶגֶב) in verses 1–5 is then explicitly interpreted as referring to Jerusalem and its sanctuaries in verse 7.

**Rabbah of the Ammonites** (רַבַּת בְּנֵֽי־עַמּ֑וֹן) was the capital of the Ammonite kingdom, located where modern-day Amman in Jordan stands today. Archaeological excavations have confirmed significant settlement from the Iron Age. The place is mentioned because Nebuchadnezzar stood at a crossroads and had to choose whether to attack Jerusalem or Rabbah first (verses 25–27). Historically, he chose Jerusalem, while Ammon was temporarily spared.

## Events and Customs

The chapter provides a rare detailed description of Babylonian divination methods in verse 26. **Belomancy** (קִלְקַל בַּחִצִּים, "shook the arrows") was a widespread practice in which arrows inscribed with the names of potential targets were shaken in a quiver; the first to fall out or be drawn indicated the will of the gods. **Teraphim** (תְּרָפִים) were household idols or cult figures used for oracle responses, known from both Mesopotamian and Canaanite religion. **Hepatoscopy** (רָאָה בַּכָּבֵד, "examined the liver") was the most important form of divination in Mesopotamia; thousands of liver texts from Babylon and Assyria have been preserved, containing detailed interpretive rules for various anomalies in the shape of sacrificial livers.

## Key Terms

The sword (חֶרֶב) is the dominant symbol of the chapter and is mentioned about fifteen times. The Hebrew expression "drawn from its sheath" (הוֹצֵאתִי חַרְבִּי מִתַּעְרָהּ) in verses 8–10 denotes irreversible judgment—once the sword is drawn, it shall not return.

The expression "strike the thigh" (סְפֹק אֶל־יָרֵךְ) in verse 17 was a gesture of grief and despair in the ancient Near East, also attested in Jeremiah 31:19.

The verse concerning the turban and crown (verse 31) uses **mitsnefet** (מִצְנֶפֶת), the high priest’s head covering, and **atara** (עֲטָרָה), the royal crown. This suggests that both priestly and royal authority will be removed. Zedekiah was the reigning king at this time.

Verse 32 contains the important expression עַד־בֹּא אֲשֶׁר־לוֹ הַמִּשְׁפָּט ("until he comes to whom judgment belongs"), which is traditionally understood as a messianic reference with an allusion to Genesis 49:10.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Babylonian chronicles confirm Nebuchadnezzar’s campaigns against both Judah and Ammon during this period. An inscription from the time of Nebuchadnezzar mentions his consultation of oracles before making military decisions, which corresponds with Ezekiel’s description. Clay liver models inscribed with divinatory texts from Babylon, dated to this period, illustrate the practice described in verse 26 and show that the prophet had precise knowledge of Babylonian rituals.
