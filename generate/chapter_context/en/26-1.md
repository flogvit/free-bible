# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 1

## People

**Ezekiel son of Buzi** is introduced here as a priest (*hakkohen*) who receives his prophetic call. The precise time reference "in the thirtieth year" (v. 1) likely refers to Ezekiel's own age—just when, according to tradition, he could have begun his priestly service (cf. Num 4:3), God instead calls him to prophetic ministry by the Kebar River. His father Buzi is otherwise unknown in biblical texts.

**King Jehoiachin** is mentioned as a chronological reference point (v. 2). He reigned for only three months in 598/597 BC before being taken into exile to Babylon. His fifth year of exile dates this vision to approximately 593 BC.

## Places

**The Kebar River** (*nehar kebar*) was a canal within the Babylonian canal system, not a natural river. It is identified with *nāru kabaru* ("the great canal") known from Akkadian texts, which ran through the area near Nippur in southern Mesopotamia. This region housed a significant Jewish exiled population.

**The land of the Chaldeans** (*erets kasdim*) is the biblical designation for Babylonia, the region where Ezekiel was living in exile.

## Key Terms

**Hashmal** (חַשְׁמַל, vv. 4, 27) is a rare Hebrew word that appears only in Ezekiel's visions. Its meaning is uncertain—possible interpretations include "glowing metal," "electrum" (an alloy of gold and silver), or a type of glowing substance. The Septuagint translates it as *elektron*.

**Chayyot** (חַיּוֹת, "living creatures") describes the four composite beings in the vision. These have parallels in Mesopotamian iconography, particularly *lamassu* and *shedu*—winged creatures with human faces and animal bodies that guarded temples and palaces in Assyria and Babylonia. For Ezekiel's Jewish audience, such images would have been familiar from their surroundings in exile, but here they are radically reinterpreted as servants of God.

**Tarshish** (תַּרְשִׁישׁ, v. 16) describes the appearance of the wheels and likely refers to chrysolite or a similar gemstone with a shimmering yellow-green color.

## Events and Customs

**The chariot-throne motif**: Although the word *merkavah* ("chariot") is not used explicitly in this chapter (it first appears in Ezek 10:2ff), Jewish interpretive tradition (*ma'aseh merkavah*) has traditionally connected this vision with a divine throne-chariot. The motif has deep roots in ancient Near Eastern thought, where gods were often depicted enthroned on chariots carried by mythical beings. In Mesopotamian tradition, the majestic radiance of the gods (*melammu*) manifested as brilliant light. Ezekiel's vision adopts and transforms this symbolism: the God of Israel is not bound to one place (such as the Jerusalem temple) but moves freely—even into the territory of the land of exile.

**Falling on one's face** (v. 28) was the common physical response to theophanies in the ancient Near East, expressing total submission and awe before the divine.
