# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 23

## Places

**Tyre** (צֹר, *Tsor*) was the most important port city of the Phoenicians, strategically located on an island and the mainland along the Lebanese coast. The chapter prophesies destruction over this city, which was known for its wealthy merchants and princes (v.8). Its trade with "all the kingdoms of the earth" (v.17) made it the commercial center of the Mediterranean Sea.

**Sidon** (צִידוֹן) is mentioned as Tyre's sister city (vv.2,4,12). In verse 4, the sea is personified speaking to Sidon: "I have had no birth pains, I have not given birth, I have not raised young men or brought up virgins." In verse 12, the people are referred to as "oppressed virgin, daughter of Sidon."

**Tarshish** (תַּרְשִׁישׁ) likely refers to Tartessos in southern Spain. The "ships of Tarshish" are called upon to lament (vv.1,14) because their harbor is destroyed. The "daughter of Tarshish" (v.10) is urged to flow freely like the Nile, for the "belt" (the harbor's restriction) no longer exists.

**Kittim** (כִּתִּים) refers to Cyprus, especially the city of Kition. In verse 1, the ships of Tarshish receive news of Tyre's fall apparently from Kittim, and in verse 12, Kittim is mentioned as a possible place of refuge that will also offer no rest.

**Chaldea and Assyria** (v.13) - This difficult verse mentions "the land of the Chaldeans" (אֶרֶץ כַּשְׂדִּים) and Assyria (אַשּׁוּר). The text seems to say that Assyria founded the land for desert peoples (צִיִּים), and that they raised siege towers and destroyed palaces. The verse points to the great powers that threatened Tyre.

## Key Terms

**Canaanites** (כִּנְעָנֶיהָ, v.8) are used here with their original meaning of "merchants" or "traders." The word *kena'ani* referred both to the ethnic group and to the trade profession, showing how strongly the Phoenicians were associated with commerce.

**Shihor and Yeor** (שִׁחֹר og יְאוֹר, v.3) - Shihor means "the dark/black" and refers to the Nile or one of its branches. The verse describes how "the seed of the Nile" and the harvest from the great river (יְאוֹר, *Jeor* - the common designation for the Nile) were Tyre's income, and the city became "a marketplace for the peoples."

**Seventy years** (שִׁבְעִים שָׁנָה, vv.15,17) are described as "the days of one king" (כִּימֵי מֶלֶךְ אֶחָד). After this period, the LORD will "visit" (יִפְקֹד) Tyre, and the city will return to its "harlot's wages" (אֶתְנַן) - its trade with all the kingdoms of the earth.

## Events and Customs

The image of **the harlot singing** (vv.15-16) to be remembered after having been forgotten was a well-known cultural reference. Prostitutes in ancient cities used music and song to attract customers. Isaiah uses this provocative image to describe how Tyre will resume its trade - she must take up the harp, walk around the city, play skillfully, and sing many songs to be remembered.

The expression **"the fortress of the sea"** (מָעוֹז הַיָּם, v.4) and the personification of the sea as childless allude to Phoenician religion, where the sea and cities were viewed as divine powers. Isaiah overturns this by showing that Tyre's strength is illusory before the God of Israel.

**The conclusion of the chapter** (v.18) is remarkably positive: Tyre's trade (סַחַר) and harlot's wages (אֶתְנַן) will ultimately be "holy to the LORD" (קֹדֶשׁ לַיהוָה). They will not be stored in treasuries but will serve those who dwell before the face of the LORD - for food, nourishment, and "ancient/dignified clothing" (מְכַסֶּה עָתִיק).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian annals confirm repeated campaigns against the Phoenician cities. Sennacherib (705-681 BC) besieged Tyre, and Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal pressured the city with tribute and blockades. Tyre's island location made a complete conquest difficult, but economic strangulation was possible. A Babylonian text mentions that Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen years (c. 585-572 BC), which corresponds to the severity of the prophecy and the seventy years mentioned in the text.
