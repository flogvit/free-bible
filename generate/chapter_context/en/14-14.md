# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 14

## People

**Asa** is presented in this chapter as a reforming king who actively removed foreign cultic objects and urged Judah to seek the Lord and keep the law (vv. 1–4). His religious faithfulness is directly linked to the peaceful period that made fortification possible (vv. 5–7). When facing an overwhelming enemy force, he demonstrated his trust in God through prayer (v. 10).

**Zerah the Cushite** is a debated figure. The term "Cushite" may refer to someone from Cush (Nubia/Ethiopia) or possibly an Arabian tribal chieftain, since "Cush" in some biblical texts can denote areas in northwestern Arabia. Some scholars have suggested a connection with Egyptian military campaigns during the 22nd (Libyan) dynasty, but this remains uncertain. The army size of "a thousand thousands" (אֶלֶף אֲלָפִים) may be a literary device to emphasize the enemy’s overwhelming strength, or the number could represent military units.

## Places

**Mareshah** (Hebrew: מָרֵשָׁה) was an important fortified city in Judah, located in the Shephelah region (the lowland between the coastal plain and the mountains). The city lay strategically on the main road from the coastal plain up to Hebron. Archaeological excavations at Tell Sandahanna (Tell Maresha) have uncovered fortifications, although the most extensive finds date to the Persian and Hellenistic periods.

**The Valley of Zephathah** (גֵּיא צְפַתָה) near Mareshah was the battlefield where Asa met the Cushite army. The valley’s location made it a natural gateway for invading forces from the south and west.

**Gerar** was an ancient city in the Negev region, near the border with Egypt. The pursuit of the enemy all the way to Gerar (vv. 12–13) indicates that the invading force came from the south or southwest.

## Events and Customs

**Religious Reform and Removal of Cultic Objects** (vv. 2–4): Asa’s reform involved removing several types of pagan cultic objects. "Foreign altars" (מִזְבְּחוֹת הַנֵּכָר) were altars dedicated to other gods. "High places" (בָּמוֹת) were open sanctuaries on hilltops, originally used in Canaanite religion. "Sacred pillars" (מַצֵּבוֹת) were upright stones used in worship. "Asherim" (אֲשֵׁרִים) were cultic poles or trees associated with the goddess Asherah. "Incense altars" (חַמָּנִים) were likely incense altars, possibly connected to sun worship, although their exact meaning remains debated.

**Fortification Building in Times of Peace** (vv. 5–7): The text highlights Asa’s strategy of building fortified cities during periods of peace. This reflects ancient Near Eastern military wisdom—strengthening defenses when resources and labor were available before crises arose. Asa’s speech to Judah (v. 6) explicitly links the peace to the people’s seeking of the Lord.

**The Army Strength** (vv. 7–8): The numbers given for Asa’s army—300,000 from Judah with large shields (צִנָּה) and spears, and 280,000 from Benjamin with small shields (מָגֵן) and bows—reflect the different military traditions of the tribes. Benjamin was traditionally known for its archers (cf. Judges 20:16).

**Asa’s Prayer** (v. 10): The prayer before battle is theologically central to the chapter. Asa expresses total dependence on God: "There is no difference with you whether one helps many or those without strength" (אֵין־עִמְּךָ לַעְזוֹר בֵּין רַב לְאֵין כֹּחַ). The phrase "we rely on you" (עָלֶיךָ נִשְׁעַנּוּ) emphasizes that military victory does not depend on human strength.

**War Booty** (vv. 12–14): After the victory, the Israelites took considerable plunder, including livestock and camels from the nomads’ tent encampments. This indicates that the invading force included nomadic elements, which may support the theory of Arabian involvement.

## Key Terms

**"To seek the Lord"** (לִדְרוֹשׁ אֶת־יְהוָה): This expression appears several times in the chapter (vv. 3, 6) and denotes more than just prayer—it involves active devotion, obedience to God’s will, and orienting the entire nation toward Yahweh worship.

**"Rest" or "quiet"** (שָׁקְטָה/הֵנִיחַ): The chapter links national peace directly to faithfulness to God. The words שָׁקְטָה ("was quiet," vv. 4, 5) and הֵנִיחַ ("gave rest," v. 5) describe the peaceful period as a direct result of Asa’s religious reforms.
