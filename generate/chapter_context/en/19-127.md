# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 127

## People

The psalm is attributed to Solomon (לִשְׁלֹמֹה), the only one of the pilgrimage psalms with this attribution. The connection to Solomon is significant for several reasons: He was known as the builder of the temple (1 Kings 6–8), and the wordplay between the name Solomon (שְׁלֹמֹה) and "peace/wholeness" (שָׁלוֹם) underscores the psalm's theme of blessing and rest. Solomon's famous wisdom regarding work, rest, and family life, as expressed in Ecclesiastes, is also reflected in this psalm's themes.

## Places

The psalm mentions "the city" (עִיר) and "the gate" (שָׁעַר). In ancient Israel, the city gate was the central place for legal decisions, business transactions, and public debate. Here the elders gathered to settle disputes and make agreements (cf. Ruth 4:1–11). When verse 5 mentions speaking with enemies "at the gate," it refers to legal confrontations where many sons gave a man authority and support in public matters.

## Key Terms

The expression "לִידִידוֹ" (lidido) in verse 2, translated as "his friend" or "the one he loves," uses the root ידד which denotes loving devotion. The same root word is used in the name Jedidiah (יְדִידְיָה), which Solomon received through the prophet Nathan in 2 Samuel 12:25, and which means "beloved of the Lord." This creates a deliberate connection between the psalm's attribution and its content.

The term "לֶחֶם הָעֲצָבִים" (bread won with toil/pain) in verse 2 uses the word עֲצָבִים, which is related to עִצָּבוֹן used regarding human striving in Genesis 3:16–17. The psalm contrasts this painful striving with the rest that God gives to His beloved ones.

## Events and Customs

The image of sons as "arrows in a warrior's hand" (verse 4) and a full quiver (verse 5) reflects the patriarchal family structure in the ancient Near East, where sons represented military strength, economic security, and social status. Many sons meant protection for the family and respect in society. This way of thinking was common throughout the entire ancient Near Eastern culture, where large families were seen as a concrete sign of divine blessing.
