# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 19

## Places

The chapter opens with Jesus leaving Galilee and coming to "the region of Judea beyond the Jordan" (v. 1). This refers to Perea, an area east of the Jordan River that was under Herod Antipas's jurisdiction. The route through Perea was the common pilgrimage path for Galileans wishing to avoid traveling through Samaria on their way to Jerusalem. This geographical transition marks the beginning of Jesus's final journey toward Jerusalem and his crucifixion.

## Events and Customs

**The Divorce Debate (vv. 3–9):** The Pharisees' question about divorce "for any cause" (κατὰ πᾶσαν αἰτίαν) reflects an ongoing rabbinic debate between two schools. The School of Shammai interpreted Deuteronomy 24:1 restrictively, allowing divorce only in cases of serious sexual immorality or adultery. The School of Hillel interpreted it liberally, permitting divorce for almost anything, even if a wife burned the food. The phrase "certificate of divorce" (βιβλίον ἀποστασίου) refers to the legal document known as the *get*, which was necessary for a woman to remarry.

**Laying Hands on Children (vv. 13–15):** It was common practice for parents to bring children to a rabbi for blessing and the laying on of hands. Rabbis were regarded as channels of God's blessing. The Greek word παιδία can include children from infancy up to early adolescence.

## Key Terms

**Eunuchs (εὐνοῦχοι, v. 12):** Jesus mentions three categories: those born without the ability to reproduce, those made eunuchs by humans (common in Eastern courts and as harem guards), and those who "have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" – a figurative expression for voluntary celibacy. This was radical in Jewish culture, where marriage and childbearing were considered religious duties.

**Palingenesia (παλιγγενεσία, v. 28):** This rare Greek word, meaning "rebirth" or "regeneration," is used in the New Testament only here and in Titus 3:5. In Stoic philosophy, it described the cyclical renewal of the universe after a cosmic conflagration (ekpyrosis). Jesus uses it to refer to the eschatological renewal of all things, when the disciples will "sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel."

**The Needle's Eye (τρυπήματος ῥαφίδος, v. 24):** The image of a camel passing through the eye of a needle was a hyperbolic idiom for impossibility. Suggestions that the "needle's eye" referred to a small city gate lack historical evidence – the point is precisely the absurdity and impossibility, which only God can make possible (v. 26).

## People

**The Rich Young Man (vv. 16–22):** Matthew describes him as νεανίσκος (young man), while Luke calls him a "ruler" (ἄρχων). The combination of youth, leadership, and great wealth (κτήματα πολλά – "many possessions") suggests he belonged to the landowning aristocracy. His claim to have kept all the commandments since his youth reflects the Pharisaic ideal of piety, where obedience to the law was carefully recorded.
