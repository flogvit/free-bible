# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 25

## Characters

Bildad from Shuhah delivers his third and final speech here, which is also the shortest of all the friends' speeches in the book (only 6 verses). Shuhah was likely a region in northwestern Arabia or east of the Jordan, associated with Abraham's son Shuah through Keturah (Genesis 25:2). The fact that Bildad's speech is so short may indicate that the traditional wisdom theology he represents has reached its limits when confronted with Job's situation.

## Key Terms

The chapter contains several important Hebrew expressions. **"Hamshel vafachad"** (הַמְשֵׁל וָפַחַד) in verse 2 describes God's "dominion and awe-inspiring power" – this word pair emphasizes God's sovereign authority. The expression **"shalom bimromav"** (שָׁלוֹם בִּמְרוֹמָיו), "peace in his heights," alludes to an ancient Near Eastern concept that even heavenly beings must be kept in order by God.

The word **"gedudav"** (לִגְדוּדָ֑יו) in verse 3, "his armies," refers to angelic hosts or heavenly beings, a concept known from several Semitic cultures. The expression **"yelud ishah"** (יְלוּד אִשָּׁה), "born of a woman," in verse 4 is a poetic term for human frailty and limitation.

## Events and Customs

Bildad's rhetoric in verses 5–6 reflects a widespread ancient Near Eastern view of human uncleanness compared to the divine. The comparison of humans to **"rimmah"** (רִמָּה, maggot/larva) and **"toleah"** (תּוֹלֵעָה, worm) was common in Mesopotamian and Canaanite literature to express humility before God. This self-abasement is also found in Babylonian prayers and hymns, where humans refer to themselves in similar terms when addressing the divine.
