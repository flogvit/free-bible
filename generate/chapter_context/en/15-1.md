# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezra 1

## People

**Cyrus (כּוֹרֶשׁ)** was the founder of the Persian Achaemenid Empire and conquered Babylon in 539 B.C.E. His first regnal year over Babylon (538 B.C.E.) marks the date of the decree in this chapter. Cyrus was known for his tolerant religious policy, which allowed deported peoples to return home and restore their places of worship. This stands in sharp contrast to the Assyrian and Babylonian practice of forced deportations.

**Sheshbazzar (שֵׁשְׁבַּצַּר)** is referred to as "the prince of Judah" (הַנָּשִׂיא לִיהוּדָה). The name is likely Babylonian (perhaps from *Šamaš-apla-uṣur* or *Sîn-ab-uṣur*). His exact identity is debated—some scholars identify him with Zerubbabel, while others view him as a separate person, possibly identical to Shenazzar in 1 Chron. 3:18. He received the temple articles and led the first group back.

**Mithredath (מִתְרְדָת)** was the royal treasurer (גִּזְבָּר – a Persian loanword). The name is Persian and means "given by Mithra" (the name of the sun god), testifying to Persian administrative presence in Babylon.

## Events and Customs

Cyrus’s decree represents a new imperial model in the Middle East. While the Assyrians and Babylonians deported peoples to break national identity, the Persians chose to support local religions and cults to ensure loyalty. The decree encourages voluntary gifts from neighbors (v. 4), reflecting the pattern of the exodus from Egypt (Exod. 12:35–36), a parallel Jewish readers would hardly have missed.

The return of the temple articles (vv. 7–11) was of enormous symbolic significance. These items represented God’s presence and had been taken to Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar as war booty and as signs of the victory of Babylonian gods. Their return now signaled a theological reversal—the God of Israel had not been defeated but had used Babylon as an instrument and now raised up Cyrus as His new instrument.

## Key Terms

The expression **"YHWH, God of Heaven" (יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם)** in verse 2 is characteristic of texts from the Persian period. The title "God of Heaven" functioned as a diplomatic expression acceptable to both Jews and Persians, and the Persians may have seen parallels with their own high god Ahura Mazda.

The word **"awakened" (הֵעִיר)** is used regarding God’s action toward Cyrus’s spirit. This term emphasizes that the actions of pagan kings are under God’s sovereign control—a central theological theme linked to Isa. 44:28–45:1, where Cyrus is called God’s "shepherd" and "anointed."

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Cyrus Cylinder**, discovered in Babylon in 1879, confirms Cyrus’s policy of returning divine statues and cult objects to their original temples. Although it does not mention the Jews specifically, it describes how Cyrus "returned (the gods) to their sanctuaries" and "gathered all the inhabitants and returned them to their homes." This provides a parallel to the decree in Ezra 1 and confirms its historical credibility.

The **Nabonidus Chronicle**, a Babylonian text, describes Cyrus’s entry into Babylon in 539 B.C.E. and confirms the peaceful transfer of power that enabled Cyrus to implement his religious policy immediately.
