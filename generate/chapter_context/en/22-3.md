# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Solomon 3

## Places

The chapter mentions that the woman searches in "the city" (הָעִיר, *ha'ir*) with its "streets and squares" (שְׁוָקִים וּרְחֹבוֹת, *shvakim urechovot*). Although the city is not explicitly named, the context points to Jerusalem. Israelite cities of that time typically had narrow streets with open squares at the city gates where trade and social activity took place. Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered remains of such urban structures from the Iron Age, including street networks and open plazas.

"Lebanon" (הַלְּבָנוֹן) is mentioned as the source of the wood for Solomon's litter (v. 9). The cedar forests of Lebanon were famous in antiquity and used for prestigious buildings throughout the Middle East. Egyptian, Assyrian, and Babylonian texts all document the import of Lebanese cedar for royal construction projects.

## People

**Solomon's sixty warriors** (vv. 7–8) form a royal guard around his bed (מִטָּה, *mittah*). The number sixty may reflect an actual military unit; we know from other ancient sources that royal guards were often organized in fixed numerical units. The description that each man has his sword at his hip "because of the terrors of the night" (מִפַּחַד בַּלֵּילוֹת) points to a guard force that protected the king especially at night, when dangers were greatest.

**"Daughters of Zion"** (בְּנוֹת צִיּוֹן, v. 11) is a poetic designation for the women of Jerusalem, who are invited to behold the king on his wedding day.

## Events and Customs

The second part of the chapter (vv. 6–11) describes a wedding procession or royal parade. The description of incense rising "from the wilderness" (v. 6) reflects the custom of perfumed processions at royal weddings. Myrrh and frankincense were costly imported goods that marked solemn occasions. The use of incense in wedding ceremonies is well documented in the ancient Middle East.

The mention of "the crown with which his mother crowned him" (v. 11) suggests a wedding custom in which the groom received a crown or wreath. This practice is known from several Middle Eastern cultures and continued in Jewish tradition; the Mishnah (Sotah 9:14) notes that bridal crowns were forbidden during times of war.

## Key Terms

**"Mother's house"** (בֵּית אִמִּי, *bet immi*, v. 4): This expression occurs several times in the Song of Solomon and points to the woman's sphere within the household. In patriarchal societies, women often had their own rooms or areas in the house. That the woman leads her lover to "mother's house" and "mother's bedchamber" marks both intimacy and legitimacy.

**Appiryon** (אַפִּרְיוֹן, v. 9): This rare word for Solomon's litter appears only here in the Bible. The word may be borrowed from Persian (*paryānk*, bed/sofa) or Greek (*phoreion*), both of which denote a closed litter or carrying-bed. Such processional vehicles were common at royal ceremonies in the ancient Middle East.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Ivory reliefs from Phoenician and Syrian workshops show royal processions with portable thrones surrounded by guards, illustrating the scenes described in verses 7–10. From Egypt, detailed depictions of decorated litters with pillars and canopies have been found that resemble the description of Solomon's *appiryon* with silver pillars and a gold base.
