# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 29

## People

**Jeconiah (Jehoiachin)** and the **gebirah** (הַגְּבִירָה, queen mother) are mentioned in verse 2 as leaders of the first deportation in 597 B.C. Jeconiah was 18 years old (according to 2 Kings 24:8) when he was taken to Babylon after only three months on the throne. Babylonian ration lists discovered in Babylon confirm his existence and refer to him as "Yaukin, king of Judah," documenting that he received rations from the Babylonian court.

**Elsasah, son of Shaphan**, and **Gemariah, son of Hilkiah** (verse 3) served as diplomatic envoys from King Zedekiah to Nebuchadnezzar. The Shaphan family was known as supporters of Jeremiah; Shaphan himself had been secretary during Josiah's reforms (2 Kings 22). Hilkiah may be related to the high priest who found the Book of the Law under Josiah, but this is uncertain.

**Ahab, son of Kolaiah**, and **Zedekiah, son of Maaseiah** (verses 21–22) were false prophets in Babylon who prophesied lies. Jeremiah predicts they will be burned by Nebuchadnezzar—a well-known Babylonian execution method also documented in Daniel 3.

**Shemaiah from Nehelam** (verses 24–32) was a false prophet in Babylon who sent letters back to Jerusalem to undermine Jeremiah. His nickname "Nehelamite" (הַנֶּחֱלָמִי) may indicate origin from a place called Nehelam, or it could be a wordplay on the Hebrew *ḥalam* (dreams), possibly an ironic reference to his claimed revelations.

## Places

**Babylon** was at this time the world's most powerful city, capital of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. The Jewish exiles were settled in their own colonies, likely near the rivers (cf. Psalm 137:1). Archaeological findings show that deported peoples were allowed a certain degree of self-government and could engage in trade and agriculture. Jeremiah 29:5–7 reflects this reality by encouraging full integration into community life.

## Events and Customs

**The seventy years** (verse 10) refer to the duration of the exile. This number has been interpreted in various ways: from the first deportation (605 B.C.) to Cyrus's edict (538 B.C.), or from the destruction of the temple (586 B.C.) until its rebuilding (516 B.C.). The number seventy also held symbolic significance in antiquity as a full generation or a rounded period of divine judgment.

**To seek the peace (shalom) of the city** (verse 7) was a radical command. Normally one would curse their oppressors, but Jeremiah instructs the people to pray for Babylon—an unusual theological position that anticipates later Jewish diaspora ethics.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **"aḥarit vetiqvah"** (אַחֲרִית וְתִקְוָה) in verse 11 means "future and hope." The word *aḥarit* implies not merely an end but a good outcome or positive result. *Tiqvah* (hope) is related to the word for "cord" or "line," evoking associations with something to hold onto.

**Hammahpekhet** and **hassinok** (verse 26) were physical instruments of punishment—a staff or yoke used to bend the body into a painful position, and neck irons or leg shackles. This shows that temple authorities had the power to punish those they considered false prophets or religious deviants.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Al-Yahudu archive**, discovered on the Iraqi antiquities market and published in 2015, contains over 100 cuneiform tablets from a Jewish settlement in Babylon dating from 572–477 B.C. These documents record the daily life of Jewish exiles who engaged in trade, owned property, and entered into contracts—precisely the kind of established existence Jeremiah encouraged in this chapter. The names in the archive include biblical names such as Gedaliah, Hanan, and Netanyahu.
