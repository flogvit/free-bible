# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 38

## People

The chapter names four court officials who accused Jeremiah: **Shephatiah son of Mattan**, **Gedaliah son of Pashhur**, **Jucal son of Shelemiah**, and **Pashhur son of Malkijah**. These were influential sarim (שָׂרִים) – high-ranking officials at King Zedekiah’s court. Notably, a clay seal impression (bulla) found in the excavations of the City of David in Jerusalem bears the name "Gedaliah son of Pashhur," and another bulla mentions "Jucal son of Shelemiah" – both named precisely in this chapter. These are rare instances where archaeological findings confirm the names of biblical figures outside the royal family.

**Ebed-Melech** ("the king’s servant") is described as a **Cushite** (Ethiopian/Nubian) and a **saris** (סָרִיס). The term saris can mean court official or eunuch. The presence of Cushites at the Judean court reflects the close ties between Judah and Egypt/Cush during this period, when Egypt was Judah’s main ally against Babylon.

## Places

**The cistern belonging to Malkijah the king’s son** (verse 6) in the guard courtyard was a water-collecting pit (בּוֹר) that was empty of water but full of mud. Such cisterns were common in Jerusalem and were hewn out of rock to collect rainwater. The fact that it belonged to a "king’s son" indicates it was located in a royal area.

**The Benjamin Gate** (verse 7), where the king sat, was likely a gate in the northern city wall, facing toward the territory of the tribe of Benjamin. City gates functioned as courtrooms and administrative centers where the king received petitions and pronounced judgments.

**The third entrance** to the house of the Lord (verse 14) was probably a private or royal entrance to the temple, possibly intended for the king, allowing discreet meetings away from public view.

## Events and Customs

The expression **"weakens the hands"** (מְרַפֵּא אֶת־יְדֵי, verse 4) was a common idiomatic description of undermining morale. The exact same expression is found in the Lachish Letters – military correspondence from the same period – where an officer complains that the leaders in Jerusalem "weaken the hands" of the people. This shows that Jeremiah’s opponents used contemporary military terminology in their accusation.

The officials’ demand for the death penalty and the king’s evasive response (verses 4–5) illustrate the political tension at court. Zedekiah’s statement, "He is in your hands," reveals a king who was powerless against his own officials – a sign of the political chaos in Jerusalem’s final days.

## Key Terms

The expression **"his life shall be his prize"** (נַפְשׁוֹ לְשָׁלָל, verse 2) is a war idiom meaning that one saves one’s life as the only gain, just as a soldier takes spoils of war. In the context of siege warfare, it meant surviving with nothing but one’s life intact.

**"Rags and worn-out clothes"** (בְּלוֹאֵי הַסְּחָבוֹת וְהַמְּלָחִים, verses 11–12) that Ebed-Melech retrieved from "under the treasury" were likely stored textiles intended for charity or practical use. The detailed description of how the rags should protect Jeremiah’s armpits from rope burns shows care and practical wisdom amid crisis.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Lachish Letters**, discovered in 1935 and dated precisely to Jerusalem’s siege in 588–586 BC, provide a unique parallel to this chapter. Letter number 6 contains the complaint that "the words of the officials weaken the hands" – the same terminology used against Jeremiah. This shows that the prophet was not alone in being accused of defeatism, and that similar tensions between the "peace party" and the "war hawks" existed in other cities of Judah during the siege.
