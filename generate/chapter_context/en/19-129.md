# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 129

## Events and Customs

The psalm reflects Israel's collective experience of oppression throughout history. The expression "from my youth" (מִנְּעוּרַי, *minne'uray*) traditionally points back to Israel's early history as a people, particularly the slavery in Egypt and the subsequent wandering. This psalm was likely used liturgically during the pilgrimage festivals, where the people remembered their history of both suffering and divine deliverance.

The blessing in verse 8 ("May the Lord bless you; may we bless you in the name of the Lord") reflects a common custom where passersby would bless harvest workers in the fields. This social practice underscores the contrasting image: Zion's enemies will not receive such a blessing because they are as barren as grass on the rooftops.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word חָרְשׁוּ (*charshu*, "plowed") in verse 3 creates a powerful image of oppression. The metaphor of plowing on someone's back describes brutal mistreatment—as if the body were soil to be worked over. The word לְמַעֲנִיתָם (*lema'anitam*, "their furrows") reinforces the picture of deep wounds and scars from whipping or other physical torture.

The expression עֲבוֹת רְשָׁעִים (*avot resha'im*, "the cords/bonds of the wicked") in verse 4 refers to the thick ropes that tied the yoke to the draft animals during plowing. God "cuts" these ropes, meaning liberation from the oppressors' control.

## Places

Zion (צִיּוֹן) is mentioned in verse 5 as the target of the enemies' hatred. In the pilgrimage context, this is especially significant—those singing this psalm are on their way up to Zion. The enemies' opposition to Zion stands in sharp contrast to the pilgrim's love for Jerusalem and the temple.

The image of "grass on the rooftops" (חֲצִיר גַּגּוֹת) in verse 6 refers to the flat clay roofs that were common in Israel. Seeds could sprout in the thin layer of soil on such roofs, but the plants quickly withered in the sun because they lacked deep roots and sufficient soil.
