# Historical and Cultural Context for Hebrews 11

## People

The chapter presents an extensive "hall of faith" list spanning from creation to the prophetic era. Abel, Enoch, and Noah represent the pre-Abrahamic period. The patriarchs Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, and Jacob form the core of the narrative (vv. 8–22). Moses receives broad coverage (vv. 23–28), followed by a rapid overview of the period of the judges featuring Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, and Samuel (v. 32). Rahab is specifically mentioned as the only non-Israelite named individually in the chapter—remarkable both because of her profession as a prostitute (πόρνη) and her Canaanite background. In Jewish tradition she was highly honored; rabbinic literature claims that several prophets descended from her.

## Places

The chapter refers to several key locations in Israel's history. "The land promised" (γῆν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας, v. 9) denotes Canaan, where the patriarchs lived as foreigners in tents. The Red Sea (Ἐρυθρὰν Θάλασσαν, v. 29) marks the exodus from Egypt. Jericho (v. 30) was the first Canaanite city to fall during the conquest. Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) is known from archaeological excavations, although the dating and interpretation of the wall remains are debated among scholars. Egypt is mentioned repeatedly as the place of Moses' upbringing at Pharaoh's court and of Israel's enslavement.

## Events and Customs

Passover (τὸ πάσχα) and the blood on the doorposts (πρόσχυσιν τοῦ αἵματος, v. 28) refer to the first Passover night in Exodus 12, where the blood protected against "the destroyer" (ὁ ὀλοθρεύων). This term reflects the Jewish understanding of a death angel. Abraham's offering of Isaac (vv. 17–19)—called *Akedah* ("the binding") in Jewish tradition—was a central theme in early Jewish theology and was regarded as the ultimate test of faith. Joseph's command concerning his bones (v. 22) reflected Egyptian and Israelite belief in the importance of burial place and linked his hope to the promised land.

## Key Terms

The Greek word ὑπόστασις (v. 1) is philosophically charged and can mean "substance," "foundation," or "assurance"—here used to express that faith gives reality to what is hoped for. The word ἔλεγχος means "proof" or "conviction" and was used in a legal context. The expression παρεπίδημοι (v. 13, "foreigners/pilgrims") describes temporary residence without citizenship—a picture early Christians used of their relationship to the world. The verb ἐτυμπανίσθησαν (v. 35) refers to torture on a "tympanon"—an instrument where the body was stretched and beaten. This is often linked to the martyrs during the persecution under Antiochus IV Epiphanes described in 2 Maccabees.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The reference to those who "were sawn in two" (ἐπρίσθησαν, v. 37) corresponds with Jewish tradition concerning the death of the prophet Isaiah, preserved in the pseudepigraphal work "The Ascension of Isaiah" (likely 1st–2nd century CE). The description of believers who wandered "in sheepskins and goatskins" (v. 37) mirrors accounts about the prophets Elijah and Elisha. The mention of caves and holes in the ground (v. 38) finds a parallel in the persecutions of the Maccabean period, when Jewish resistance fighters hid in caves in the Judean wilderness—something attested by archaeological discoveries from caves in Judea (including the Qumran area).
