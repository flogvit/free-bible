# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 27

## People

**The Daughters of Zelophehad** – Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah – represent a rare case in the Old Testament where women are named and act as legal agents. Their father Zelophehad belonged to the tribe of Manasseh through the lineage Manasseh-Machir-Gilead-Hepher. That he "died for his own sin" (v. 3) without having participated in Korah’s rebellion suggests that he died as part of the wilderness generation condemned not to enter the land. The daughters’ bold action before the entire assembly was unusual in a culture where women rarely spoke in public gatherings.

**Joshua, son of Nun** is formally introduced here as Moses’ successor. He had already served as Moses’ assistant and military leader (Exodus 17; Numbers 11:28) and was one of the two spies who gave a positive report (Numbers 13–14). The description of him as "a man in whom is the Spirit" (v. 18) points to his spiritual equipage for the leadership role.

## Places

**Mount Abarim** (הַר הָעֲבָרִים) was a mountain range east of the Jordan, where Mount Nebo was the highest point with a view over the Promised Land. The name means "mountains on the other side" or "crossings," from the perspective of Canaan. From there Moses could see the land he was not allowed to enter. Archaeological investigations have identified this area in present-day Jordan, overlooking the plain of Jericho and the Jordan Valley.

## Events and Customs

**Inheritance rights in the ancient Near East** normally followed male lines. The case of Zelophehad’s daughters was groundbreaking because it challenged this norm. Interestingly, parallels exist in Mesopotamian legal texts from Nuzi (ca. 1500–1400 BC), where daughters could inherit under certain circumstances when there were no sons. God’s decision in this case established a legal precedent that was recorded as permanent law in Israel.

**Laying on of hands** (סָמַךְ, *samakh*) at the installation of Joshua was a ritual act that transferred authority and blessing. This custom became foundational for later ordination rituals in Jewish tradition (*semikha*) and has parallels in the ancient Near East where kings and officials were formally installed through physical actions symbolizing the transfer of power.

## Key Terms

**"Urim"** (הָאוּרִים) is mentioned in verse 21 as a means to seek God’s will. These were likely sacred objects (often mentioned together with Thummim) that the high priest carried in the breastpiece, used to receive divine guidance in important decisions. The exact method of use is unknown, but it represented an oracular system intended to compensate for the fact that Joshua, unlike Moses, did not speak with God "face to face."

**"Your dignity/glory"** (מֵהוֹדְךָ, *mehod'kha*) in verse 20 describes the transfer of Moses’ authority to Joshua. The Hebrew word *hod* means majesty or glory and suggests that Joshua received part of, but not all of, Moses’ unique position. Rabbinic tradition commented that Moses’ face was like the sun, while Joshua’s was like the moon.
