# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 1

## People

**Theophilus** (Θεόφιλε) is the recipient of both the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles. The name means "friend of God" or "loved by God." The title "most excellent" (κράτιστε) used for him in Luke 1:3 suggests he may have been a high-ranking Roman official or a wealthy patron. Some scholars believe the name could be symbolic, representing all Christian readers.

**Two men in white clothes** (ἄνδρες δύο... ἐν ἐσθήσεσι λευκαῖς, v. 10) are heavenly messengers who announce Jesus' return to the Galilean disciples. White garments were associated with heavenly beings in Jewish and early Christian tradition (cf. Luke 24:4).

**Mary, the mother of Jesus** (Μαριὰμ τῇ μητρὶ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ) and **Jesus' brothers** (τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς αὐτοῦ) are mentioned among those gathered in prayer (v. 14). This is the last time Mary is mentioned in the New Testament.

**Matthias** (Μαθθίαν) and **Joseph Barsabbas** (Ἰωσὴφ τὸν καλούμενον Βαρσαββᾶν, ὃς ἐπεκλήθη Ἰοῦστος) are the two candidates proposed to replace Judas (v. 23). Matthias' name is a short form of Mattithiah, meaning "gift of the Lord." Joseph also had the Latin surname Justus ("the righteous"), which was common among Jews in the diaspora. The fact that both had followed Jesus from John's baptism shows that the group of disciples was larger than the twelve.

## Places

**The Mount of Olives** (ὄρος τοῦ καλουμένου Ἐλαιῶνος) lies east of Jerusalem, separated from the city by the Kidron Valley. Luke specifies that the distance was "a Sabbath day's journey" (σαββάτου ἔχον ὁδόν), which according to rabbinic tradition was about 2,000 cubits (approximately 880 meters). This was the maximum distance a Jew could walk on the Sabbath without breaking the Sabbath command. The Mount of Olives had messianic significance in Jewish tradition, and the angels' promise of Jesus' return (v. 11) may be connected to this expectation.

**The upper room** (τὸ ὑπερῷον) was a room on the top floor, typical for wealthy homes in Jerusalem. Such rooms were often used for prayer, study, and religious gatherings because they provided privacy and quiet. It may be the same room used at the Last Supper (Luke 22:12).

**Hakeldama** (Ἁκελδαμάχ) is Aramaic for "Field of Blood" (v. 19). Tradition places this field in the Valley of Hinnom, south of Jerusalem. The area was already associated with death and judgment in Jewish consciousness due to idolatry that had taken place there in Old Testament times (Jer 7:31-32).

## Events and Customs

**The forty days** (διʼ ἡμερῶν τεσσεράκοντα) between the resurrection and the ascension have symbolic significance in biblical tradition. The number forty often marks periods of preparation and testing: Moses on Sinai (Exod 24:18), Elijah's journey to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8), and Jesus' temptation in the wilderness. This period gave the apostles time for teaching and confirmation of the resurrection through many "convincing proofs" (τεκμήριοις).

**The casting of lots** (ἔδωκαν κλήρους) to choose Matthias followed an ancient Israelite practice for seeking God's will (Prov 16:33; Lev 16:8). The method could involve stones or sticks with names that were cast or drawn from a container. This is the last time casting lots is mentioned in the New Testament – after Pentecost, we see the church being led by the Holy Spirit in other ways.

**The number 120** (ὡς ἑκατὸν εἴκοσι, v. 15) is remarkable because Jewish tradition (Mishnah, Sanhedrin 1:6) required a minimum of 120 men to establish a local council (sanhedrin) in a city. This may suggest that the early church was already organized as a legitimate community within a Jewish framework.

## Key Terms

**"The kingdom for Israel"** (τὴν βασιλείαν τῷ Ἰσραήλ, v. 6) reflects the disciples' continued expectation of a political messianic kingdom. The verb ἀποκαθιστάνεις ("restore") was used regarding national restoration in Jewish hope. Jesus' answer does not deny the coming of the kingdom but redirects the focus from timing and politics to mission and power.

**"Witnesses"** (μάρτυρες, v. 8) is the Greek word that later gave rise to "martyr." In a legal context, it meant an eyewitness who could confirm facts. For the apostles, this meant being eyewitnesses to Jesus' life, death, and resurrection – a fundamental criterion for apostolic authority (v. 21-22).

**"Service and apostleship"** (τῆς διακονίας ταύτης καὶ ἀποστολῆς, v. 25) combines two central concepts. Διακονία denotes service, while ἀποστολή (from ἀποστέλλω, "to send out") describes the mission as an envoy with authority from the sender. The concept had parallels in the Jewish shaliach institution, where an envoy acted with full authority on behalf of the sender.
