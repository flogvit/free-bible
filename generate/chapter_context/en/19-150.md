## Places

The psalm mentions "his sanctuary" (בְּקָדְשׁוֹ, *beqodsho*) and "the expanse of his might" (בִּרְקִיעַ עֻזּוֹ, *birqia uzzu*). The sanctuary likely refers to the temple in Jerusalem, the central place of worship in Israel. The phrase "the expanse of his might" points to heaven as God’s cosmic dwelling. This parallelism connects earthly and heavenly worship—the praise in the temple reflects the eternal praise in God’s heavenly throne room.

## Events and Customs

The psalm offers a detailed glimpse into the practices of temple music by mentioning a variety of instruments. Wind instruments include the shofar (ram’s horn), which was used during festivals and ceremonies. Stringed instruments include the nevel (harp) and kinnor (lyre). Percussion includes the tof (hand drum) and tsiltselim (cymbals) in two varieties—sounding cymbals and clashing cymbals. In addition, machol (dance) is mentioned as part of worship, along with ugav, likely a flute or wind instrument.

## Key Terms

The imperative "hallelu" (הַלְלוּ/הַלְלוּהוּ) appears ten times in this short psalm, in addition to "hallelujah" (הַלְלוּ יָהּ) in verses 1 and 6. This creates an intense liturgical rhythm. The verb is in the plural imperative form and invites the entire congregation to participate. The closing phrase "kol haneshama" (כֹּל הַנְּשָׁמָה, "everything that has breath") extends praise from the temple to include all living beings. The word *neshama* (breath, life-giving spirit) connects to the creation account where God breathes the breath of life into humanity (Gen 2:7), suggesting that even the very breath of life is meant for praise.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries confirm the instruments mentioned in the psalm. Bronze cymbals and depictions of lyre players have been found at excavations in Israel. A relief from Nineveh shows Jewish captives with lyres, confirming the role of these instruments in Israelite tradition.
