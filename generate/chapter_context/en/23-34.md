## Places

### Edom and Bozrah

The chapter directs a specific prophecy of judgment against Edom (vv. 5–6), with Bozrah (בָּצְרָה) mentioned as a central location for God’s judgment. Edom was a kingdom southeast of the Dead Sea, in the mountainous region that today constitutes southern Jordan. Bozrah was one of Edom’s most important cities, likely identical to present-day Buseirah, about 50 km north of Petra. Archaeological excavations have uncovered significant Iron Age ruins at the site, including fortifications and administrative buildings that testify to the city’s former glory.

The relationship between Israel and Edom was marked by long-standing hostility, which forms the background for the intense prophecy of judgment in this chapter.

## Events and Customs

### Sacrificial Terminology and Holy War

The chapter employs extensive sacrificial terminology to describe God’s judgment: "a sacrifice" (זֶבַח) in Bozrah, with blood from "rams and goats" (כָּרִים וְעַתּוּדִים) and "wild oxen" (רְאֵמִים, vv. 6–7). This language links political-military judgment to cultic practice, portraying the destruction of enemies as an offering to God. The Hebrew word חֶרֶם ("herem", vv. 2, 5) denotes something devoted to total destruction—a concept known from the holy war tradition, where spoils of war were to be destroyed as a gift to the deity.

## Key Concepts

### Cosmic Dissolution

Verse 4 describes how "all the host of heaven shall wear away" and the heavens will be "rolled up like a scroll". The expression צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם (host of heaven) refers to the stars, which in the ancient Near East were often worshipped as deities. The image of the heavens being rolled up like a scroll reflects the conception of the sky as an outstretched tent or skin—a familiar motif in Old Testament cosmology.

### Lilit and Desert Demons

Verse 14 mentions לִילִית (lilit), a being associated in Mesopotamian folklore with night and dangerous spirits. This is the only occurrence of the word in the Bible. In Akkadian texts, related beings called *lilītu* appear. The context places lilit among other desert-dwelling creatures such as צִיִּים (desert beasts) and שָׂעִיר (often translated "goat demons" or "wild goats"), together painting a picture of total civilizational collapse in which demonic beings take over human settlements.

### Tohu and Bohu

Verse 11 uses the expressions קַו־תֹהוּ (measuring line of chaos) and אַבְנֵי־בֹהוּ (stones of emptiness). The word pair תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ otherwise occurs only in Genesis 1:2, describing the state of the earth before creation. By employing these creation terms in a context of judgment, the prophet portrays Edom’s fate as an "un-creation"—a return to the chaotic condition that existed before God ordered the world.
