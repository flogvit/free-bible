# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 2

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical locations that form an important framework for understanding. **Kittim** (v. 10) refers to Cyprus and the western Mediterranean islands, while **Kedar** represents the Arab nomadic tribes in the east. Together they mark the extremes of the known world—from west to east—and are used rhetorically to illustrate that no other nation has exchanged their gods as Israel has done.

**Noph** (Memphis) and **Tahpanhes** (v. 16) were important Egyptian cities. Memphis was Egypt's ancient capital, located south of the Nile Delta at the transition to Upper Egypt, while Tahpanhes (Greek: Daphne) was a border fortress in northeastern Egypt. Both places represent the Egyptian threat and Judah's futile attempts to seek military support there. **Shihor** (v. 18) denotes either a branch of the Nile or the Nile itself, while "the River" (נָהָר) refers to the Euphrates and thus Assyria.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains extensive criticism of Baal worship that had taken root in Judah. The expression "on every high hill and under every green tree" (v. 20) describes the typical cult sites for Canaanite fertility worship. These **high places** (במות) were often situated on natural elevations with sacred trees or pillars (Asherahs), where rituals connected to rain and fertility were performed.

The accusation that the people say to **the tree, "You are my father," and to the stone, "You gave me birth"** (v. 27) reflects Canaanite practice in which wooden images (Asherahs) and stone pillars (masseboth) functioned as cult objects. This represented a complete reversal of the Creator-creature relationship—the people worshiped what was created rather than the Creator.

## Key Terms

**Chesed** (חֶסֶד, v. 2) is often translated "steadfast love" or "mercy" and denotes covenant loyalty. Here it refers to Israel's original devotion to the Lord in the wilderness—a picture of marital faithfulness.

The expression **"a fountain of living water"** (מְקוֹר מַיִם חַיִּים, v. 13) versus **"broken cisterns"** (בֹּארֹת נִשְׁבָּרִים) is a powerful image in a dry region. Living (flowing) water was far more valuable than stagnant well water. Broken cisterns—hewn out of limestone to collect rainwater—could not hold water and were worthless.

**The Shittah vine** (שֹׂרֵק, v. 21) was a noble grape variety known for its quality, likely from the Sorek Valley west of Jerusalem. The image of the noble vine becoming wild grapes illustrates Israel's degeneration.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Judah have uncovered numerous **cult objects** from this period (late 700s to early 600s BCE) that confirm the prevalence of syncretistic religion. Figurines of a female deity (likely Asherah), found in large numbers in Jerusalem and surrounding areas, illustrate the religious mixture the chapter condemns. Inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom mentioning "Yahweh and his Asherah" show that many Israelites combined Yahweh worship with Canaanite fertility religion.
