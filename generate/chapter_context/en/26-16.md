## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical and political entities that play important roles in the allegory. **Jerusalem** (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם) is personified as a woman and is the main focus of the entire chapter. The city is described with Canaanite origins – "your father was an Amorite and your mother was a Hittite" (v. 3) – pointing to Jerusalem's pre-Israelite history as a Canaanite city (Jebus of the Jebusites) before David conquered it.

**Samaria** (vv. 46, 51, 53, 55) is referred to as Jerusalem's "older sister" and represents the Northern Kingdom of Israel, which fell to the Assyrians in 722 BC. **Sodom** (vv. 46-56) is portrayed as the "younger sister" and serves as a shocking point of comparison – Jerusalem has sinned worse than that notoriously wicked city. **Egypt**, **Assyria**, and **the land of Canaan toward Chaldea** (vv. 26-29) are mentioned as Jerusalem's "lovers", referring to Judah's political alliances and religious syncretism with these nations. The **daughters of the Philistines** (vv. 27, 57) and the **daughters of Aram** (v. 57; textual variants have 'Edom') are mentioned as neighboring peoples who despised Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains several references to ancient Near Eastern customs. The description of the abandoned infant (vv. 4-6) – with the umbilical cord not cut, the child not washed, salted, or swaddled – reflects actual birth procedures in antiquity. Salting newborns was a common practice in the Middle East, likely for cleansing and skin care.

The marriage rituals in verse 8 – spreading the garment (כָּנָף, kanaf, "wing/edge") over the woman and making a covenant – mirror actual Israelite marriage practices (cf. Ruth 3:9). **Child sacrifice** (vv. 20-21) with the expression "make them pass through the fire" (הַעֲבִיר) refers to the Molek cult practiced in the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem. Archaeological finds from Carthage and other Phoenician colonies confirm such practices in Canaanite-Phoenician religion.

**High places** (בָּמוֹת, vv. 16) and **elevated sites** (רָמָה, vv. 24-25, 31, 39) were cultic installations used for idolatry, often located at crossroads and on heights. The Hebrew word **גֶּב** (vv. 24, 31, 39), often translated "vaulted house" or "brothel", likely denotes a type of cultic prostitution structure.

## Key Terms

Several Hebrew words and expressions require explanation. **תּוֹעֵבָה** (to'evah, "abomination") occurs repeatedly and is a technical term for actions that are cultically and morally detestable to God. **זָנָה** (zanah, "commit adultery") is used metaphorically throughout the chapter regarding idolatry and political alliances – a common prophetic metaphor where the covenant relationship between God and Israel is compared to marriage.

The expression **עֵרֹם וְעֶרְיָה** ('erom ve'eryah, vv. 7, 22, 39, "naked and bare") uses two synonymous words to emphasize total nakedness and vulnerability. **בְּרִית עוֹלָם** (berit 'olam, v. 60, "everlasting covenant") points forward to a future restoration that surpasses the covenant Israel broke. **כִּפֶּר** (kipper, v. 63, "atone") is an important cultic term connected with reconciliation and forgiveness.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem and Judah have uncovered numerous **figurines of the fertility goddess** (often identified as Astarte/Asherah) from the 700s BC, confirming the widespread syncretism that the chapter condemns. Finds of **cultic installations** at city gates in several Judean towns, as well as remains that may be **child sacrifices in Topheth** in the Valley of Hinnom, correspond with the text's accusations.

Assyrian and Babylonian annals document Judah's shifting alliances with Egypt, Assyria, and Babylon, which the chapter criticizes as "adultery". Letters from Lachish and other sources confirm the political instability and desperate diplomatic maneuvers in Judah's final decades.
