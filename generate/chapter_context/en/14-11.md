# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 11

## People

**Rehoboam** (רְחַבְעָם, "the people have expanded") was the son of Solomon and Naamah the Ammonite. As king over the now-reduced kingdom of Judah, he faced the enormous challenge of stabilizing a divided monarchy. His marriages to Mahalath, David's great-granddaughter through Jerimoth, and especially to Maacah, daughter (or granddaughter) of Absalom, reveal a deliberate strategy to strengthen his legitimacy through ties to the Davidic dynasty.

**Shemaiah** (שְׁמַעְיָהוּ, "the Lord has heard") is described as "a man of God" (אִישׁ־הָאֱלֹהִים), a title denoting a prophet with special authority. His prophetic intervention prevented civil war between Judah and Israel (verses 1–4).

## Places

The chapter mentions fifteen fortified cities that Rehoboam built up (verses 6–10). These were strategically located in an arc around Jerusalem from the south and west: **Bethlehem** and **Etam** to the north, **Tekoa** toward the wilderness, **Beth-shur**, **Shoco**, **Adullam**, **Gath**, **Mareshah**, and **Ziph** in the Shephelah region, **Adoraim**, **Lachish**, and **Azekah** toward the southwest, as well as **Zorah**, **Aijalon**, and **Hebron**. Notably, none of these cities lie on the northern border facing Israel, suggesting that defense was primarily directed against Egypt and the Philistines.

**Lachish** is particularly well-documented archaeologically. Excavations have uncovered massive fortifications from the Iron Age that correspond to this period of building defenses.

## Events and Customs

**The expulsion of the Levites** (verses 13–15) describes how Jeroboam removed the legitimate priests and Levites, appointing his own priests for the high places, "the goats" or "hairy demons" (שְׂעִירִים; cf. Leviticus 17:7), and the golden calves. This led to a religious refugee flow into Judah, strengthening the kingdom's spiritual identity.

**Royal marriage politics** were common in antiquity for building alliances and ensuring dynastic stability. Rehoboam's eighteen wives and sixty concubines (verse 21), along with his strategy of distributing his sons to fortified cities with ample supplies (verse 23), were deliberate measures to prevent succession disputes and secure loyalty in the provinces.

## Key Terms

**Matsur/metsura** (מָצוֹר/מְצֻרוֹת) – "fortress/fortified cities" – refers to cities with defensive walls, towers, and often double gate complexes. Such cities served as refuges for the population and storage sites for military supplies.

**Nagid** (נָגִיד) – translated as "prince" or "leader" – is used in verse 22 regarding Abijah's position. The term indicates an appointed heir or military leader, showing that Rehoboam actively planned the succession by appointing Maacah's son over older brothers.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at several of the mentioned cities have uncovered fortification structures datable to the 10th–9th centuries BCE. At **Lachish**, remains of a massive city wall and a palace-fort from this period have been found. Similar defensive structures are documented at **Azekah**. These findings support the picture of a systematic fortification campaign in Judah during the early divided monarchy.
