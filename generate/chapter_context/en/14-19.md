## People

**Jehu, son of Hanani**, is mentioned as "the seer" (Hebrew: *haḥozeh*) who confronted Jehoshaphat upon his return. His father Hanani had previously been a seer who rebuked King Asa (2 Chron 16:7). This illustrates a prophetic dynasty in which both generations served as God’s spokespersons to the kings of Judah.

**Amariah** is identified as "the chief priest" (*kohen harosh*) with responsibility for religious matters. **Zebadiah, son of Ishmael**, is described as "the prince" (*nagid*) over the house of Judah with responsibility for royal and civil affairs. This division between religious and civil jurisdiction represents a form of separation of powers in Jewish administration.

## Places

The chapter notes that Jehoshaphat traveled among the people "from Beersheba to the hill country of Ephraim" to bring them back to the Lord. Beersheba in the south and the hill country of Ephraim in the north mark the entire territory of Judah. This extends into what was traditionally the northern kingdom’s area, which may reflect Jehoshaphat’s influence or regions with a Jewish population in the border zone.

## Events and Customs

Jehoshaphat’s judicial reform represents a systematization of the legal system in Judah. He appointed judges in all fortified cities and established a central court of appeal in Jerusalem. The instructions to the judges to avoid partiality and bribery (*massa panim* – to lift the face/show partiality, and *miqqaḥ shoḥad* – to take a bribe) reflect formulations from the books of Moses (Deut 16:19) and demonstrate continuity in Israelite legal tradition.

## Key Terms

The expression "you are not judging for man but for the Lord" (v. 6) expresses a theocratic judicial principle in which the judge acts as God’s representative. The Hebrew word *qetseph* ("wrath") in verses 2 and 10 describes God’s anger as a concrete consequence of disobedience. The term *shotrim* ("officers/officials") in verse 11 refers to administrative officials who assisted the judges.
