# Historical and Cultural Context for John 1

## People

**John the Baptist** is introduced as a witness sent from God (vv. 6-8, 19-34). He was a well-known historical figure, also mentioned by the Jewish historian Josephus in *Jewish Antiquities* (18.5.2), who describes him as a righteous man who urged the Jews toward virtue and baptism. The delegation of priests and Levites from Jerusalem (v. 19) represents the religious establishment that wanted to assess whether John was a legitimate prophetic figure.

**The first disciples** are named specifically: Andrew, Simon (who receives the name Cephas/Peter), Philip, and Nathanael (vv. 40-49). Andrew and Peter came from Bethsaida (v. 44), a fishing village on the northeastern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Archaeological excavations at et-Tell, identified as possible Bethsaida, have uncovered a significant first-century fishing village with fishing gear and anchors.

## Places

**Bethany beyond the Jordan** (v. 28) was the place where John baptized. This is not the same Bethany located near Jerusalem (cf. John 11:18), but a site east of the Jordan River. The exact location is debated, but tradition points to the area around Wadi el-Kharrar in present-day Jordan, where remains of Byzantine churches and baptismal pools have been found.

**Nazareth** is mentioned with some disdain by Nathanael (v. 46). Archaeological studies show that Nazareth was a small, insignificant village during Jesus' time, with perhaps only 200-400 inhabitants. Excavations have revealed simple dwellings carved into limestone, confirming the modest character of the place and explaining Nathanael's skepticism.

## Events and Customs

**Baptism** was not unknown in Jewish tradition. Ritual cleansing in a *mikveh* (ritual bath) was common practice. Proselyte baptism was used for Gentiles who converted to Judaism. What was new with John's baptism was that it was practiced on Jews as a sign of repentance. The Pharisees' question about his authority to baptize (v. 25) shows that baptism was associated with messianic or prophetic expectations.

**To untie the sandal strap** (v. 27) was an task considered so degrading that even Jewish slaves could not be required to perform it according to rabbinic tradition. John's statement thus emphasizes extreme humility toward Jesus.

**The tenth hour** (v. 39) refers to the Jewish method of timekeeping, which began at sunrise (around 6 a.m.). The tenth hour therefore corresponds to about 4 p.m. Alternatively, if John uses Roman timekeeping (starting from midnight), it would mean around 10 a.m. Most scholars assume Jewish timekeeping.

**Under the fig tree** (v. 48) – The fig tree provided good shade and was often used as a place for quiet meditation and study of Scripture. Rabbinic tradition refers to studying under the fig tree as an image of piety. Jesus' knowledge that Nathanael had been under the fig tree suggests supernatural insight.

## Key Terms

**Logos** (ὁ λόγος, "the Word") in vv. 1-14 is a key term that would resonate with several cultural contexts. For Jewish readers, it could recall God's creative word in Genesis 1 and wisdom in Proverbs 8. For readers familiar with Greek philosophy, especially Stoic thought, *logos* was a concept for the divine principle of reason permeating the cosmos. John uses the term uniquely by identifying the *logos* as a person who became flesh.

**Messiah/Christ** (v. 41) – The text gives the Aramaic term *Messias* (מְשִׁיחָא) and translates it into Greek (*Christos*). Both mean "the anointed one" and point to the expectation of a coming deliverer and king from David's line.

**Rabbi** (vv. 38, 49) was an honorific title that literally means "my great one" or "my master." In Jesus' time, this was a respectful form of address for teachers, although the formal rabbinic system was only established after the destruction of the temple in 70 AD.

**Lamb of God** (ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, vv. 29, 36) would evoke associations for Jewish listeners with the Passover lamb (Exodus 12), the daily morning and evening offering (*tamid*, Exodus 29:38-42), and possibly to the servant in Isaiah 53:7 who was "led like a lamb to the slaughter." The image combines the concept of sacrifice with substitutionary suffering.

**Jacob's ladder** (v. 51) – Jesus' words about angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man allude directly to Genesis 28:12, where Jacob sees a ladder between heaven and earth with God's angels. Jesus identifies himself as the new meeting point between heaven and earth, symbolically replacing Bethel ("house of God") as the place of divine revelation.
