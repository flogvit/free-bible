# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 50

## Key Concepts

### Bill of Divorce and Debt Slavery (v. 1)
The chapter opens with two legal images from Israelite law that God uses rhetorically. **סֵפֶר כְּרִיתוּת** (séfer kerītūt, "bill of divorce") refers to the document mentioned in Deuteronomy 24:1-4 that a man was required to give his wife upon divorce. The second image concerns selling children to creditors to cover debts, a practice regulated in Exodus 21:7 and 2 Kings 4:1. God's point is that Israel chose exile through its own sin—God has neither formally rejected them nor sold them to others.

### The Servant Figure and "Limmudim" (vv. 4-9)
This passage is traditionally regarded as the third of four "Servant of the Lord" songs in Isaiah. The Hebrew **לְשׁוֹן לִמּוּדִים** (leshōn limmūdīm) means "tongue of disciples" or "tongue of the learned" and describes one who is trained to speak God's words. The expression **יָעִיר לִי אֹזֶן** ("he awakens my ear") uses a Hebrew idiom for being attentive and receptive—an image of daily revelation and guidance from God.

### Physical Abuse (v. 6)
The description of blows to the back, having one's beard plucked out, and being spat upon represents extreme shame in the ancient Near East. Plucking someone's beard was a serious insult (cf. Nehemiah 13:25), and spitting in the face was the deepest form of contempt (Numbers 12:14; Deuteronomy 25:9). The servant willingly accepts this disgrace.

### Flintstone and Legal Dispute (vv. 7-8)
**חַלָּמִישׁ** (ḥallāmīsh, "flintstone") was known as a very hard type of stone, used for knives and tools. The legal language in vv. 8-9 ("who will contend with me," "let us stand up together") reflects Israelite judicial practice where the parties stood face to face before the judge at the city gate.

## Events and Customs

### Fire and Torches as Self-Reliance (v. 11)
The closing verses use the image of people kindling their own lights (**קֹדְחֵי אֵשׁ**, "kindlers of fire") and walking in the glow of their own torches (**זִיקוֹת**, zīqōt). This contrasts with those who walk in darkness but trust in the Lord (v. 10). The image may refer to the use of torches in religious rituals or simply to human self-confidence—trusting in one's own wisdom rather than God's light.
