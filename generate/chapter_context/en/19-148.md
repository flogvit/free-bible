## Key Concepts

Psalm 148 contains several Hebrew expressions with deep theological significance. The word **צְבָאָיו** (tseva'av, "his hosts") in verse 2 refers to heavenly beings and is also used of the stars as an organized army under God's command. The expression **שְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם** (shemei hashamayim, "the heavens of heavens") in verse 4 reflects the ancient Israelite concept of multiple heavenly spheres, with the highest being God's dwelling place. **הַמַּיִם אֲשֶׁר מֵעַל הַשָּׁמָיִם** ("the waters above the heavens") points back to the creation account in Genesis 1:7, where God separated the waters with the firmament.

The term **תַּנִּינִים** (tanninim, "sea monsters/dragons") in verse 7 denotes mythical sea creatures that in surrounding cultures often represented chaotic forces. In Israelite theology, these are subject to God and are called upon to praise him. The psalm's conclusion uses the expression **קֶרֶן** (qeren, "horn") in verse 14, an image of strength and power. That God "lifts up the horn" for his people means that he grants them honor and victory. The designation **עַם־קְרֹבוֹ** ("the people who are near him") emphasizes Israel's unique covenantal position.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's structure follows a cosmic pattern moving from heaven (verses 1–6) to earth (verses 7–14), reflecting the Israelite worldview. This literary form has parallels in Egyptian and Mesopotamian hymns where all creation is called as witnesses, but with a crucial difference: In Israelite tradition, creation praises the Creator, not deified natural forces. The list of creatures—from angels to reptiles, from kings to young people—constitutes a complete catalog of existence and was likely used in temple praise liturgies, possibly during major festivals where all creation was symbolically represented.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The psalm's universal perspective—in which all peoples and kings are called to praise—reflects a theology in which Yahweh is understood as the God of the whole world. The text's similarities with the Song of the Three Young Men (an addition to Daniel 3 in the Greek translation) show how this cosmic tradition of praise continued to develop in Jewish literature.
