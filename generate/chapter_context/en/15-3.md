# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezra 3

## People

**Jeshua (Joshua) son of Jozadak** was the high priest and belonged to the Zadokite priestly line. His father, Jozadak, had been taken into exile by Nebuchadnezzar (1 Chron 5:41). Jeshua represented the restoration of the legitimate priesthood and is also mentioned in the books of the prophets Haggai and Zechariah as a central figure in the rebuilding effort.

**Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel** was of Davidic descent (1 Chron 3:17–19). His name is Babylonian (*Zēr-Bābili*, "Offspring of Babylon"), indicating that he was born during the exile. Together with Jeshua, he represented the dual leadership—political and religious—that characterized this period.

**Kadmiel and the sons of Judah**, as well as **the sons of Henadad**, are mentioned as Levites who oversaw the temple work (v. 9). They formed a unified Levitical workforce.

## Events and Customs

**The seventh month (Tishri)** was the most important festival month in the Jewish calendar. It included the New Year’s feast (Rosh Hashanah), the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), and the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot). The fact that the people gathered specifically during this month underscores their religious priorities—they resumed sacrifices before the temple was built, showing that continuity in worship was more important than having a completed building.

**The Feast of Tabernacles (חַג הַסֻּכּוֹת)** was celebrated according to the regulations in Num 29:12–38, with specific daily offerings. This feast held special significance as a pilgrimage festival and recalled the wilderness wanderings, which resonated deeply with a people who had just returned from exile.

**The foundation-laying ceremony** included elements reminiscent of Solomon’s temple dedication (2 Chron 5–7), with priests in their official garments, trumpets, and Levites from the line of Asaph playing cymbals. The praise "For he is good; his steadfast love endures forever over Israel" (כִּי טוֹב כִּי־לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ עַל־יִשְׂרָאֵל) was a liturgical formula known from the Psalms (Ps 136) and temple worship.

**The differing reactions of the generations** are poignantly described in vv. 12–13: The older ones who had seen the first temple wept aloud, while others shouted for joy. The sounds mingled so that one could not distinguish the shouts of joy from the cries of weeping. This reflects the theological tension between gratitude for the rebuilding and sorrow that the new temple could not compare with the first.

## Key Terms

**The altar on its old foundation (עַל־מְכוֹנֹתָיו)**—the expression literally means "on its foundations" and indicates that the new altar was placed exactly where the original one had stood. This was theologically crucial to ensure continuity with pre-exilic worship and legitimacy for the renewed cult.

**Morning and evening (לַבֹּקֶר וְלָעָרֶב)** refers to the daily burnt offering (*tamid*), which according to Exod 29:38–42 was to be offered regularly. The resumption of this sacrifice marked the restoration of Israel’s covenant relationship with God.

**Fear of the peoples in the lands (בְּאֵימָה עֲלֵיהֶם מֵעַמֵּי הָאֲרָצוֹת)** (v. 3) suggests that the returned Jews felt threatened by surrounding nations. Nevertheless, they built the altar—their fear drove them to God rather than away from Him.

## Places

**Sidon, Tyre, and Lebanon**—cedar trees from Lebanon were transported by sea route to **Joppa (Jaffa)**, exactly the same trade route used during Solomon’s temple construction (2 Chron 2:15–16). The Phoenicians from Sidon and Tyre were known as skilled carpenters and merchants, and payment in kind (food, drink, and oil) was common practice in ancient commerce. That Persian permission (רִשְׁיוֹן, *risyjon*) was required shows that this was an officially sanctioned activity under Cyrus’s decree.
