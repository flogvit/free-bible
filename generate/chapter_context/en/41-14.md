## People

**Simon the Leper** (v. 3) was the host of the meal in Bethany. The nickname "leper" (Greek: λεπροῦ) likely indicates that he had previously suffered from a skin disease, possibly healed by Jesus himself. His ability to host a meal shows that he was no longer ritually unclean. The woman who anointed Jesus is not named in Mark. The spikenard ointment she used (μύρον νάρδου πιστικῆς) was imported from India and represented approximately a year's wages for a laborer (300 denarii).

**The High Priest** (ὁ ἀρχιερεύς, v. 53, 60–63) led the trial against Jesus. He is not named in Mark, but based on chronology this was Caiaphas, who according to Josephus served as high priest from about 18–36 AD. In 1990, archaeologists discovered a bone ossuary in Jerusalem with the inscription "Joseph son of Caiaphas," which very likely belonged to this high priest's family.

## Places

**Bethany** (Βηθανία, v. 3) was located on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, about 3 km from Jerusalem. The village served as Jesus' base during Holy Week, outside the overcrowded city. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of a first-century village with several houses and tombs.

**Gethsemane** (Γεθσημανί, v. 32) means "oil press" in Aramaic (gat shemanim). The place was located at the foot of the Mount of Olives, likely in an olive grove where oil was produced. The current Garden of Gethsemane contains olive trees that may be descendants of those standing there in antiquity. The area has been identified with reasonable certainty based on early Christian traditions and topographical descriptions.

**The High Priest's Courtyard** (αὐλή, v. 54) where the trial took place was probably located in the wealthy Upper City area of Jerusalem. Excavations beneath the current "Church of St. Peter in Gallicantu" have uncovered a rich Palestinian house from the first century with a dungeon and flogging area, but this identification is disputed.

## Events and Customs

**Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread** (τὸ πάσχα καὶ τὰ ἄζυμα, v. 1) was the most important Jewish festival marking the exodus from Egypt. The Passover lamb was slaughtered on the afternoon of Nisan 14, and the meal was eaten after sunset (the beginning of Nisan 15). The festival drew enormous crowds to Jerusalem.

**The Anointing** (v. 3–9) has both hospitable and messianic overtones. The alabaster jar had to be broken because its neck was sealed to preserve the quality of the perfume. Jesus interprets the action as preparation for burial (ἐνταφιασμός), which was unusual since anointing normally took place after death.

**The Passover Meal** (v. 12–25) followed a set liturgy with four cups of wine, unleavened bread, bitter herbs, and the lamb. The Greek word ἀνάγαιον (v. 15) refers to a large room on an upper floor, "furnished" (ἐστρωμένον) with dining couches. Reclining at table (ἀνακειμένων, v. 18) was standard for festive meals, in contrast to the hurried standing meal of the first Passover.

## Key Terms

**Abba** (Αββα, v. 36) is the Aramaic word for "father" and represents one of the few Aramaic words preserved in the New Testament. Its use shows an intimate yet respectful form of address that was unusual in Jewish prayer practice. Mark immediately translates it with the Greek ὁ πατήρ for his non-Jewish readers.

**Sanhedrin** (συνέδριον, v. 55) was the Jewish council consisting of 71 members under the leadership of the high priest. According to later Jewish law (Mishnah), nighttime trials in capital cases were problematic, but whether these rules applied during Jesus' time is disputed.

**Blasphemy** (βλασφημία, v. 64) refers to Jesus' claim to be the Messiah and the Son of Man who will sit at God's right hand. For the high priest this constituted an improper appropriation of divine authority, especially the combination of Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13 which Jesus applies to himself.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The ossuary of Caiaphas' family, discovered in 1990 in a tomb cave south of Jerusalem, contained bone remains of a man in his sixties. The urn is richly decorated, indicating the family's high status. This find provides a direct archaeological link to the participants in Jesus' passion narrative.
