# Historical and Cultural Context for Philippians 2

## People

**Timothy** (vv. 19-23) was Paul’s most trusted coworker, a young man with a Jewish mother and Greek father from Lystra in Asia Minor. He had been with Paul since the second missionary journey and knew the church in Philippi well, having been present at its founding (Acts 16:1-3, 12). Paul describes their relationship as that of father and son, reflecting the Greco-Roman custom of spiritual mentorship.

**Epaphroditus** (vv. 25-30) was the messenger sent by the Philippians who had brought financial support to Paul while he was in prison. His name is derived from the goddess Aphrodite and was common in the Greco-Roman world. His serious illness during his stay with Paul, and the church’s concern when they heard about it, illustrate the real communication challenges of antiquity—news took weeks to travel between Rome and Philippi along the Via Egnatia.

## Key Terms

**Μορφή (morphē)** – "form" or "shape" (vv. 6-7): This Greek word denotes not merely outward appearance, but the essential, inner form that expresses a being’s true nature. When Paul says Christ was "in the form of God," he makes a claim about Christ’s divine essence, not just his appearance. The transition to "the form of a servant" therefore marks a radical change in status.

**Ἁρπαγμός (harpagmos)** – "something to be grasped" or "something to hold onto" (v. 6): This rare word can mean either something one strives to seize or something one already holds and clings to. In the context of Greco-Roman culture, where gods and emperors demanded honor and worship, the contrast is striking: Christ chose to relinquish his rights rather than exploit them.

**Κένωσις (from ἐκένωσεν)** – "to empty himself" (v. 7): This expression has given its name to "kenosis theology." In antiquity, voluntary renunciation of status was almost unthinkable—honor and position were defended at all costs. Christ’s self-humbling was radically countercultural.

**Σπένδομαι (spendomai)** – "poured out as a drink offering" (v. 17): This is cultic language drawn from both Jewish and Greco-Roman sacrificial practices. The drink offering was wine poured over the burnt offering as a concluding act. Paul views his potential martyrdom as an addition to the Philippians’ own sacrifice of faith.

## Events and Customs

**Crucifixion** (v. 8): When Paul specifies "death on a cross," he emphasizes the most degrading form of execution in the Roman Empire. Crucifixion was reserved for slaves, rebels, and the lowest criminals—never for Roman citizens. For listeners in a Roman colony like Philippi, where many were veterans or descendants of veterans, the cross was a symbol of shame and a display of power. That Christ voluntarily chose this death turned all social values upside down.

**Imperial Cult and Universal Worship** (vv. 9-11): The language that "every knee should bow" and "every tongue confess" has clear parallels with the imperial cult, which was especially prominent in Roman colonies like Philippi. Inscriptions from the period show that the emperor was honored with titles such as "Lord" (κύριος) and "Savior." Paul’s application of Isaiah 45:23 to Jesus was both a theological claim and an implicit political challenge.

**Λειτουργία (leitourgia)** – "service" or "ministry" (vv. 17, 30): This word originally referred to public duties performed by wealthy citizens for the community—financing festivals, ships, or building projects. In a religious context, it was used of priestly service. When Paul uses it to describe both the Philippians’ faith and Epaphroditus’ mission, he elevates their practical service to a sacred act.
