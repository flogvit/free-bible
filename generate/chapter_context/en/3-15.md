# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 15

## Events and Customs

This chapter deals with ritual impurity related to bodily discharges—both normal (such as menstruation and seminal emission) and abnormal (such as prolonged discharges in men and women). In the Old Testament world, such conditions were not primarily a matter of hygiene or sin, but of cultic purity that affected one’s ability to approach the sanctuary. The chapter clearly distinguishes between short-term impurity (lasting until evening) and longer periods (seven days or more), depending on the nature of the discharge.

Similar purity laws concerning bodily discharges are found in Mesopotamian texts, while Egyptian sources also show concern for ritual purity. What sets Israelite practice apart is its systematic connection to the tabernacle and the idea that God’s holy dwelling among His people (v. 31) required impurity to be handled correctly.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **זָב** (*zāv*) refers to a man with an abnormal discharge, while **זוֹב** (*zōv*) is the discharge itself. The exact medical nature of this discharge is uncertain, but the text focuses on cultic status rather than diagnosis. The term **נִדָּה** (*niddāh*) is used for menstruation and the period of impurity associated with it. The word is linked to the root נדד, which can mean "to remove" or "to separate," and describes both the physical condition and the cultic status.

The expression **מַיִם חַיִּים** (*mayim ḥayyim*, "living water") in verse 13 refers to flowing water from a spring or stream, as opposed to stagnant water. This was considered more effective for ritual cleansing. The phrase **טָמֵא עַד־הָעָרֶב** ("unclean until evening") marks the shortest form of impurity, where a new day at sunset restored purity after washing.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of *mikvaot* (ritual cleansing baths) from later periods can illustrate how the purity laws were practiced in reality. Although the oldest finds of mikvaot date to the Hasmonean period (around 150 B.C.), they show continuity with biblical requirements for full-body washing in water. The requirement that clay vessels must be broken while wooden objects could be rinsed (v. 12) reflects the understanding that porous clay permanently absorbs impurity, whereas wood could be cleansed.
