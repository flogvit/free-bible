## Places

Jeremiah 48 contains an unusually detailed geographical catalog of Moabite cities and places. **Nebo** (vv. 1, 22) was both a mountain and a city in Moab. Mount Nebo is known from the books of Moses as the place where Moses saw the Promised Land, while the city of Nebo mentioned here was a Moabite settlement area. **Kirjathaim** (vv. 1, 23) is identified with Khirbet el-Qureiyat, a city also mentioned in the Mesha Stele. **Heshbon** (vv. 2, 34, 45) was an important city in northern Moab, originally an Amorite city under King Sihon. **Horonaim** (vv. 3, 5, 34) likely lay in southern Moab and is also mentioned in the Mesha Stele. **Dibon** (vv. 18, 22), modern Dhiban, was Moab's capital and the place where the Mesha Stele was found. **Aroer** (v. 19) lay by the Arnon Valley and marked Moab's southern border. **Arnon** (v. 20) was the river that formed a natural boundary between Moab and the regions to the north. Many of these cities – including **Sibmah** (v. 32), **Jazer** (v. 32), **Elealeh** (v. 34) and **the waters of Nimrim** (v. 34) – were known for their wine production.

## Key Concepts

**Chemosh** (כְּמוֹשׁ, vv. 7, 13, 46) was Moab's national god. The Mesha Stele confirms his central role in Moabite religion, where King Mesha attributes his victories to Chemosh. The prophecy that Chemosh will go into exile along with his priests (v. 7) reflects the ancient practice whereby conquerors took the gods' images of the defeated as war booty.

The wine metaphor in verses 11–12 uses the term **dregs/lees** (שְׁמָרָיו). This refers to the wine production process where wine left on the dregs without being poured into new vessels retained its flavor but could become too strong. The metaphor describes Moab's undisturbed existence without experience of exile.

The **mourning rituals** described in verse 37 – shaved head, cut-off beard, scratched hands and sackcloth – were pagan mourning customs forbidden by Israelite law (Lev 19:27–28), but widespread in Moab and surrounding cultures.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Mesha Stele** (also called the Moabite Stone), found in Dibon in 1868 and dated to about 840 BC, is directly relevant to this chapter. The inscription mentions several of the same cities as Jeremiah 48: Dibon, Aroer, Nebo, Kirjathaim, Horonaim and Medeba. The stele describes King Mesha's military victories and building projects, offering insight into Moabite self-understanding and Chemosh worship. It confirms that in the 800s BC, Moab was a prosperous and independent nation – precisely the condition verse 11 describes with the metaphor of wine left undisturbed. The parallels between place names in Jeremiah 48 and the Mesha Stele strengthen the chapter's historical grounding.
