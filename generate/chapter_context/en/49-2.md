# Historical and Cultural Context for Ephesians 2

## Events and Customs

The chapter mentions the division between the circumcised and uncircumcised (v. 11), reflecting one of the most fundamental social and religious boundaries in antiquity. Jews used the terms *ἀκροβυστία* (uncircumcision/uncircumcised) and *περιτομή* (circumcision) as identity markers. For Jews, circumcision was a covenant sign dating back to Abraham, while Gentiles were regarded as ritual outsiders. This terminology could be derogatory when applied to "the others," which Paul hints at by writing "those who are called"—he indicates that these are human designations.

The expression "citizenship of Israel" (*πολιτεία*, v. 12) draws language from the social structure of the Greco-Roman city-state. Citizenship granted rights, protection, and belonging. For Gentiles in Ephesus, a city where citizenship was highly valued, this imagery would have been immediately understandable. To be a "foreigner" (*ξένος*) and "stranger" (*πάροικος*) meant living without legal protection or social belonging—a marginalized existence many in the congregation knew from personal experience.

## Key Terms

The phrase "ἄρχοντα τῆς ἐξουσίας τοῦ ἀέρος" (the ruler of the power of the air, v. 2) reflects a widespread ancient belief that evil spiritual powers dwelt in the space between earth and heaven. Both Jewish apocalyptic literature and Greco-Roman folklore placed demons and evil spirits in this sphere. For readers in Ephesus, where magical practices and spirit worship were very common, this reference would have carried strong resonance.

The Greek word *μεσότοιχον* (dividing wall, v. 14) likely has a concrete reference to the barrier in the Jerusalem Temple that separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner areas. This wall bore inscriptions warning non-Jews of death if they proceeded further. Archaeologists have discovered two such warning signs, one in 1871 and another in 1935, containing the Greek text forbidding Gentiles to pass under threat of death. Paul uses this as an image of the deeper spiritual separation that Christ has removed.

The term *ἀκρογωνιαῖος* (cornerstone, v. 20) refers either to the foundation stone by which the entire structure was oriented or to the capstone that completed the construction. In ancient building practices, the cornerstone was crucial for a building's stability and structure. Archaeological finds from the Jerusalem Temple and other monumental buildings show how massive and precisely fitted such stones had to be.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The mentioned warning signs from the Jerusalem Temple are particularly relevant to this chapter. The complete inscription in Greek reads: "No foreigner may enter within the fence and barrier around the sanctuary. Whoever is caught will be responsible for their own death." This physical barrier, which Paul likely saw many times himself, becomes in Ephesians 2 a powerful symbol of the hostility between Jews and Gentiles that Christ has broken down. It is historically remarkable that Paul was later accused of bringing a Gentile past exactly this barrier (Acts 21:28–29).
