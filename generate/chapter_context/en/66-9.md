## Places

### The River Euphrates
Euphrates (Εὐφράτῃ, v. 14) was the great river that formed the eastern border of the Roman Empire against the Parthian Empire. For John’s contemporary readers, the Euphrates represented a real geopolitical threat—the Parthians were the only people who had inflicted significant military defeats on the Romans. Fear of an invasion from the east was widespread in the first century. In Old Testament tradition, the Euphrates marked the boundary of the promised land (Gen 15:18) and served as the route for God’s punishing armies from Assyria and Babylon (Isa 7:20; Jer 46:10). The fact that four angels are held bound at this river links apocalyptic judgment to a concrete geographical point charged with both political fear and biblical symbolism.

## Key Terms

### Abyssos (ἄβυσσος) – the abyss
The Greek word ἄβυσσος (vv. 1, 2, 11) denotes a deep, bottomless place that functions as a prison for evil powers. In the Septuagint, it translates the Hebrew *tehom* (the deep). In Jewish apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch), the abyss was the place where fallen angels were held captive. The well with its key and the rising smoke suggest a volcanic imagery, possibly influenced by observations of volcanic activity in the Mediterranean region.

### Abaddon and Apollyon
The name is given in both Hebrew (Ἀβαδδών) and Greek (Ἀπολλύων), both meaning "destroyer" (v. 11). In Hebrew, Abaddon appears in Job 26:6 and Prov 15:11 as a designation for the realm of the dead. The Greek name Apollyon could evoke associations with the god Apollo for contemporary readers, who was the protector of Emperor Domitian. Grasshoppers were also one of Apollo’s symbols—this may represent a subtle polemic against emperor worship.

### Akrides (ἀκρίδες) – the locusts
The locusts (vv. 3–11) draw their symbolism from Joel’s plague of locusts (Joel 1–2), where the devastation caused by a swarm is described in military terms. The description combines features from various sources: horse heads (cf. Joel 2:4: "They look like horses"), women’s hair (v. 8—possibly a reference to the long hair of Parthian warriors, or part of the frightening hybrid description), lions’ teeth (Joel 1:6), and scorpion stings (life-threatening pain without killing). The five months (vv. 5, 10) correspond to the natural life cycle of locusts from spring to autumn.

### Pharmakon (φαρμάκων) – sorcery/poison
The word φαρμάκων (v. 21) covers a range of meanings including medicines, poisons, and magical potions. In antiquity, the boundary between medicine, poison, and magic was fluid. The term could refer to abortifacients, love potions, or curse rituals. In Roman society, such practices were widespread, and accusations of *veneficium* (poisoning/sorcery) were common in legal proceedings.

## Events and Customs

### Materials of Idolatry
The list of idol materials in verse 20 (gold, silver, bronze, stone, wood) follows a descending scale of value known from prophetic polemic (Dan 5:4, 23; Ps 115:4–7). This reflects the pervasive idol worship in Roman cities, where statues of gods and emperors at all price levels filled temples, homes, and public spaces. For Christians in Asia Minor, contact with such idolatry was unavoidable in trade, guilds, and social settings.

### The Four Sins
The enumeration of sins in verse 21—murder, sorcery, sexual immorality, and theft—represents violations of both the second table of the Ten Commandments and fundamental natural law recognized throughout antiquity. "Porneia" (πορνεία) included in Jewish-Christian understanding a broader range of sexual immorality than was accepted by the surrounding society, especially in connection with temple prostitution and cultic meals.
