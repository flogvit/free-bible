## People

**Haggai and Zechariah** are mentioned in verse 1 as the prophets who encouraged the people to resume building the temple. Both prophets have their own books in the Old Testament, and their ministry can be dated to 520 B.C., the second year of Darius's reign. Zechariah is identified as "son of Iddo" (Aramaic: *bar-ʿIddo*), while in Zechariah 1:1 he is called son of Berechiah, son of Iddo – which can be explained by the fact that "son" in Semitic usage can also mean "descendant."

**Tattenai** (Aramaic: *Tattenay*), **governor of the province "Beyond the River"** (*paḥat ʿavar nahara*), was the Persian official responsible for the area west of the Euphrates, which included Syria, Phoenicia, and Judah. His name is confirmed in Babylonian cuneiform tablets from this period, where he is referred to as "Tattannu, governor of Eber-Nari" (Akkadian: *Tattannu pāḫat Ebir-nāri*). This archaeological finding confirms the historical accuracy of the biblical account.

**Shethar-Bozenai** (Aramaic: *Šetar Boznay*) is mentioned together with Tattenai in verses 3 and 6 as an official who participated in the investigation of the temple building and was a co-author of the letter to King Darius.

**Sheshbazzar** (Aramaic: *Šešbaṣṣar*) is mentioned in verses 14–16 as the one who received the temple vessels from Cyrus and laid the foundation of the temple. The relationship between Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel has been debated among scholars – some believe they are the same person, while others hold that Sheshbazzar led the first return while Zerubbabel took over leadership later.

## Places

**"Beyond the River"** (Aramaic: *ʿavar nahara*) was the name of the Persian satrapy that included all areas west of the Euphrates. This administrative division was established during the Persian Empire and is frequently mentioned in Persian and Babylonian sources from this period. The province was one of the most important in the empire and included strategically vital trade routes.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the Persian administrative practice of written reporting to the king. The letter from Tattenai (verses 6–17) follows the standard format for official Persian correspondence: sender identification, greeting to the king, presentation of the case, and request for an archive search. The Persians were known for their efficient bureaucratic administration, and verse 17 specifically mentions "the treasury" (Aramaic: *bet ginzayyaʾ*, בֵּית גִּנְזַיָּא) in Babylon as the place where documents should be searched.

The expression "the eye of their God was on the elders of the Jews" (verse 5) reflects the biblical concept of God's protective care and active intervention in history, which here enabled construction to continue while the matter was under investigation.

## Key Terms

**"Large stones"** (Aramaic: *ʾeven gelal*, אֶבֶן גְּלָל) in verse 8 refer to large, cut stone blocks. The description that "wood is laid in the walls" may refer to a construction method where wooden beams were embedded in stone walls for stability – a technique also mentioned in connection with Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:36).

**"Decree"** or "command" (Aramaic: *ṭeʿem*, טְעֵם) is a key word in the chapter and refers to official royal authorization. The Jews' right to build rested on such a royal decree from Cyrus, and Tattenai's investigation concerned whether this decree actually existed.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

A Babylonian cuneiform tablet from the reign of Darius I mentions "Tattannu, governor of Eber-Nari" (Akkadian: *Tattannu pāḫat Ebir-nāri*), which corresponds directly to Tattenai in this chapter. The tablet is dated to 502 B.C., only a few years after the events described in Ezra 5, and confirms that Tattenai was a historical person with the position attributed to him in the text.
