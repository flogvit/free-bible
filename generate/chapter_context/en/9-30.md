## People

**Ahinoam of Jezreel and Abigail of Carmel** were David’s two wives who were taken captive during the Amalekite raid (verse 5). Both were rescued when David recovered the plunder from the Amalekites.

**Abiathar, the priest**, son of Ahimelech, carried the ephod that David used to seek the Lord’s will regarding whether he should pursue the raiders (verses 7–8). God’s answer was a clear confirmation: "Pursue them! You will overtake them and rescue the captives" (verse 8).

**The Egyptian slave** (verses 11–15) was an abandoned servant of an Amalekite master. He had been sick for three days and left to die. After David gave him food and drink, he led David to the Amalekite camp in exchange for a promise of protection.

## Places

**Ziklag** was David’s base in the southern border region, which was plundered and burned by the Amalekites while David and his men were away (verses 1–3).

**The Besor Ravine** (נַחַל הַבְּשׂוֹר) served as a geographical dividing point where 200 exhausted men were left behind (verses 9–10, 21). This watercourse, usually identified with Wadi Gaza or Nahal Besor, flows westward toward the Mediterranean Sea. The name may mean "cool" or "glad tidings."

**The cities mentioned in verses 27–31** form a network of settlements in southern Judah and the Negev region where David sent gifts from the plunder. The list includes Bethel (likely a southern town, not the northern sanctuary), Ramoth-negev, Jattir, Aroer, Siphmoth, Eshtemoa, Rakal, the towns of the Jerahmeelites, the towns of the Kenites, Hormah, Borashan, Athach, and Hebron. These were places where David and his men had previously stayed (verse 31).

## Events and Customs

**The Amalekite raid** (verses 1–2) took advantage of David’s men being away. They plundered the Negev region, including the land of the Kerethites, Judah, and Caleb’s Negev (verse 14). The text emphasizes that they did not kill anyone but took women and children captive—this was economically motivated, as captives could be sold into slavery or ransomed.

**Distribution of war plunder** became a point of contention when some of the men (called "wicked and worthless fellows" in verse 22) refused to share with the 200 who were too exhausted to fight. David’s principle that those who guarded the equipment should share equally with those who fought (verse 24) was established as a lasting statute in Israel: "חֹק וּמִשְׁפָּט" – "statute and ordinance" (verse 25).

**Gifts to the elders of Judah** (verses 26–31) were a strategic move by which David shared the plunder with his allies in the south. He called it "a blessing from the plunder of the Lord’s enemies" (verse 26).

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **"וַיִּתְחַזֵּק דָּוִד בַּיהוָה"** (verse 6) – "David strengthened himself in the Lord his God" – describes an active act of seeking spiritual renewal amid crisis, when his own men threatened to stone him.

The word **"פִּגְּרוּ"** (verses 10, 21) means "to be too exhausted to continue" and describes a physical state of total exhaustion. The 200 men left at the Besor Ravine were not deserters but soldiers who literally could not go on.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The account of the Egyptian slave (verses 11–15) offers insight into the treatment of slaves in the ancient Near East. That his master abandoned him because he was sick was common practice—a sick slave was an economic burden during a campaign. The food given to him—a fig cake (פֶלַח דְּבֵלָה) and clusters of raisins (צִמֻּקִים)—were typical travel rations that could be stored for long periods in the hot climate.

The time frame for the battle (verse 17) – "from dawn until evening the next day" – indicates nearly a full day of fighting. Only 400 young men who rode on camels managed to escape.
