# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 39

## People

**Potiphar** (פּוֹטִיפַר) is an Egyptian official with the titles "סְרִיס פַּרְעֹה" (Pharaoh's courtier/eunuch) and "שַׂר הַטַּבָּחִים" (chief of the bodyguard). The name is Egyptian and likely means "the one whom Ra has given." The title *saris* could denote a castrated court official, but in ancient Egypt it was often used more generally for high-ranking court officials, which explains why he was married. His position as "chief of the bodyguard" (or "of the slaughterers") indicates a military-administrative role responsible for Pharaoh's personal security.

**Potiphar's wife** remains unnamed in the biblical text, which is a deliberate literary device. Later Jewish and Islamic tradition gave her the name Zuleika. Her role illustrates the power that high-ranking Egyptian women could wield within the household, while her false accusation highlights the vulnerability of slaves within the legal system.

## Places

**Potiphar's house** represents a wealthy Egyptian nobleman's home from either the Middle or New Kingdom. Archaeological excavations of such houses reveal complex structures with separate quarters for women, servants, and storage rooms. Joseph's role as steward ("over the house") corresponds to the well-known Egyptian position *imy-r pr* (householder), a high-ranking administrative post documented in many Egyptian inscriptions.

**The prison** (בֵּית הַסֹּהַר) is described as the place "where the king's prisoners were bound." This was likely a state prison for political prisoners and court personnel who had fallen out of favor, rather than an ordinary jail. Egyptian sources confirm the existence of such special institutions linked to the royal court.

## Events and Customs

**Slave purchase and household slaves**: The chapter begins with Joseph being sold to Potiphar by the Ishmaelites. In ancient Egypt, trade in Asian slaves was common, and many Semitic names are preserved in Egyptian household records from this period. Slaves could attain significant responsibility and trust, as Joseph's career illustrates.

**Sexual seduction and false accusations**: The story of a woman attempting to seduce a young man and then falsely accusing him when he rejects her has a remarkable parallel in the Egyptian "Tale of Two Brothers" (Papyrus d'Orbiney, c. 1200 BCE). This literary parallel shows that the motif was known in Egyptian culture and lends authentic color to the narrative.

## Key Terms

**"Ivri"** (עִבְרִי) – "Hebrew": This is the term Potiphar's wife uses for Joseph (vv. 14, 17). The word is consistently used in Genesis when Egyptians or other foreigners refer to Israelites and may be related to the Egyptian term *ʿapiru/habiru*, a social designation for wandering or marginalized peoples found in many ancient Near Eastern texts.

**"Sin against God"** (וְחָטָאתִי לֵאלֹהִים): Joseph's refusal is motivated not only by loyalty to Potiphar but also as a sin against God. This theological perspective is central—adultery is understood as a breach of divine order, not merely a social offense.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian administrative papyri from the Middle and New Kingdoms document Semitic slaves in Egyptian households, some holding positions similar to Joseph's stewardship role. The Brooklyn Papyrus (c. 1740 BCE) contains a list of household slaves in which approximately half have Semitic names, confirming the historical plausibility of the setting for the Joseph narrative. Egyptian wall paintings from noble tombs also depict Asian servants in various roles within wealthy households.
