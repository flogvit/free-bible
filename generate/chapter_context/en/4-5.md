# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 5

## Events and Customs

### Expulsion of the Unclean from the Camp (vv. 1–4)  
The chapter begins with the command to send out persons with skin diseases (צָרוּעַ, *tsarua*), those with a discharge (זָב, *zav*), and those who had become unclean through contact with dead bodies. This reflects the Israelite understanding of the camp as a holy space where God "dwells among them" (v. 3). The purity requirements were not primarily hygienic but theological—uncleanness and holiness could not exist side by side. Similar concepts of sacred zones with stricter purity regulations are found in Mesopotamian temple complexes.

### The Ritual for the Suspected Wife – Sotah (vv. 11–31)  
This ritual, known in rabbinic tradition as the *sotah* ritual, is an ordeal (a divine judgment) where the outcome is left to divine intervention. A man who suspects his wife of adultery but lacks proof brings her to the priest. The ritual involves "bitter water" (מֵי הַמָּרִים, *mei ha-marim*) mixed with dust from the tabernacle floor and ink from the curses written on a scroll. Ordeals were common in the ancient Near East; Hammurabi’s law code (§132) prescribed an ordeal by river for women accused of adultery—the woman was thrown into the river, and if she survived, she was declared innocent. The Israelite ritual is notably milder and leaves judgment entirely to God.

### Restitution and Guilt Offering (vv. 5–10)  
The laws concerning restitution in cases of fraud include full repayment plus one-fifth. If the wronged party is dead and has no redeemer (גֹּאֵל, *goel*), the restitution goes to the priest. The term *goel* refers to a close relative responsible for protecting the family’s interests—the same institution that later plays a central role in the book of Ruth.

## Key Terms

**מַעַל (ma'al)** – "treachery" or "breach of faith," used both for sin against God (v. 6) and marital unfaithfulness (vv. 12, 27). The word implies a breach of trust within a covenant relationship.

**רוּחַ קִנְאָה (ruach qin'ah)** – "the spirit of jealousy" (vv. 14, 30). This expression describes the husband’s intense suspicion, whether justified or not. The ritual addresses both possibilities and provides a resolution when the accusation can neither be proven nor disproven.

**מִנְחַת קְנָאֹת (minchat qena'ot)** – "the grain offering for jealousy" (vv. 15, 18). The offering of barley flour without oil or incense was unusual. The absence of these ingredients marked this as a mourning or trial offering rather than an offering of joy.

**אָמֵן אָמֵן (amen amen)** – The woman’s double "amen" (v. 22) is her consent to submit to divine judgment. This is one of the earliest occurrences of "amen" used as a confirmation in a liturgical context.
