## People

The chapter introduces three new central figures. **Nabal** (נָבָל) was a wealthy man from Maon with large flocks—3,000 sheep and 1,000 goats—which made him one of the richest in the region. His name means "fool" or "senseless" in Hebrew, and the text emphasizes that he lived up to his name. He is described as a "Calebite" (כָלִבִּי), which either refers to the lineage of Caleb from the tribe of Judah, or it may be a wordplay on "dog-like" behavior.

**Abigail** (אֲבִגָיִל), Nabal's wife, is described as both discerning (טוֹבַת־שֵׂכֶל) and beautiful (יְפַת תֹּאַר). Her name means "my father is joy" or "source of joy." She demonstrates diplomatic skills and theological insight that surpass those of most men in the narrative. **Ahinoam from Jezreel** is briefly mentioned as David's second wife at the end of the chapter.

## Places

The **Wilderness of Paran** (מִדְבַּר פָּארָן) in the south was a harsh desert region where David sought refuge after Samuel's death. **Maon** and **Carmel** (not to be confused with Mount Carmel in the north) are located in the hill country of Judah, about 12 km south of Hebron. Carmel was a fertile area suitable for sheep farming, and archaeological surveys have confirmed settlement in this region during the Iron Age. The area lies in the transition zone between the arable highlands and the Negev desert, which explains why David and his men could operate there.

## Events and Customs

**Sheep Shearing** (גְּזֹז צֹאן) was one of the most important events of the year for sheep farmers. It marked the end of the sheep's wool-growing season and brought in significant income. The shearing was celebrated with large feasts—the text compares Nabal's feast to "a king's feast" (v. 36). Culturally, it was expected that wealthy householders would show generosity on such occasions, especially toward those who had provided protection.

**The Protection System** David offered was an informal but widespread arrangement in the ancient Near East. Armed groups provided security for shepherds and their flocks against robbers and hostile nomads, receiving food and gifts in return. Nabal's servants confirm that David's men were "like a wall" (חוֹמָה) around them. To reject such protection after having received it was a serious breach of the honor of hospitality and an insult to the protector.

## Key Terms

The expression **"son of Belial"** (בֶּן־בְּלִיַּעַל, v. 17, 25) literally means "son of worthlessness" and describes a person without moral value or social decency. It is used concerning Nabal to characterize his unreasonable behavior.

**"One who urinates against the wall"** (מַשְׁתִּין בְּקִיר, v. 22, 34) is a coarse expression that literally means "one who urinates against the wall"—a euphemism for all males in a household. David's oath not to let anyone "who urinates against the wall" survive until morning expresses his intention of total destruction.

Abigail's image that the soul of David's enemies will be "slung away like from a sling pouch" (כַּף הַקָּלַע, v. 29), while his own soul is "bound in the bundle of the living" (צְרוֹר הַחַיִּים), is a poetic expression that later became significant in Jewish burial customs—the abbreviation תנצב״ה (תְּהִי נַפְשׁוֹ צְרוּרָה בִּצְרוֹר הַחַיִּים) is still used on Jewish tombstones.
