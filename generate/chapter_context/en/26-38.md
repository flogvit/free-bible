# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 38

## People

**Gog** is a mysterious figure who cannot be securely identified with any known historical person. The name may derive from the Akkadian *Gūgu*, referring to Gyges of Lydia (ca. 680–644 B.C.), a powerful king known throughout the Middle East. Gyges sent ambassadors to the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal and was famous for his wealth. However, in Ezekiel’s prophecy, Gog functions more as an archetypal enemy from the north than as a specific historical figure—he represents the ultimate threat against God’s people in the end times.

The title "prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal" (נְשִׂיא רֹאשׁ מֶשֶׁךְ וְתֻבָל) has been the subject of much discussion. The word *rosh* (רֹאשׁ) can mean either "head/chief" or be a proper name. Meshech and Tubal were peoples in eastern Anatolia, known from Assyrian sources as *Muški* and *Tabal*.

## Places

The chapter contains an extensive geographical list that paints a picture of a worldwide coalition. **Magog** (מָגוֹג) is mentioned in Genesis 10:2 as a son of Japheth and was associated in antiquity with peoples north of the Caucasus. The Jewish historian Josephus identified Magog with the Scythians.

**Paras** (פָּרַס) is Persia, **Cush** (כּוּשׁ) is Nubia/Ethiopia, and **Put** (פּוּט) is Libya—these represent lands to the south and east of Israel. **Gomer** (גֹּמֶר) was the Cimmerians, a people from the Black Sea region, while **Beth-Togarmah** (בֵּית תּוֹגַרְמָה) was located in eastern Anatolia, possibly in the Armenia region. The expression "the far north" (יַרְכְּתֵי צָפוֹן) carried connotations of mystery and threat in Old Testament tradition.

**Sheba and Dedan**, along with **the merchants of Tarshish** (v. 13), represent trading nations—Sheba and Dedan in Arabia, and Tarshish likely in the western Mediterranean (possibly Spain).

## Key Terms

The expressions **"in the latter years"** (בְּאַחֲרִית הַשָּׁנִים, v. 8) and **"in the last days"** (בְּאַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים, v. 16) mark this as an eschatological prophecy. These are technical terms in prophetic literature for the decisive future when God will intervene definitively.

**"The navel of the earth"** (טַבּוּר הָאָרֶץ, v. 12) is a cosmological concept placing Jerusalem/Israel at the center of the world. This idea was widespread in the ancient world, where each people tended to view their own holy place as the midpoint of the earth.

The image of **"hooks in your jaws"** (חַחִים בִּלְחָיֶיךָ, v. 4) refers to the ancient practice of leading prisoners of war using hooks or rings through the lips or nose, known from Assyrian reliefs. Here it is used ironically to show that God controls even the most powerful enemy.

## Events and Customs

The description of Gog’s army with **horses, horsemen, shields, small shields, and swords** (vv. 4–5) reflects typical military equipment of the 7th–6th centuries B.C. Cavalry forces from the north, especially the Scythians and Cimmerians, were feared throughout the Middle East for their rapid and brutal attacks.

God’s judgment through **pestilence, blood, torrential rain, hail, fire, and sulfur** (v. 22) draws imagery from earlier divine interventions in Israel’s history, particularly the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah and the plagues in Egypt. This pattern of cosmic warfare was also known from other Near Eastern texts where gods fought against forces of chaos.
