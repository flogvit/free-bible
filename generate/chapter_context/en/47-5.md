# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 5

## Key Terms

**Σκῆνος (skēnos) – "tent":** Paul uses the tent metaphor (vv. 1, 4) to describe the physical body. The image was familiar from Greek philosophical tradition regarding the perishability of the body. For Jewish readers, it evoked associations with the tabernacle and the Feast of Tabernacles, where people lived in temporary dwellings as a reminder of the Israelites' wilderness journey.

**Ἀρραβών (arrabōn) – "pledge/down payment":** This is a Semitic loanword (v. 5) common in commercial contracts throughout the eastern Mediterranean region. It denoted an advance payment that guaranteed full payment later. Papyrus documents from Egypt show that this word was used in contracts for purchasing everything from livestock to real estate. Paul applies it to the Holy Spirit as God's guarantee of the coming resurrection.

**Βῆμα (bēma) – "judgment seat":** In verse 10, Paul uses the expression "the judgment seat of Christ" (βήματος τοῦ Χριστοῦ). The Corinthians were well acquainted with the bēma—the raised platform in the agora (marketplace) where the Roman proconsul pronounced judgments. This concrete landmark in Corinth made Paul's illustration immediately understandable to his readers.

**Πρεσβεύομεν (presbeuomen) – "we are ambassadors":** In verse 20, Paul describes himself as an ambassador for Christ. In the Roman world, ambassadors (legati) were official representatives who spoke with full authority on behalf of the emperor or the senate. They enjoyed diplomatic immunity and delivered messages that had to be received as if they came directly from the sending power.

**Καταλλαγή (katallagē) – "reconciliation":** This term (vv. 18–20) has its background in Greek diplomatic language concerning the restoration of peaceful relations between hostile parties. The word appears four times in this passage (both as a noun and a verb), underscoring the central importance of the theme. Paul uses it theologically to describe God's initiative to restore the relationship with humanity through Christ.

## Events and Customs

Paul's opponents in Corinth seemed to "boast about outward appearances" (v. 12: "ἐν προσώπῳ καυχωμένους" – "those who boast about what is visible/on the face"). In Greco-Roman rhetorical culture, speakers were evaluated based on appearance, eloquence, and charismatic presence. Paul's opponents may have criticized him for his unimpressive demeanor and lack of rhetorical brilliance. Paul contrasts this by emphasizing the heart and inner life.

The expression "whether we are beside ourselves" (v. 13: ἐξέστημεν) may refer to ecstatic experiences that were valued in certain religious circles in Corinth. The city was known for its mystery cults and ecstatic religious practices. Paul relativizes the significance of such experiences and contrasts them with sober service to the church.
