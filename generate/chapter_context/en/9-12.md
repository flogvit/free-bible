# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 12

## People

**Bedan (verse 11)** is a mysterious figure. The name appears only here in Samuel’s summary of the deliverers God sent. Some scholars suggest this may be a variant of "Barak" (as in the LXX), others that it refers to Samson (the Hebrew בְּדָן can be interpreted as "from Dan"), or that it was a judge whose account was not preserved. This shows that Israel’s historical memory included traditions not all recorded in the biblical texts we have today.

## Events and Customs

**Samuel’s public declaration of innocence (verses 3–5)** follows a known pattern from the ancient Near East, where officials at the beginning or end of their service publicly accounted for their integrity. The questions about oxen, donkeys, oppression, and bribes cover the most common forms of corruption among leaders. Calling upon the Lord and "his anointed" (the king) as witnesses gives the declaration legal weight. Similar declarations of innocence are found in Egyptian texts where officials defend themselves in the afterlife.

**Thunder and rain during the wheat harvest (verses 17–18)** were extraordinary in Israel. The wheat harvest takes place in May–June, which is the dry season when rain almost never occurs. That Samuel could summon a storm at this time demonstrated God’s sovereign control over nature and confirmed Samuel’s prophetic authority. For the people, this was an unmistakable sign, since natural rain at this time of year would have been disastrous for the crops.

## Key Terms

**"The righteous acts of the Lord" (צִדְקוֹת יְהוָה, *tsidqot Adonai*, verse 7)** is an expression describing God’s saving actions in history. It does not refer to abstract righteousness but to concrete deeds where God showed faithfulness to the covenant by delivering His people. The term also appears in Judges 5:11 and Micah 6:5.

**"Nothingness" (תֹּהוּ, *tohu*, verse 21)** is used concerning idols and is the same word found in Genesis 1:2 ("formless and void"). Samuel deliberately uses this creation term to emphasize that idols represent chaos and non-existence, in contrast to the living God who created order.
