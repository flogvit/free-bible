# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 12

## People

**Rehoboam** (Hebrew: רְחַבְעָם, "the people have expanded") was Solomon's son by the Ammonite woman Naamah. According to 1 Kings 14:21, he was forty-one years old when he became king. His choice of advisors—the young men he had grown up with at court—over the experienced elders reflects a pattern known from other ancient cultures where crown princes surrounded themselves with peers who later became their closest counselors.

**Jeroboam son of Nebat** (יָרָבְעָם בֶּן־נְבָט) was from the tribe of Ephraim and had previously served as overseer of forced labor under Solomon. The prophet Ahijah from Shiloh had earlier prophesied that he would rule over ten tribes (1 Kings 11:29-39), a prophecy which verse 15 refers to as now fulfilled. His exile in Egypt under Pharaoh Shishak (Sheshonq I) places the events around 930 B.C. Egyptian sources confirm Sheshonq I's reign and his later campaign against both Israel and Judah.

**Adoram** (אֲדֹרָם, also called Adoniram) was responsible for forced labor—a position that had existed since David's time. The fact that the people stoned him specifically shows how hated the system of forced labor was.

## Places

**Shechem** (שְׁכֶם) lies in the valley between Mounts Ebal and Gerizim, in the heart of the territory of the tribe of Ephraim. The place had deep symbolic significance: here Abraham built his first altar in Canaan, here Joshua renewed the covenant, and here Joseph was buried. Archaeological excavations at Tell Balata have uncovered significant structures from the Bronze Age and Iron Age. The choice of Shechem for the coronation ceremony acknowledged the importance of the northern tribes and their historical connection to the site.

**Bethel** (בֵּית־אֵל, "house of God") was located at the southern border of Jeroboam's kingdom, strategically positioned on the main road between north and south. The place had a sacred tradition linked to Jacob's dream (Genesis 28). Archaeological finds at Beitin (traditionally identified with Bethel, although some scholars suggest alternative locations) show settlement and cultic activity throughout the Iron Age.

**Dan** (דָּן) was located at the kingdom's northern border, near the main source of the Jordan River. Excavations at Tell el-Qadi have uncovered a significant cult site from the Iron Age with an elevated platform ("bama") that may have supported Jeroboam's calf image. Dan had a cultic tradition dating back to the period of the judges (Judges 18).

**Penuel** (פְּנוּאֵל) was located east of the Jordan at the Jabbok River, where Jacob wrestled with the angel. Jeroboam's fortification of both Shechem and Penuel shows a strategy to control both the western highlands and the Transjordanian trade routes.

## Events and Customs

**Forced labor** (מַס) was a system in which Israelites were required to perform labor for state building projects. While this was common in the ancient Near East, it conflicted with Israelite traditions of freedom. The people's complaint uses the words "yoke" (עֹל) and "heavy burden"—terms that recall slavery in Egypt and stirred strong emotions.

**The golden calves** (עֶגְלֵי זָהָב) that Jeroboam made were probably not intended as idols in themselves, but as a throne or footstool for the invisible God—parallel to the cherubim in the Jerusalem temple. In Canaanite and Syrian religion, the ox was associated with the god El and the storm god Baal. The words "Here are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt" (v. 28) almost exactly echo the outcry at Sinai (Exodus 32:4), which the biblical writer clearly marks as a repetition of the same sin.

**The festival in the eighth month** (vv. 32-33) was Jeroboam's alternative to the Feast of Tabernacles, which was celebrated in the seventh month in Jerusalem. By moving the celebration one month and establishing alternative sanctuaries, he created a complete parallel cult system that made pilgrimages to Jerusalem unnecessary.

## Key Terms

**"Scorpions"** (עַקְרַבִּים) in verses 11 and 14 likely refer to whips with metal spikes or hooks at the end, not literal scorpions. The name comes from the painful effect that resembled a scorpion sting. This was a known instrument of torture and punishment in antiquity.

**"To your tents, O Israel!"** (לְאֹהָלֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל, v. 16) was a cry of rebellion that recalled the semi-nomadic past when tribes could easily break camp and go their separate ways. The same cry was used during Sheba's revolt against David (2 Samuel 20:1). The expression "What portion do we have in David?" marks a total rejection of the House of David's claim to the loyalty of the northern tribes.

**"The matter was from the Lord"** (סִבָּה מֵעִם יְהוָה, v. 15)—the word סִבָּה means "turning" or "circumstance" and expresses the theological understanding that even Rehoboam's bad decision served God's greater plan, which had been foretold through the prophet Ahijah.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Tel Dan Stele**, discovered in 1993-94, is an Aramaic monument from the 800s B.C. that mentions "the House of David" (ביתדוד)—the first extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty. This inscription confirms that the division between "the House of Israel" and "the House of David" described in 1 Kings 12 was a political reality also recognized by neighboring peoples.

**Sheshonq I's campaign list** at Karnak Temple in Egypt, dated to around 925 B.C., mentions cities in both Israel and Judah. This confirms that Jeroboam's Egyptian protector actually existed and was active in the region shortly after the kingdom's division. The list includes cities such as Megiddo, Taanach, and possibly Gibeon.

At Bethel and Dan, archaeologists have found cultic installations from Iron Age II that match the Bible's description of high places. At Dan, a large stone platform and remains of what may have been an altar were discovered, along with cultic objects supporting the account of the site's function as the sanctuary of the northern kingdom.
