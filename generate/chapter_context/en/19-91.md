## Key Concepts

Psalm 91 contains several Hebrew expressions that carry deep theological significance. The phrase **"seter elyon"** (סֵתֶר עֶלְיוֹן, "the shelter of the Most High," v. 1) denotes a hidden, protected place in God's nearness. **"Tsel Shaddai"** (צֵל שַׁדַּי, "the shadow of the Almighty") draws imagery from desert culture, where shade represented life-giving protection against the scorching sun. The combination of four divine names in the psalm—Elyon, Shaddai, YHWH, and Elohim—underscores God's complete sovereignty.

The term **"dever"** (דֶּבֶר, "pestilence," vv. 3, 6) was a feared reality in the ancient Near East, often understood as divine judgment. **"Ketev"** (קֶטֶב, "destruction," v. 6) was later personified in Jewish tradition as a demon that ravaged at midday. The word pair **"tsinnah vesoherah"** (צִנָּה וְסֹחֵרָה, "shield and surrounding protection," v. 4) refers to protective equipment—tsinnah is a large shield, while soherah (from the root ס-ח-ר, "to surround") denotes a defense that encircles and protects.

## Events and Customs

The animals mentioned in verse 13—the lion (שַׁחַל), viper (פֶּתֶן), young lion (כְּפִיר), and serpent (תַנִּין)—represented chaotic forces and deadly dangers in ancient Near Eastern thought. To "tread upon" them symbolized victory over evil and chaos, a familiar motif from Egyptian and Mesopotamian iconography where kings were depicted trampling snakes and lions.

The final verses (14–16) shift to direct divine speech, an unusual structure in the Psalms. This pattern has parallels with oracle texts from the temple, where priests conveyed God's answers to prayers. Some scholars suggest the psalm may have functioned as a blessing pronounced over someone seeking refuge in the sanctuary, or as a promise given to a king before war.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Fragments of Psalm 91 were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls (11Q11), where it is included in a collection of four psalms used for exorcism and protection against evil spirits. This testifies that in late Jewish times, the psalm was understood as a protective formula with power to drive away demons. Jewish tradition (Midrash Tehillim) attributes the authorship of the psalm to Moses, and it holds an important place in synagogue liturgy, especially on the Sabbath.
