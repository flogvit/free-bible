# Historical and Cultural Context for Nahum 3

## Places

### No-Amon (Thebes)
Verse 8 mentions "No-Amon" (נֹא אָמוֹן), the great Egyptian city of Thebes, as a historical example of a powerful city that fell. Thebes was Egypt’s religious capital and the seat of the god Amun. The city lay on the Nile in Upper Egypt and was famous for its massive temple complexes at Karnak and Luxor. In 663 B.C., Thebes was plundered by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal—a trauma still fresh in memory when Nahum wrote. The prophet uses the fall of Thebes as a rhetorical argument: If even mighty No-Amon could not withstand Assyria, how can Nineveh expect to escape judgment?

### Cush, Egypt, Put, and Libya
Verse 9 lists Thebes’s powerful allies: Cush (Nubia/northern Sudan), Egypt, Put (likely the region of Libya or Eritrea), and the Lubim (Libyans). This reflects the 25th Egyptian dynasty, when Kushite pharaohs ruled Egypt and could mobilize resources from large parts of Northeast Africa. Despite this enormous alliance, Thebes fell—a point Nahum strongly emphasizes.

## Events and Customs

### Public Humiliation of a Conquered City
Verses 5–6 describe a punishment method in which the city’s "skirts" are uncovered and she becomes "a spectacle" (כְּרֹאִי). This reflects ancient practices of publicly shaming a conquered city personified as a woman. Assyrian reliefs and texts document rituals where conquered peoples were stripped and paraded. The image of casting "abominations" (שִׁקֻּצִים) upon someone alludes to the treatment of prostitutes and adulterers, who were publicly disgraced.

### Locust Imagery
Verses 15–17 use detailed images of locusts (יֶלֶק, אַרְבֶּה, גּוֹב גֹּבָי) to describe both the army’s destruction and Nineveh’s merchants and officials. Locust swarms were a well-known disaster in the Middle East, and Assyrian texts document their devastating power. The point is twofold: Nineveh will be devoured as by locusts, and its many merchants and officials will vanish as quickly as locusts fly away when the sun warms them.

## Key Terms

### "City of Blood" (עִיר דָּמִים)
The opening phrase in verse 1 is a technical term also used for Jerusalem in Ezekiel 22:2 and 24:6. It denotes a city guilty of systematic bloodshed and violence. For Nineveh, this refers to Assyrian warfare known for massacres, deportations, and terror.

### "Sorcery" and "Fornication" (כְּשָׁפִים, זְנוּנִים)
Verse 4 describes Nineveh as a "harlot" with "sorceries." This is prophetic imagery for political and economic seduction—vassal treaties, trade agreements, and diplomatic alliances that bound nations to Assyria. "Sorcery" may also allude to the actual prevalence of magic and divination in Assyrian religion and statecraft.

## Chapter-Specific Findings
Assyrian palace reliefs from Nineveh, especially from the palaces of Sennacherib and Ashurbanipal (now in the British Museum), show graphic scenes of exactly the kind of warfare Nahum describes: sieges, massacres, captive transports in chains, and severed heads. These reliefs confirm the "city of blood" characterization. Reliefs depicting the conquest of Lachish in Judah (701 B.C.) illustrate the systematic brutality of the Assyrians that the prophet condemns.
