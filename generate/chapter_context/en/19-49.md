# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 49

## People

The psalm is attributed to the "sons of Korah" (בְנֵי־קֹרַח), a Levitical family of singers. Although their ancestor Korah perished during the rebellion against Moses (Numbers 16), his descendants survived and became prominent temple musicians. This psalm has the character of wisdom instruction rather than praise, suggesting that the sons of Korah also served as wisdom teachers within the cultic setting.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's theme concerning wealth and death reflects a widespread practice in the ancient Near East where wealthy individuals built monumental tombs and named territories after themselves (v. 12). Archaeological finds from the Iron Age, such as the tomb chambers in Silwan near Jerusalem, contain inscriptions where officials named their graves—a practice the psalm criticizes as vain. The idea of "redeeming" (פדה) one's life from death (vv. 8–9) alludes to the legal concept of ransom money, but the psalm emphasizes that no material wealth can purchase freedom from the realm of the dead.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **שְׁאוֹל** (sheol) in verses 15–16 denotes the realm of the dead or the dwelling place of the deceased. In Israelite thought, sheol was not a place of punishment but a shadowy existence where all the dead resided, separated from God. The psalm is remarkable because it expresses hope that God will "redeem" (יִפְדֶּה) the psalmist's soul from sheol (v. 16)—an early glimpse of faith in life beyond death. The expression **כִּי יִקָּחֵנִי** ("for he takes me") uses the same verb (לקח) as regarding Enoch (Genesis 5:24) and Elijah (2 Kings 2:3, 5), suggesting a divine rescue from death's grasp.

The paired terms **בְּנֵי אָדָם** and **בְּנֵי־אִישׁ** (v. 3) distinguish between "mankind" in general and "distinguished men," that is, both lowly and high in society—a way of saying "all people without exception."
