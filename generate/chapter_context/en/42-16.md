# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 16

## Characters

**Lazarus** (Λάζαρος) is the only named person in all of Jesus' parables. The name is the Greek form of the Hebrew "Eleasar" (אֶלְעָזָר), which means "God helps." This choice of name is likely intentional and underscores the theological point of the parable: the poor man who received no help from humans was helped by God. The rich man remains unnamed, creating an ironic contrast—he who had everything in life has nothing to identify him in eternity.

**Abraham** appears in the parable as an authority figure in the afterlife. In Jewish tradition, "Abraham's bosom" (κόλπος Ἀβραάμ) was a picture of the place of honor beside the patriarch in paradise, corresponding to the most distinguished seat at a meal. This expression also occurs in rabbinic literature as a description of the state of the righteous after death.

## Events and Customs

**The stewardship system (οἰκονομία)** described in the parable of the dishonest manager reflects common practice in the Roman-Hellenistic society. Wealthy landowners often hired stewards (οἰκονόμος) to administer their estates. These stewards had considerable authority to enter into contracts on behalf of the owner. The bills of debt (γράμματα) were legal documents specifying rental obligations, often paid in kind. The measures mentioned—100 baths of oil (about 3,400–3,900 liters) and 100 cors of wheat (about 36,000 liters)—represent large values and indicate that this concerned significant tenancy agreements.

**Divorce practices** (v. 18) were a controversial topic in that time. Jewish law permitted divorce based on Deuteronomy 24:1, but there was disagreement over its interpretation. The school of Hillel allowed divorce for almost any reason, while the school of Shammai accepted only adultery. Jesus' teaching here is stricter than both and must be seen in connection with his broader criticism of religious leaders who circumvented the intent of the law.

## Key Terms

**Mammon (μαμωνᾶς)** is an Aramaic loanword (ממון) meaning "wealth" or "property." When Jesus calls it "unrighteous mammon" (μαμωνᾶ τῆς ἀδικίας), he likely refers to the tendency of wealth to be acquired or used in unjust ways. The personification of mammon as a rival to God (v. 13) was a powerful rhetorical device that portrayed wealth as an idol.

**Hades (ᾅδης)** is used in v. 23 for the abode of the dead. In Greco-Roman thought, Hades was the realm of the underworld, while in the Septuagint it translates the Hebrew "Sheol." In this parable, Hades is portrayed as a place of torment (βάσανος), separated from the dwelling place of the righteous by an insurmountable chasm. This reflects a development in Jewish eschatology during the intertestamental period, where Sheol came to be understood as divided into different compartments for the righteous and the unrighteous.

**Purple and fine linen (πορφύραν καὶ βύσσον)** were the most expensive textiles in antiquity. Purple was extracted from rare snails and was so costly that it was reserved for kings and the very wealthiest. Fine Egyptian linen (βύσσος) was likewise a luxury product. The fact that the rich man dressed this way "every day" (καθʼ ἡμέραν) emphasizes his extravagant lifestyle.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of debt bills and economic documents from the first century, particularly from the Judean desert and Egypt, confirm the details in the parable of the steward. These documents show how lease contracts and debt relationships were administered, and how stewards had authority to alter terms. Papyrus finds also reveal standard forms for such documents, with space to specify quantities—just as the manager asks the debtors to write new figures.
