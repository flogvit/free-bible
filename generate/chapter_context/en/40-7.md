## Events and Customs

The chapter mentions "on that day" (ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, v. 22) as a reference to the future day of judgment. This concept was deeply rooted in Jewish eschatology and alludes to "the day of the Lord" known from the prophets (Amos 5:18-20; Joel 2:1). In this context, prophecy, exorcism, and miracles performed "in Jesus’ name" are mentioned—practices that were familiar in the religious landscape of Jesus’ time. Both Jewish and Hellenistic circles knew of exorcists and miracle workers who claimed divine authority.

The conclusion of the chapter describes the crowd’s reaction to Jesus’ teaching, where he is compared with "the scribes" (οἱ γραμματεῖς, v. 29). The scribes were experts in the Torah who traditionally taught by citing earlier authorities in a chain of tradition. That Jesus taught "as one having authority" (ὡς ἐξουσίαν ἔχων) suggests that he spoke directly and independently, without relying on rabbinic precedents.

## Key Terms

The word **ὑποκριτής** (hypokrites, v. 5) comes from the world of theater and originally denoted an actor who used a mask. In a religious context, it acquired the meaning "one who plays a role" or "hypocrite"—someone who displays outward piety without inner truth. The expression **κάρφος** (splinter) versus **δοκός** (beam, vv. 3-5) uses deliberate exaggeration typical of Semitic rhetorical style to underscore the absurdity of self-deception.

The formulation **ὁ νόμος καὶ οἱ προφῆται** (the Law and the Prophets, v. 12) was a standard designation for the Hebrew Bible. The "golden rule" that Jesus formulates positively here existed in negative form with Rabbi Hillel: "What you hate, do not do to your neighbor" (b. Shabbat 31a).

The expression **ψευδοπροφῆται** (false prophets, v. 15) had strong resonance from the Old Testament (Jer 23:16; Ezek 13:1-16). The image of "wolves in sheep’s clothing" alluded to pastoral experiences that were widely known in the region.

## Chapter-Specific Matters

The parable of the two builders (vv. 24-27) reflects real construction conditions in the Palestinian landscape. Archaeological investigations of houses from this period show that solid foundation on rock was crucial for a building’s durability. Winter storms in the region could cause sudden water accumulation in wadis (dry riverbeds), which quickly eroded sandy ground and weak foundations. Many houses were built on rocky slopes where people cut directly into the rock to create a stable base—a practice documented archaeologically in cities such as Capernaum and Nazareth.
