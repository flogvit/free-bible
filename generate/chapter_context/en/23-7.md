# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 7

## Events

The chapter is set during the Syro-Ephraimite War (ca. 735–732 B.C.), one of the best-documented crises in Judah’s history. King Rezin of Aram (Syria) and King Pekah of Israel formed an alliance to resist the growing Assyrian threat under Tiglath-Pileser III. When Judah, under King Ahaz, refused to join the coalition, they decided to depose Ahaz and install a puppet king called "the son of Tabeel" (v. 6)—likely an Aramean who would be loyal to their interests. This plan threatened to end the Davidic dynasty. Parallel accounts are found in 2 Kings 16 and 2 Chronicles 28, which report that Ahaz ultimately chose to seek help from Assyria instead of trusting in the Lord’s promise through Isaiah.

## People

**Ahaz** was king of Judah (ca. 735–715 B.C.) and the son of Jotham. He is described negatively in 2 Kings 16 for introducing idolatry and even sacrificing his own son. His name (אָחָז, "he has grasped") may be a shortened form of Jehoahaz. An Assyrian inscription from Tiglath-Pileser III mentions "Jehoahaz of Judah" as a tributary, confirming his historicity.

**Rezin** (רְצִין) was the last king of the Aramean kingdom in Damascus. He is mentioned in Assyrian sources as "Rasunnu of Damascus." Assyria conquered Damascus in 732 B.C., and Rezin was killed.

**Pekah, son of Remaliah** (פֶּקַח בֶּן־רְמַלְיָהוּ) was king of Israel (ca. 740–732 B.C.). He seized power through a coup and pursued an anti-Assyrian policy. Isaiah refers to him dismissively only as "the son of Remaliah" (vv. 4, 5, 9), emphasizing his lack of legitimacy.

**Shear-Jashub** (שְׁאָר יָשׁוּב, "a remnant shall return") was Isaiah’s son, whose symbolic name carried a prophetic message of both judgment and hope.

## Places

**"The aqueduct of the upper pool, on the road to the Fuller’s Field"** (v. 3) was a strategically important location for Jerusalem’s water supply. Ahaz likely inspected the city’s defenses and water resources in anticipation of a siege. This place is probably identical with the area where the Siloam Tunnel was later built under Hezekiah, in the Kidron Valley outside the city walls.

## Key Terms

**"Almah"** (הָעַלְמָה, v. 14) means "young woman of marriageable age" and refers to a specific, known woman ("the young woman"). The word does not explicitly denote virginity (unlike *betulah*), but it usually implies an unmarried woman. The Septuagint translated this with *parthenos* ("virgin"), which laid the groundwork for the messianic interpretation in Matthew 1:23.

**"Immanuel"** (עִמָּנוּ אֵל, "God with us") functions as a sign with double meaning: In the immediate context, the name points to deliverance from the two hostile kings, but it also carries deeper theological content about God’s presence with His people.

**"Butter and honey"** (חֶמְאָה וּדְבַשׁ, vv. 15, 22) paradoxically represent both blessing and judgment. This was the diet of nomads and indicates that the land will be depopulated so that arable land becomes pasture—riches for the few who remain, but a sign of devastation.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The annals of Tiglath-Pileser III, found in Nimrud (ancient Kalhu), confirm the historical framework of this chapter. The inscriptions mention Tiglath-Pileser’s campaigns against Damascus and Israel, the tribute from "Jehoahaz of Judah," and the conquest of Israelite cities. This confirms that Ahaz indeed sought Assyrian help, as described in 2 Kings 16:7–9, and that the immediate fulfillment of the prophecy (that the two kings would fall) took place as predicted.
