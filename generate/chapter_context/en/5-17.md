# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 17

## Events and Customs

### Astral Worship and Idolatry (vv. 2–7)  
The chapter forbids the worship of the sun, moon, and "the host of heaven" (צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם, *tseva hashamayim*). This reflects the widespread astral religion in Mesopotamia and Canaan at that time. Sun gods such as Shamash (Mesopotamia) and moon worship in Harran were dominant in the region. In Egypt, the sun god Ra was worshipped as the supreme deity. The prohibition against worshipping celestial bodies marks a radical break with the surrounding culture, where heavenly bodies were often viewed as deities or manifestations of divinity.

### The Stoning Ritual and Witness Procedure (vv. 5–7)  
The requirement for two or three witnesses before a death sentence could be carried out was unusual in ancient times. In many surrounding cultures, an accusation by one person was sufficient. That the witnesses had to cast the first stones served as a solemn reminder of the responsibility involved in giving testimony—false witnesses would themselves become guilty of bloodshed. The execution was to take place "at your gates" (שְׁעָרֶיךָ, *she'arekha*), which was the traditional location for legal judgments and public affairs.

## Places

### "The Place the Lord Will Choose" (vv. 8–10)  
The expression "the place that the Lord your God will choose" refers to the central sanctuary where difficult legal cases were to be decided. In the context of Deuteronomy, this is a forward-looking expression pointing toward Jerusalem, even though it is not mentioned by name. This centralized judicial system, involving priests and judges, formed the basis for what later became known as the Sanhedrin in Jewish tradition.

## Key Terms

### Mum (מוּם) – Blemish/Defect (v. 1)  
The Hebrew word *mum* describes physical defects that rendered an animal unsuitable for sacrifice. This concept reflects the idea that the best should be given to God—not what was useless or worthless. Similar requirements for unblemished sacrificial animals appear in other Old Testament texts and contrast with practices in some surrounding cultures, where defective animals could be offered to lesser deities.

### Mishne haTorah (מִשְׁנֵה הַתּוֹרָה) – "Copy of the Law" (v. 18)  
The king was to write his own "copy of this law" from the priests' exemplar. The Hebrew expression *mishne haTorah* can mean either "a copy of the law" or "a repetition/doubling of the law." The Septuagint translated this as *deuteronomion* ("second law"), which gave rise to the book's Greek and Norwegian names. The requirement for a personal copy ensured that the king knew the content of the law and emphasized that even the monarch stood under God's law.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

### The Law of the King and Ancient Royal Ideology  
The law concerning kings in verses 14–20 is remarkable in its limitation of royal power. While Assyrian and Egyptian royal inscriptions highlight the king's divine status and unlimited authority, this text presents an anti-imperialist conception of kingship. The prohibitions against acquiring many horses, wives, and wealth stand in direct contrast to Solomon's later practices (1 Kings 10–11). The prohibition against sending the people back to Egypt to obtain horses reflects both Egypt's role as a center for horse trade and the theological significance of the Exodus from Egypt—a return would symbolically reverse salvation.
