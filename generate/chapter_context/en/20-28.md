# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 28

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains several proverbs that reflect the socioeconomic reality of ancient Israel. Verse 3 describes a poor man who oppresses other poor people, comparing him to "a flooding rain that sweeps away bread". This image reflects Palestine's climate, where sudden, heavy downpours could destroy crops and cause famine. The vulnerability of an agricultural society to natural forces made this a powerful metaphor for destructive oppression.

Verse 8 mentions the practice of taking interest (נֶשֶׁךְ, *neshekh*) and usury (תַרְבִּית, *tarbit*). The Mosaic Law forbade Israelites from charging interest to poor countrymen (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:36–37). This practice was widespread in surrounding cultures but was viewed as oppression of the vulnerable in Israel. The proverb reflects that wealth gained through unjust means will ultimately go to those who help the poor.

## Key Terms

The word תּוֹרָה (*torah*) appears four times in this chapter, distributed across three verses (vv. 4, 7, 9), which is unusually high for a single chapter in Proverbs. In wisdom literature, *torah* refers not only to the Mosaic Law but also to wisdom instruction in general—the guidance of parents and teachers. However, the context of verses 4 and 9 suggests a broader understanding that includes God's revealed will.

The expression רַע עָיִן (*ra ayin*, "evil eye") in verse 22 denotes a stingy or envious person. In Semitic culture, the "eye" symbolized one's attitude toward others; an "evil eye" represented greed and lack of generosity, while a "good eye" meant generosity (cf. Prov 22:9).

## Chapter-Specific Themes

Several verses (2, 12, 15–16, 28) address political leadership and its consequences for the people. Verse 2 describes how a nation's rebellion leads to many rulers—a situation that characterized the Northern Kingdom of Israel with its frequent dynastic changes and coups. Verse 15 compares an evil ruler over a poor people to "a roaring lion and a charging bear", predators that were real threats in ancient Israel. These proverbs reflect the wisdom tradition's interest in just rule as the foundation for societal stability.
