# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 15

## People

**Agag** was the king of the Amalekites whom Saul spared despite God's command. The name "Agag" may have been a dynastic title for Amalekite kings, similar to "Pharaoh" in Egypt. In Numbers 24:7, "Agag" is mentioned in Balaam's prophecy about Israel's future king, suggesting this was an established royal title. Agag's fate – being "hewn in pieces" (וַיְשַׁסֵּף) by Samuel – reflects the brutal reality of ancient Near Eastern warfare and God's judgment on the Amalekites' cruelty.

**The Kenites** (verse 6) were a nomadic group with historical ties to Israel through Moses' father-in-law Jethro (Judges 1:16). Their friendly treatment of the Israelites during the exodus from Egypt (verse 6: "you showed kindness to all the people of Israel when they came up out of Egypt") earned them protection in this conflict. This illustrates the ancient Near Eastern principle that historical "hesed" (loyal love/kindness) created lasting obligations between peoples.

## Places

**Carmel** (verse 12) does not refer to the well-known Mount Carmel by the coast, but to a town in the hill country of Judah, south of Hebron (modern Khirbet el-Kirmil). Here Saul erected a "monument" (יָד, literally "hand") for himself – a practice known from several ancient Near Eastern cultures where kings raised stones or monuments to mark their victories.

**Gilgal** (verses 12, 21, 33) served as an important religious and political center during this period. It was here that Saul was confirmed as king (1 Samuel 11:15), and the site held symbolic significance as Israel's first campsite after crossing the Jordan. That Samuel executed Agag "before the Lord in Gilgal" underscores the religious character of the action.

**Havilah and Shur** (verse 7) – "from Havilah to Shur" – describes the Amalekite territory as a vast desert region in northern Sinai and the Negev, extending toward the Egyptian border.

## Events and Customs

**Herem** (חֵרֶם) – "ban" or "devotion to destruction" – was a radical form of holy war where all spoils of war were to be destroyed as an offering to God. Nothing was to be kept for personal gain. This concept also appears in other ancient Near Eastern cultures; the Mesha Stele from Moab (c. 840 BC) describes similar practices where King Mesha "devoted" Israelite cities to his god Chemosh. The laws of herem in Deuteronomy were intended to prevent Israel from adopting Canaanite idolatry and to ensure that victory was attributed to God alone, not human greed.

**The king's self-glorification** through erecting a monument (verse 12) was common practice in the ancient Near East. Assyrian and Egyptian kings raised such monuments to mark victories. That Saul did this before meeting Samuel suggests his focus was on his own honor rather than obedience to God.

## Key Terms

**נִחַמְתִּי** (nichamti, verses 11 and 35) is often translated "I regret", but the Hebrew word primarily concerns sorrow and a change of action, not admitting fault. When the text says God "regretted" making Saul king, it expresses deep sorrow over Saul's disobedience and a change in how God would act going forward. Verse 29 balances this by emphasizing that "the Glory of Israel does not lie or regret", showing the tension between God's relational response and His unchanging character.

**קֶסֶם** (qesem, "divination") and **תְּרָפִים** (teraphim, "household gods/idols") in verse 23 were widespread forms of religious practice in surrounding cultures. Terafim were divine images or cult objects used to seek guidance or protection. Samuel's comparison of disobedience with these practices was a powerful condemnation – Saul's rebellion against God's word was as serious as open idolatry.
