## Places

**Nineveh** is described in this chapter as a "great city for God" (עִיר־גְּדוֹלָה לֵאלֹהִים), with an extent of "three days’ journey" (v. 3). The expression may refer to the entire metropolitan area including suburbs, or to the time it took to visit all districts with preaching.

## Events and Customs

**Fasting and sackcloth** (צוֹם og שַׂקִּים) were widespread penitential rituals throughout the ancient Near East, not only in Israel. Assyrian texts document similar practices during national crises, where kings could impose fasting to avert divine wrath. What is unique in Jonah 3 is that even animals are included in the fasting and wearing of sackcloth (vv. 7–8). Similar practices involving animals in mourning rituals are known from Persian sources (Herodotus). Sitting in ashes (v. 6) was an additional sign of deep humility and sorrow.

**The king’s decree** (vv. 7–8) follows the form of Mesopotamian royal decrees, where the king acts together with his "nobles" (גְדֹלָיו). This formulation reflects the court structure with counselors and high officials who participated in important decisions.

## Key Terms

The word **נֶהְפָּכֶת** (nehpakhet, "will be overthrown") in verse 4 is ambiguous in Hebrew. It can mean physical destruction (as with Sodom and Gomorrah, where the same root is used) or radical change/repentance. This double meaning may be intentional: the city was indeed "overturned" – not by destruction, but by moral transformation.

The expression **מִי־יוֹדֵעַ** ("who knows?") in verse 9 represents a humble attitude toward divine sovereignty. The same phrasing appears in Joel 2:14 and 2 Samuel 12:22, where people hope for God’s mercy without demanding it. This shows that the Ninevites understood that repentance does not automatically guarantee forgiveness, but opens up the possibility of it.
