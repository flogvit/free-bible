# Historical and Cultural Context for Jude 1

## People

**Jude and James (v. 1):** The author identifies himself as "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James." This James is almost certainly James "the Lord's brother," the leader of the church in Jerusalem (Gal 1:19; Acts 15). By calling himself the brother of James rather than the brother of Jesus, Jude demonstrates humility—he defines himself through his more well-known brother and his service to Christ, not through kinship with Jesus.

**Cain, Balaam, and Korah (v. 11):** These three figures from the Old Testament represent a triad of rebellion that was well known in Jewish tradition. Cain stood for envy and murder (Gen 4), Balaam for greed and error (Num 22–24; 31:16), and Korah for rebellion against God-given authority (Num 16). In late Jewish literature, these three were often grouped together as archetypes of godlessness.

**The Archangel Michael (v. 9):** The story of Michael's dispute with the devil over Moses' body does not come from the Old Testament but from a Jewish tradition that, according to the church father Origen, was preserved in a writing called *Assumptio Mosis* (The Assumption of Moses). However, the exact source text is lost, and the tradition may have existed in several versions. This shows that Jude, like other New Testament authors, could use well-known Jewish traditions to illustrate his points.

## Places

**Sodom and Gomorrah (v. 7):** These cities at the southern end of the Dead Sea had become a standard example of God's judgment on sin in antiquity. Archaeological investigations have suggested several possible locations, including Bab edh-Dhra and Numeira, although identification remains debated. For Jewish and Christian readers in the first century, these city names were immediately recognizable as warning examples.

## Events and Customs

**Love Feasts (v. 12):** The Greek word *agapais* (ἀγάπαις) refers to the communal meals of early Christians. During this period, love feasts were probably closely linked with the celebration of the Lord's Supper, although the exact relationship between them varies in the sources. These meals were central to the fellowship of the church and were held in private homes. Jude describes how false teachers participated in these meals and became like "spots" (σπιλάδες)—dangerous obstacles that threatened the community from within. This practice of communal meals is also mentioned in 1 Cor 11:17–34 and the Didache.

**Enoch's Prophecy (vv. 14–15):** Jude quotes directly from 1 Enoch 1:9, a pseudepigraphal writing that was very popular in Jewish circles at this time. The Dead Sea Scrolls contain several copies of 1 Enoch, confirming its widespread use. By calling Enoch "the seventh from Adam," Jude uses a traditional genealogical designation that emphasized Enoch's honorable place in salvation history.

## Key Terms

**"The faith once for all delivered" (v. 3):** The expression *tē hapax paradotheisē ... pistei* (τῇ ἅπαξ παραδοθείσῃ ... πίστει) uses *hapax* ("once for all") to emphasize the final and unchangeable character of the faith. This indicates that apostolic teaching was already regarded as a fixed deposit to be preserved and defended.

**"Dominion" and "glorious powers" (v. 8):** The Greek words *kyriotēta* (κυριότητα) and *doxas* (δόξας) likely refer to angelic hierarchies. In Jewish and early Christian thought, the angelic world was organized into various classes. That false teachers "scoff" at these powers may indicate that they rejected all spiritual authority beyond their own revelation.

**"Sensual people without the Spirit" (v. 19):** The contrast between *psychikoi* (ψυχικοί, "sensual/natural") and those who have *pneuma* (πνεῦμα, "Spirit") reflects a distinction also found in Paul (1 Cor 2:14). This may be polemic against gnosticizing groups that claimed to be "spiritual" (*pneumatikoi*), while Jude turns the accusation back on them.
