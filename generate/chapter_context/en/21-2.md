# Historical and Cultural Context for Ecclesiastes 2

## People

The author describes himself with royal authority and vast resources, referring to surpassing "all who were before me in Jerusalem" (verses 7, 9). The descriptions of building projects, gardens, water reservoirs, slaves, and wealth (verses 4–9) mirror the biblical accounts of Solomon’s reign in 1 Kings 4–10. The reference to being the second king with Jerusalem as his capital fits Solomon’s position. This "royal mask" lends authority to speak about the limitations of pleasure and wealth from firsthand experience.

## Places

Jerusalem is mentioned twice (verses 7, 9) as the location of the author’s activities. The descriptions of gardens, parks, and water pools (verses 5–6) have interesting parallels with archaeological findings. South of Jerusalem lie the so-called "Solomon’s Pools" – three large ancient water reservoirs that supplied the area with water. The word *pardes* (פַּרְדֵּס, "park/paradise") in verse 5 is a loanword from Persian denoting an enclosed royal garden, which may suggest a later dating of the text, or that such garden layouts were known in the region through cultural contact.

## Key Terms

The word *hevel* (הֶבֶל) appears eight times in this chapter (verses 1, 11, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23, 26) and is central to understanding the message. It is often translated as "vanity" or "futility", but its basic meaning is "breath" or "vapor" – something fleeting that quickly disappears. In this chapter, it describes how even the greatest human achievements and joys lack lasting substance.

The expression *re'ut ruach* (רְעוּת רוּחַ, "chasing after wind") in verses 11, 17, and 26 reinforces this image. It describes a futile striving for something unattainable, like trying to grasp the wind.

The word *chelek* (חֵלֶק, "portion/share") in verses 10 and 21 is an important term denoting one’s allotted portion in life. Unlike inheritance passed on to descendants, *chelek* is what a person enjoys here and now.

## Events and Customs

The description of slave ownership in verse 7 ("slaves and female slaves, and those born in the house") reflects the economic reality of the ancient Near East. The distinction between purchased slaves and "those born in the house" (those born within the household) was legally and socially significant. Those born in the house often held higher status and were more trusted.

Verse 8 mentions *shiddah veshiddot*, a rare Hebrew expression that is highly debated. Many translations interpret it as "concubines" or "harem", which would fit the description of royal luxury and Solomon’s many wives and concubines according to 1 Kings 11:3. Others interpret it as musical instruments or other luxury items.
