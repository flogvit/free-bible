# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 7

## People

**Abinadab and Eleazar** are mentioned in connection with the keeping of the ark in Kiriath-Jearim. Abinadab was likely a Levite or a man of high standing in the local community, since his house was chosen to house the ark. That his son Eleazar was "consecrated" (קִדְּשׁוּ) to guard the ark implies a formal dedication to holy service. This was necessary because the ark required special treatment and protection.

**Samuel’s role** expands significantly in this chapter. He now appears as prophet, priest (offering a burnt offering), and judge over all Israel. This combination of functions was unusual in an Israelite context.

## Places

**Kiriath-Jearim** (קִרְיַת יְעָרִים, "City of Forests") lay on the border between Judah and Benjamin, about 13 km west of Jerusalem. The site is usually identified with Tell Deir el-Azhar. The ark remained here for twenty years—a remarkably long period that shows how central worship life had fallen into decay.

**Mizpah** (הַמִּצְפָּה, "Watchtower") was an important gathering place in the territory of Benjamin, probably Tell en-Nasbeh, about 12 km north of Jerusalem. Archaeological excavations have revealed a significant Iron Age settlement with massive fortification walls. Mizpah served as a religious and political gathering point during this period.

**Beth-car** (בֵּית כָּר) is mentioned as the place where the pursuit of the Philistines stopped. The name means "House of the Lamb," and its location is uncertain, but it was probably west of Mizpah in the direction of the Philistine heartland.

**Samuel’s circuit as judge** included Bethel, Gilgal, Mizpah, and his hometown Ramah. This annual journey covered the central highlands and ensured religious and judicial administration in an area lacking centralized leadership.

## Events and Customs

**The water ritual** (verse 6), where the Israelites "drew water and poured it out before the Lord," is unique in the Old Testament. This was likely a symbolic expression of repentance—to "pour out one’s heart like water" (cf. Lamentations 2:19)—or an act expressing complete dependence on God. Some scholars see parallels to later Jewish practice during the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot).

**The burnt offering of a suckling lamb** (טְלֵה חָלָב) was unusual because Samuel, not an Aaronic priest, performed the sacrifice. This can be explained by the fact that the sanctuary at Shiloh had been destroyed and the regular priesthood was in disarray. The term "whole burnt offering" (עוֹלָה כָּלִיל) emphasizes that the entire animal was burned—nothing was held back.

**The worship of Baal and Ashtoreth** (verses 3–4) represented the dominant Canaanite fertility religions. Baal was the storm and fertility god, while Ashtoreth (עַשְׁתָּרוֹת, plural form) was the goddess of fertility. Archaeological finds from this period show widespread syncretism in Israel, with numerous Ashtoreth figurines discovered in Israelite settlements.

## Key Terms

**"Ebenezer"** (אֶבֶן הָעָזֶר) means "Stone of Help" and is a memorial stone monument. Samuel gave the stone this name with the declaration, "Thus far has the Lord helped us." Interestingly, the Israelites had been defeated at a place with the same name in chapter 4. The new Ebenezer thus marks a reversal of that earlier tragedy.

**"Serenim"** (סַרְנֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים) is a special title for the five rulers of the Philistines. The word סֶרֶן is non-Semitic and likely originates from the Philistines’ Aegean background.

**"The hand of the Lord"** (יַד־יְהוָה) is an expression describing God’s active intervention. In this chapter, the Lord’s hand works through thunder against the Philistines—a motif that points back to God’s thunderous victory at the Sea of Reeds and portrays the Lord as the true storm god, not Baal.
