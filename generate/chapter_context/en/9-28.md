## People

**The Woman of En-Dor** (בַּעֲלַת־אוֹב, *ba'alat-ov*) is the central figure in this chapter. The term *ba'alat-ov* literally means "a woman who is master over an ov" – a concept referring to either a spirit from the realm of the dead or the medium herself. Such mediums were widespread in the ancient Near East and served as intermediaries between the living and the dead. That Saul himself had expelled these people from the land (v. 3, cf. Lev 19:31; Deut 18:10-11), yet now seeks one out, underscores the deep irony and desperation of his situation.

**Achish**, king of the Philistines in Gath, appears as someone who has full confidence in David. His offer to make David his bodyguard (v. 2) places David in an impossible conflict of loyalty that is resolved only in chapter 29.

## Places

**Shunem** (v. 4) was located in the Jezreel Valley at the foot of Mount Gilboa, a strategically important site where the Philistines gathered their army. The place is known from other biblical texts, including as the home of the Shunammite woman in 2 Kings 4. **Gilboa** (v. 4), the mountain region where Saul encamped, lies on the other side of the valley – about 5-6 kilometers away – and would become the site of his final defeat and death (1 Sam 31).

**En-Dor** (עֵין דּוֹר, v. 7) was located north of Shunem, meaning Saul had to sneak past or around the enemy camp to reach it. The name means "the spring of Dor" and refers to a water source in the area. The dangerous nighttime journey underscores Saul's desperation.

## Events and Customs

**Necromancy** – the practice of summoning the dead to obtain information – was strictly forbidden under Israelite law (Lev 19:31; 20:6, 27; Deut 18:10-12), but was widespread in surrounding cultures. In Mesopotamia we find similar practices, where the dead were consulted through specialists. The fact that the text describes Samuel actually appearing (vv. 12-14) has been a subject of theological debate throughout history – some argue it was a deception, others that God allowed a genuine revelation.

**Divine guidance** (v. 6) could be sought in Israel through three channels: dreams, Urim (the priests' lot-casting instruments associated with the ephod), and prophets. That all three were closed to Saul signals that God had completely withdrawn from him. This stands in sharp contrast to how David consistently received answers when he asked the Lord.

**The Hospitality Meal** (vv. 21-25) that the woman serves Saul is remarkable. Slaughter of a "fattened calf" (עֵגֶל מַרְבֵּק, *egel marbeq*) – a specially fattened calf kept indoors for festive occasions – represented significant hospitality. Combined with unleavened bread (matzot), which could be prepared quickly, she provides a fallen king his last meal before he goes to death.

## Key Terms

The word **אוֹב** (*ov*) used for the medium has uncertain etymology, but refers either to the spirit that is summoned, the pit or hole from which the spirit comes, or the person who performs the ritual. The Septuagint translates it as *engastrimythos* ("one who speaks from the belly"). The Hebrew **אֱלֹהִים** (*elohim*) in verse 13, which the woman uses when describing what she sees, can mean "god," "gods," or "divine/supernatural being," and here refers to a spirit rising from the realm of the dead.
