# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 5

## People

**Jebusites** were the Canaanite group that controlled Jerusalem before David's conquest. They had held the city since before the Israelites entered the land, and Jerusalem is mentioned as a Jebusite city already in Judges 1:21. Their mockery of David—that even "the blind and the lame" could drive him away—reflected their confidence in the city's natural defenses on the height of Zion.

**Hiram of Tyre** (חִירָם מֶֽלֶךְ־צֹר) was king over the Phoenician city-state of Tyre. His sending of craftsmen and cedar trees to David marked the beginning of a long-lasting diplomatic and commercial relationship between Israel and Phoenicia that continued into Solomon's time. The Phoenicians were known throughout the Mediterranean region for their expertise in timber work and stone masonry.

## Places

**Jerusalem** (also called Jebus) was strategically located on a hill between Judah to the south and the northern tribes, making it an ideal choice as capital of the united kingdom. The city did not belong to any of Israel's tribes, which made it a neutral capital. "The fortress of Zion" (מְצֻדַת צִיּוֹן) refers to the fortified core of the original city on the southeastern hill.

**Valley of Rephaim** (עֵמֶק רְפָאִים) was a fertile valley southwest of Jerusalem that formed a natural approach route from the Philistine coastal plain up into the hill country. The name likely refers to the legendary "Rephaim" giants who, according to tradition, had lived in the area. The valley was of great strategic importance as it provided access to the Judean highlands.

## Events and Customs

**The Covenant at Hebron** (verse 3) followed ancient Near Eastern custom where a king formally entered into a mutually binding agreement with the people. The expression "made a covenant" (וַיִּכְרֹת בְּרִית) refers to the ritual in which animals were cut in two, and the parties walked between the pieces. That the covenant was made "before the Lord" gave it religious sanction and made God both witness and guarantor.

**The anointing** of David as king over all Israel was his third anointing—first by Samuel (1 Sam 16), then by Judah (2 Sam 2:4), and now by all the tribes. Multiple anointings were not unusual when a king expanded his rule. The oil symbolized God's Spirit and divine selection.

## Key Terms

**Millo** (הַמִּלּוֹא) means "the filling" and likely refers to a fill project or terrace construction that strengthened the city's defenses and expanded buildable area on the steep hill. This became an ongoing building project through several generations.

**Tsinnor** (צִנּוֹר) in verse 8 is a disputed word, traditionally translated "water shaft" or "tunnel." It may refer to the water system that provided access to the city, possibly what later came to be called Warren's Shaft.

**Baka trees** (בְּכָאִים) in verses 23-24 are difficult to identify with certainty but were likely a type of balsam or mulberry tree. The sound of "a marching in the tops of the baka trees" (ק֧וֹל צְעָדָ֛ה בְּרָאשֵׁ֥י הַבְּכָאִ֖ים) was interpreted as a sign of God's invisible army advancing.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered what is called "the Stepped Stone Structure" on the southeastern hill, a massive terrace construction that may be remains of David's fortifications or the Millo. Controversy surrounds its dating, but several scholars link parts of the structure to the 10th century BC. In addition, water systems have been found, including Warren's Shaft, which may shed light on how David captured the city.
