# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 5

## People

**King Hiram of Tyre** is a central figure in this chapter. He was the ruler of the Phoenician city-state of Tyre and had already enjoyed a friendly relationship with David (v. 15). Hiram reigned during a period when Tyre was at the height of its power as a trading nation. Phoenician kings named Hiram are known from biblical sources and later ancient records, and chronologically this aligns with Solomon's reign in the 900s BC. Hiram's role as a supplier of building materials and craftsmen reflects Tyre's position as a leader in trade and craftsmanship in the region.

**Adoniram** is mentioned as being in charge of the forced labor (v. 28). He likely held the same office under David (2 Sam 20:24, where he is called Adoram) and continued serving under Rehoboam, where he was stoned to death during the rebellion against royal authority (1 Kings 12:18). His long career testifies to continuity in the royal administration.

**The wise men with whom Solomon is compared** – Ethan the Ezrahite, Heman, Calcol, and Darda, "the sons of Mahol" (v. 11) – represent a well-known wisdom tradition. Ethan and Heman are also associated with psalms (Ps 88–89), and the designation "sons of Mahol" may mean "sons of singers," suggesting a connection between wisdom and music.

## Places

**Lebanon** was the source of the famous cedar trees that were sought after throughout the Middle East. The cedar from Lebanon was the finest building material available and was used in temples and palaces across the ancient world. The Lebanon mountains lay outside Israelite control, which made the alliance with Tyre necessary.

**From Tiphshah to Gaza** (v. 4) marks the northern and southern boundaries of Solomon's dominion. Tiphshah was located on the Euphrates River, while Gaza lay at the border toward Egypt. This describes a kingdom extending from the Euphrates to the Egyptian border – the maximum extent of Israelite power in history.

**"From Dan to Beersheba"** (v. 5) is the traditional description of Israel's core territory from north to south, representing the entire settlement area of the people.

## Events and Customs

**The treaty between Solomon and Hiram** follows the pattern for international trade agreements in antiquity. The agreement stipulated that Tyre would supply cedar and cypress trees, as well as expert craftsmanship, while Israel paid with agricultural products – wheat and olive oil (v. 25). Such annual deliveries of food to city-states dependent on imports were common in the eastern Mediterranean region.

**Forced labor (מַס, mas)** was a widespread practice in ancient monarchies. Solomon's system of rotating work crews – one month in Lebanon and two months at home (v. 28) – shows an organized administration. This practice later created discontent and contributed to the division of the kingdom after Solomon's death.

**Transportation of timber by sea** (v. 23) describes a well-known Phoenician practice. The timber was tied together into rafts (דֹּבְרוֹת, doverot) and shipped along the coast to a port closer to Jerusalem, probably Jaffa.

## Key Terms

**"From cedar in Lebanon to hyssop on the wall"** (v. 13) expresses the totality of Solomon's knowledge of nature – from the largest and most majestic tree to the smallest plant. Hyssop was a small plant used in purification rituals. This shows that Solomon's wisdom included what we would today call botany and zoology.

**"Rochav lev" (רֹחַב לֵב)** – "breadth of heart" (v. 9) – describes Solomon's intellectual capacity. In Hebrew thought, the heart was the seat of understanding and will, not merely emotions. The expression denotes a comprehensive ability to understand.

**"Kor" (כֹּר)** was a large volume measure, estimated at 220–230 liters (equivalent to a homer). The enormous quantities of food mentioned in vv. 2–3 and v. 25 were intended to demonstrate the wealth and capacity of the kingdom.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at several sites have uncovered quarries with hewn stone blocks from the Iron Age that can be linked to large-scale building projects. At Jerusalem, remains of quarries have been found using techniques that correspond to the description in v. 31 of "great, costly stones, cut stones."

Phoenician influence on Israelite architecture is well documented. Excavations at several temples in the region reveal architectural features consistent with the description of cooperation between Israelite and Phoenician craftsmen. The term "Giblite" (הַגִּבְלִים, v. 32) refers to the inhabitants of Gebal (Byblos), another Phoenician city-state known for its expertise in stonework.
