# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Solomon 6

## Places

**Tirzah** (verse 4) was the first capital of the northern kingdom of Israel after the division of the kingdom (c. 930–880 B.C.), before Omri established Samaria. The city was known for its beauty and strategic location in the hills of Ephraim. Archaeological excavations at Tell el-Far'ah (north) have uncovered a prosperous city with fine buildings from this period. The mention of Tirzah alongside Jerusalem as an ideal of beauty suggests either an early dating of the text or a poetic tradition that preserved the city's reputation long after it lost its political significance.

**Gilead** (verse 5) refers to the fertile highlands east of the Jordan River, known for their rich grazing lands. The region was famous for its flocks of goats and aromatic plants, including the well-known "balm of Gilead" (Jer 8:22).

## Key Terms

**"Terrifying like an army with banners"** (אֲיֻמָּה כַּנִּדְגָּלֽוֹת, *ajumma kannidgalot*, verses 4 and 10) is an unusual expression that combines beauty with awe. The word *nidgalot* may mean "troops carrying banners" or "stars of the heavenly host in formation." The image conveys majestic splendor that both attracts and overwhelms.

**"My mother's only one"** (אַחַת הִיא לְאִמָּהּ, *achat hi le'immah*, verse 9) expresses that the beloved is unique and most highly valued by her mother. In Old Testament culture, chosen children held special status and blessing. The phrase "the daughters saw her and called her blessed" (וַֽיְאַשְּׁר֔וּהָ) uses the verb *ashar* in the pi'el form—the same root found in Proverbs 31:28, where children honor their mother.

**"Chariots of my noble people"** (מַרְכְּבוֹת עַמִּי־נָדִיב, *markevot ammi-nadiv*, verse 12) is one of the most puzzling verses in Song of Solomon. The text is unclear but seems to describe a sudden, surprising exaltation—the soul is unexpectedly placed among noble chariots.

## Events and Customs

**The king's harem** (verses 8–9), with sixty queens, eighty concubines, and countless young women (*alamot*), serves as a contrast to highlight the uniqueness of the beloved. Among all these women, she is "one"—unique and without equal. Concubines (*pilagshim*) had lower legal status than queens, but their children could inherit.

**The nut garden** (גִּנַּת אֱגוֹז, *ginnat egoz*, verse 11), with walnut trees, vineyards, and pomegranates, describes a cultivated pleasure garden. The walnut tree (*egoz*) is mentioned only here in the Old Testament and was a valuable imported commodity. Such gardens served as private retreats for recreation and romantic encounters.
