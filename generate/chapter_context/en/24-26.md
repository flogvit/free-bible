# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 26

## People

**Jehoiakim** was the son of King Josiah, who favored religious reform, but he was installed as king by Pharaoh Neco II after Josiah’s son Jehoahaz was deposed (2 Kings 23:34). His original name was Eliakim, but Pharaoh Neco changed it to Jehoiakim. In this chapter, "the beginning of his reign" (v. 1) serves as the time marker, placing these events around 609–608 B.C.

**Ahikam, son of Shaphan** (v. 24) belonged to an influential family that had supported Josiah’s reforms. His father Shaphan was secretary under Josiah and was involved in the discovery of the Book of the Law (2 Kings 22:8–14). Ahikam himself had been part of the delegation that consulted the prophetess Huldah. The Shaphan family represented a pro-prophetic faction at court that repeatedly protected Jeremiah.

**Elnatan, son of Achbor** (v. 22) was a court official sent to Egypt to bring back the prophet Uriah. The same person is mentioned later in Jer 36:12, 25, where he was present when Jeremiah’s scroll was read and was among those who urged the king not to burn it.

**Micah of Moresheth** (v. 18) refers to the prophet Micah, who prophesied about a century earlier during King Hezekiah’s reign. His prophecy concerning the destruction of Zion (Mic 3:12) is cited as precedent showing that a prophet could pronounce judgment on Jerusalem without being executed.

**Uriah, son of Shemaiah** (vv. 20–23) was a prophet from Kiriath-jearim who delivered the same message as Jeremiah. His fate contrasts sharply with Jeremiah’s: he fled to Egypt, was brought back by Jehoiakim’s envoys, executed, and buried in a common grave.

## Places

**The Temple Court** (Hebrew: *chatsar beit-YHWH*) was the outer courtyard where people from all the towns of Judah gathered for worship (v. 2). This was a public space where prophets could reach a large audience, especially during festivals when pilgrims flocked to Jerusalem.

**The New Gate** (v. 10) was likely a gate in the temple wall built by King Jotham (2 Kings 15:35). This gate served as a meeting place for legal decisions, and it was here that "the officials of Judah" sat to judge Jeremiah’s case.

**Kiriath-jearim** (v. 20) was the hometown of the prophet Uriah, located about 13 km west of Jerusalem. The city is best known as the place where the ark of the covenant was kept for twenty years (1 Sam 7:1–2). That a prophet came from this town underscores that prophetic activity was not limited to Jerusalem.

**Shiloh** (vv. 6, 9) is cited as a warning example. Shiloh had been an important cultic site where the tabernacle stood during the period of the judges, but it was destroyed—likely by the Philistines around 1050 B.C. Jeremiah’s comparison between the temple and Shiloh was shocking because it implied that even the Lord’s house could be destroyed.

## Events and Customs

**The Trial of Jeremiah** reflects the legal system in ancient Judah. The priests and prophets acted as accusers, "the officials of Judah" (שָׂרֵי יְהוּדָה) served as judges, and the people shifted roles from accuser to jury. The charge was that he had prophesied against the temple and the city (v. 11), which his opponents considered a crime worthy of death. The phrase "you shall die" (*mot tamut*, v. 8) was a formal death sentence.

**The Precedent Argument** in verses 17–19 shows a remarkable legal practice: the elders cite an earlier case (Micah under Hezekiah) to establish that prophetic judgment does not automatically qualify for the death penalty. This is a rare example of using prophetic precedent in a legal context.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The name "Elnatan, son of Achbor" is attested in archaeological finds. A bulla (seal impression) with the inscription "Belonging to Elnatan, son of Achbor" was discovered in Iron Age excavations in the region of Judah, confirming the historicity of this court official who played a role in the persecution of the prophet Uriah.
