# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 5

## Places

**The country of the Gerasenes** (v. 1) lies on the eastern side of the Sea of Galilee, in an area known as the Decapolis. Textual traditions vary between "the country of the Gerasenes," "the Gadarenes," and "the Gergesenes." Gerasa (modern Jerash in Jordan) is located about 50 km from the sea, while Gadara is closer. Regardless of which reading is original, the region points to a predominantly non-Jewish district, confirmed by the large herd of pigs—pig farming was forbidden among Jews but common in Greco-Roman areas.

**The Decapolis** (v. 20) was a loose confederation of ten Hellenistic cities, mainly east of the Jordan River. These cities had significant Greco-Roman culture and population, with temples for pagan gods and typical Hellenistic urban planning. The fact that the healed man proclaims the message in the Decapolis marks an early spread of the gospel about Jesus into non-Jewish territory.

## People

**Jairus** (Ἰάϊρος, v. 22) bears a Hebrew name meaning "he shines" or "he awakens." As **synagogue ruler** (ἀρχισυνάγωγος), he held a prominent position in the local Jewish community. The synagogue ruler was responsible for organizing worship services, maintaining the building, and overseeing public Scripture reading. That a man of such status seeks out Jesus and falls at his feet shows the desperation of the situation and an implicit recognition of Jesus' authority.

## Events and Customs

**Graveyards and impurity** (vv. 2–5): The man who lived among the tombs was in a state of permanent ritual impurity according to Jewish law. Contact with the dead or burial places made a person unclean (Lev 19:16). His existence among the graves, combined with demonic possession, represented total exclusion from society—both religiously and socially.

**The bleeding woman and ritual impurity** (vv. 25–34): The woman's twelve-year hemorrhage made her continuously ritually unclean according to Lev 15:25–27. Everything she touched became unclean, and anyone who touched her or anything she had sat on had to cleanse themselves. This meant she was excluded from temple worship, the synagogue, and normal social interaction. Her secret touching of Jesus' garment was bold—according to the law, she would have made Jesus unclean. Instead, Jesus heals her and calls her "daughter," fully restoring her to society.

**Funeral customs** (vv. 38–40): The loud mourning with weeping and wailing (ἀλαλάζοντας) at Jairus' house was expected practice. According to the Mishnah, even poor families were to have at least two flute players and one professional mourner at a funeral. A synagogue ruler's household would have had far more extensive funeral rites. The professional mourners were already present, confirming that the girl was truly dead.

## Key Terms

**Legion** (Λεγιών, v. 9): A Latin loanword denoting a Roman military unit of 4,000–6,000 soldiers. The name indicates both the large number of demons and possibly carries undertones of Roman military occupation. That the legion ends up in the sea could remind Jewish listeners of the destruction of the Egyptian army at the Red Sea.

**"God, the Most High"** (τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ὑψίστου, v. 7): This expression was used by both Jews and Gentiles. For Gentiles, it could refer to their supreme deity, while for Jews it was a title for the God of Israel. Its use in a pagan area is significant—even demons recognize who Jesus is.

**"Talitha koum"** (Ταλιθα κουμ, v. 41): Mark preserves Jesus' Aramaic words and translates them for his readers. "Talitha" means "little girl" or "lamb," a tender expression. The preservation of the original words underscores the eyewitness character and authenticity of the tradition.
