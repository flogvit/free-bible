# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Peter 5

## People

**Silvanus (Silas)** is mentioned in verse 12 as the letter's bearer or possibly its secretary (διὰ Σιλουανοῦ). This is likely the same person who was Paul's coworker on his second missionary journey (Acts 15:40; 17:14-15) and who is named in several of Paul's letters. His Latin name (Silvanus) and Greek/Aramaic name (Silas, possibly from Hebrew שְׁאִילָא) suggest a Jewish-Christian with Roman citizenship, which Acts confirms (Acts 16:37). As a trusted messenger between the apostles and the congregations, he played an important role in the early church's communication network.

**Mark** is referred to in verse 13 as "my son" (ὁ υἱός μου), indicating a spiritual father-son relationship. This is probably John Mark, Barnabas's nephew (Col 4:10), who according to early tradition (Papias, early 2nd century, cited by Eusebius) later recorded Peter's preaching in the Gospel of Mark. His presence with Peter in "Babylon" confirms the close connection between these two, which the church father Eusebius also documents.

## Places

**Babylon** (verse 13) is a debated place name. Three interpretations exist: (1) The literal Babylon in Mesopotamia, which had a Jewish population; (2) a military camp in Egypt called Babylon; or (3) a code name for Rome. The third interpretation is most widespread among scholars, since "Babylon" is used as a keyword for Rome in Jewish apocalyptic literature (cf. Rev 17-18) due to the parallel between the two empires that destroyed the temple. Peter's presence in Rome is confirmed by early Christian tradition (Clement, ca. 96 AD; Ignatius, ca. 110 AD).

## Events and Customs

**The eldership** (presbyteros) described in verses 1-4 reflects early Christian congregation organization. The term is drawn from the synagogue's form of governance, where elders formed a leadership council. Peter uses three related images: "elder" (πρεσβύτερος), "shepherd" (ποιμαίνω), and "oversight" (ἐπισκοπέω), showing that these functions were not yet sharply separated as they would be later. The warnings against greed and domineering suggest that church leaders could receive financial support, but that this created temptations.

**The wreath of victory** (στέφανος) mentioned in verse 4 was a well-known symbol in Greco-Roman culture. Such wreaths were given to athletic winners, military heroes, and at festive occasions. They were usually made from plants that withered quickly, such as laurel, olive leaves, or celery. Peter's description of "the unfading crown of glory" (ἀμαράντινον) – derived from the amaranth plant (ἀμάραντος, "unfading"), known for retaining its color – creates a deliberate contrast to the perishable honors in the Greco-Roman world.

## Key Terms

**Κλῆρος** (kleros, verse 3) is often translated as "those entrusted to you" or "the congregation". The word originally means "lot" or "inheritance lot" and here refers to believers as God's allotted inheritance given to the leaders. This language draws from the Old Testament where Israel was God's "inheritance" (Deut 9:29). Interestingly, this word is the origin of our modern term "clerical/clergy", although here it describes the congregation, not the leaders.

**Ἐγκομβόομαι** (egkomboomai, verse 5) is a rare verb meaning "to bind on oneself" and refers to the slave's work garment (enkomboma), an apron that was tied fast. The image recalls Jesus tying a towel around himself before washing the disciples' feet (John 13:4). Peter thus calls for concrete, visible humility characteristic of the servant role – a radical message in an honor-shame culture where social status was decisive.
