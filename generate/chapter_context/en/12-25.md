# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 25

## People

**Nebuchadnezzar II** (נְבֻכַדְנֶאצַּר) led the final siege of Jerusalem in his nineteenth regnal year (v. 8). His personal presence at the start of the siege (v. 1) and his judgment against Zedekiah at Riblah (vv. 6–7) underscore the gravity of the situation.

**Zedekiah** (צִדְקִיָּהוּ), Judah’s last king, was in his ninth to eleventh regnal year during the siege (vv. 1–2). His attempted escape through "the gate between the two walls" (v. 4), capture on the plains of Jericho (v. 5), and brutal punishment at Riblah (vv. 6–7) mark the final collapse of the monarchy.

**Nebuzaradan** (נְבוּזַרְאֲדָן) held the title רַב־טַבָּחִים ("chief of the executioners" or "commander of the guard"). He was responsible for the actual destruction of Jerusalem (vv. 8–10), organizing the deportations (v. 11), and selecting those who were to remain (v. 12). He also brought religious and civic leaders to Riblah for execution (vv. 18–20).

**Seraiah** (שְׂרָיָה) was the high priest (כֹּהֵן הָרֹאשׁ), who was captured and executed at Riblah (vv. 18, 21).

**Zephaniah** (צְפַנְיָהוּ) was the "second priest" (כֹּהֵן מִשְׁנֶה), acting high priest, also executed at Riblah (v. 18).

**Gedaliah son of Ahikam son of Shaphan** (גְּדַלְיָהוּ בֶּן־אֲחִיקָם בֶּן־שָׁפָן, v. 22) was appointed governor in Mizpah over those remaining in Judah. He sought to calm the military leaders and urged cooperation with the Babylonians (v. 24). A seal impression bearing the inscription "Belonging to Gedaliah, who is over the house," found at Lachish, confirms his historical existence.

**Ishmael son of Nethaniah son of Elishama** (יִשְׁמָעֵאל בֶּן־נְתַנְיָה בֶּן־אֱלִישָׁמָע) was of royal lineage (מִזֶּרַע הַמְּלוּכָה, v. 25). He murdered Gedaliah along with the Jews and Babylonians who were with him in Mizpah.

**Evil-Merodach** (אֱוִיל מְרֹדַךְ), the Babylonian Amel-Marduk ("man of Marduk"), reigned from 562–560 BC. In the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin’s exile, he lifted Jehoiachin’s head from prison (v. 27), gave him a place of honor above other captive kings (v. 28), and provided for his daily needs for the rest of his life (vv. 29–30). This gives the book an unexpected horizon of hope.

## Places

**Jerusalem** at this time was a fortified city with double walls. The chapter mentions "the gate between the two walls that is by the king’s garden" (שַׁעַר בֵּין הַחֹמֹתַיִם אֲשֶׁר עַל־גַּן הַמֶּלֶךְ, v. 4), which was likely located at the southern end of the city near the Pool of Siloam. Archaeological excavations have uncovered extensive layers of fire from 586 BC in the City of David.

**The plains of Jericho** (עַרְבוֹת יְרֵחוֹ, v. 5) were the Jordan Valley north of the Dead Sea, where Zedekiah was captured during his escape attempt. This was the natural route eastward toward Transjordan.

**Riblah** (רִבְלָה) in the land of Hamath (vv. 6, 20–21), in present-day Syria, served as Nebuchadnezzar’s headquarters during his campaigns against the Levant. Here Zedekiah was judged and leaders were executed.

**Mizpah** (הַמִּצְפָּה, vv. 23, 25), identified with Tell en-Nasbeh about 12 km north of Jerusalem, became the administrative center for the Babylonian province of Judah after Jerusalem’s destruction.

## Events and Customs

**The siege of Jerusalem** (588–586 BC) lasted from Zedekiah’s ninth year to his eleventh year (vv. 1–2). The Babylonians built a דָּיֵק (siege ramp, v. 1) around the city. The resulting famine is described as so severe that there was no bread for the people (v. 3).

**The punishment of Zedekiah** (vv. 6–7) follows a pattern known from Assyrian and Babylonian sources regarding the treatment of rebellious vassal kings. Killing his sons before his eyes before blinding him ensured that the last thing he saw was the end of his own dynasty.

**The destruction of the temple** (vv. 9, 13–17) occurred on the seventh day of the fifth month in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth year (v. 8). The detailed lists of temple furnishings emphasize the total loss: the bronze sea, the pillars, and the stands that Solomon had made were broken up for transport to Babylon.

**The executions at Riblah** (vv. 18–21) targeted religious and administrative leadership: the high priest Seraiah, the second priest Zephaniah, the three gatekeepers, as well as military and civic leaders—totaling seventy-two persons.

**The murder of Gedaliah** (v. 25) took place in the seventh month. Ishmael, of royal descent, killed Gedaliah along with the Jews and Babylonians in Mizpah.

**Flight to Egypt** (v. 26): All the people, from the least to the greatest, including the army commanders, fled to Egypt out of fear of Chaldean retaliation.

## Key Terms

**מִשְׁפָּט** ("judgment/trial," v. 6) indicates that Zedekiah was brought before a formal court for his breach of oath as a vassal. In the ancient Near Eastern context, breaking a vassal treaty was a serious offense.

**דַּלַּת הָאָרֶץ** ("the poorest of the land," v. 12) refers to the lowest social class who remained as vineyard workers (כֹּרְמִים) and farmers (יֹגְבִים). Babylonian policy was to deport the elite while allowing poor peasants to stay.

**נָשָׂא אֶת־רֹאשׁ** ("lifted his head," v. 27) is an idiom meaning to show favor and grant restoration, often used in connection with pardoning prisoners.

**אֲרֻחָה** ("allowance/rations," v. 30) refers to the daily food supply that Jehoiachin received from the Babylonian king for the rest of his life.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Babylonian Chronicles** confirm Nebuchadnezzar’s conquest of Jerusalem in 597 BC (the first deportation under Jehoiachin). Unfortunately, the section covering 586 BC is missing.

**Jehoiachin Ration Tablets**, discovered in Babylon, document food allocations to "Ya’ukinu, king of Yahud" and his sons, confirming that Jehoiachin was kept alive in Babylon and eventually received improved conditions as described in this chapter (vv. 27–30).

**Lachish Letters**, ostraca found at Lachish from the time just before Jerusalem’s fall, offer a glimpse into the atmosphere in Judah during the Babylonian invasion, with one letter noting that signal fires from several cities were no longer visible.

**Fire layers and arrowheads** of Babylonian type found in excavations in Jerusalem, especially in the City of David and the Jewish Quarter, provide archaeological evidence of the destruction described in this chapter.
