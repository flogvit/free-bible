# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 5

## Places

**Horeb** (vv. 2, 4) is the mountain where the covenant was made. God spoke "face to face" with the people from the fire on this mountain (v. 4). Moses identifies the new generation directly with the covenant-making event—even though they were not physically present at Horeb, they are regarded as part of the same covenant partner: "It was not with our fathers that the Lord made this covenant, but with us, who are all of us here alive today" (v. 3).

## Events and Customs

**The Decalogue (the Ten Commandments)** is presented as the very heart of the covenant relationship between God and Israel. The structure follows the pattern of ancient Near Eastern treaties: God's self-identification (v. 6), a historical prologue about deliverance from Egypt (v. 6), and stipulations (the commandments, vv. 7–21). A key difference from Exodus 20 is the Sabbath commandment (vv. 12–15): here the verb שָׁמוֹר (shamor, "keep/observe") is used instead of זָכוֹר (zakhor, "remember"), and the rationale given is deliverance from Egypt rather than creation. This underscores the chapter's focus on the exodus as the foundation for ethical living.

**The stone tablets** (v. 22) are explicitly mentioned as written by God Himself and given to Moses. This emphasizes the divine authority and permanence of the commandments within the covenant relationship.

**The people's fear and Moses as mediator** (vv. 23–27) is central to the chapter. The people experienced God's presence as overwhelming—the fire, the darkness, and the mighty voice (vv. 23–26)—and asked Moses to be their intermediary. God approved this arrangement (vv. 28–31), establishing Moses' unique role as lawgiver and mediator.

## Key Terms

**בְּרִית (berit)** – "covenant" (vv. 2–3) is central to the chapter. Moses' rhetoric in verse 3, that the covenant was not made with "our fathers" but with "us who are standing here today," is a powerful actualization that makes the historical event present reality for the new generation.

**פָּנִים בְּפָנִים (panim be-fanim)** – "face to face" (v. 4) describes the direct communication between God and the people at Horeb. This expression denotes an immediate, personal revelation, even though the people's fear later led to Moses becoming their mediator (vv. 5, 23–27).

**אֵל קַנָּא (El qanna)** – "jealous God" (v. 9) expresses God's exclusive claim on Israel's loyalty within the covenant relationship. The word carries connotations of intense passion and concerns God's rightful demand for faithfulness from His people.

**שָׁמוֹר (shamor)** – "keep/observe" (v. 12) introduces the Sabbath commandment and is characteristic of Deuteronomy's version of the Decalogue, in contrast to זָכוֹר (zakhor, "remember") in Exodus 20:8.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

The chapter's structure shows a clear movement: introduction and actualization (vv. 1–5), the Decalogue itself (vv. 6–21), and an extended narrative about the people's response and Moses' role (vv. 22–33). The narrative framework (vv. 1–5, 22–33) makes up nearly half of the chapter and serves to actualize the historical covenant-making event for the new generation standing on the plains of Moab.

Verse 22 marks an important distinction: "These words the Lord spoke to all your assembly"—this limits the Decalogue as what God spoke directly to the people, while the rest of the law was mediated through Moses.
