# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 86

## Key Concepts

Psalm 86 is rich in theologically charged Hebrew expressions that require closer explanation. The term **חָסִיד** (*chasid*) in verse 2 denotes someone who is faithful within the covenant relationship with God—not primarily "pious" in a moral sense, but one who stands in a mutual relationship of loyalty to the Lord. Thus, the psalmist appeals to his covenant relationship with God as the foundation for his prayer.

Verse 15 contains an almost liturgical formula: **אֵל־רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וְרַב־חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת** ("a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness"). This is a direct echo of God's self-revelation in Exodus 34:6–7, the so-called "formula of grace" that became central to Israel's theology and liturgy. By quoting this formula, the psalmist places his prayer within the framework of God's revealed character.

The term **שְׁאוֹל תַּחְתִּיָּה** (*she'ol tachtiyyah*, "the lowest part of Sheol") in verse 13 refers to the deepest region of the abode of the dead—an expression for life's ultimate threat and a metaphor for extreme danger or suffering.

## Events and Customs

The prayer that God would make **אוֹת לְטוֹבָה** ("a sign for good") in verse 17 reflects a widespread custom in ancient Israel where people asked for visible confirmations of God's will or favor. Such signs could serve both as personal assurance and as testimony to enemies and opponents—the psalmist desires that his enemies would see the sign and "be put to shame."

The expression **בֶּן־אֲמָתֶךָ** ("son of your female servant") in verse 16 indicates that the psalmist sees himself as born into service for God, likely as one belonging to a family with long-standing ties to temple service or the covenant community. This was a stronger expression of loyalty than simply calling oneself "servant."

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Psalm 86 contains numerous phrases and expressions that have parallels in other psalms (including Psalms 25, 27, 40, 54, and 69). This may indicate the use of an established repertoire of liturgical formulations.
