## People

**Tartan** (תַרְתָּן) is not a proper name but an Assyrian military title designating the supreme commander, second only to the king himself. This title is also known from 2 Kings 18:17, where a tartan was sent against Jerusalem. The fact that Sargon dispatched his tartan against Ashdod shows this was a significant military operation, even though the king did not lead the campaign personally.

**Sargon II** (סַרְגוֹן) ruled over Assyria from 722–705 B.C. and is the only Assyrian king with this name mentioned in the Bible. His name was known for a long time only from this verse until archaeological excavations in the 19th century confirmed his existence. He succeeded Shalmaneser V and completed the conquest of Samaria in 722 B.C.

## Places

**Ashdod** (אַשְׁדּוֹד) was one of the five major Philistine city-states, strategically located along the coastal route known as the Via Maris between Egypt and Mesopotamia. The city lay about 5 km from the Mediterranean coast in southern Canaan. During this period, Ashdod led a rebellion against Assyrian dominance, supported by Egypt and Cush, which provoked Sargon’s punitive expedition in 711 B.C.

**Cush** (כּוּשׁ) refers to the kingdom south of Egypt, roughly corresponding to present-day Sudan and parts of Ethiopia. At this time, the 25th Egyptian dynasty, also called the Cushite or Nubian dynasty, controlled Egypt. Pharaoh Shabaka and his successors sought to expand their influence northward, bringing them into conflict with Assyria.

## Events and Customs

**Prophetic sign-acts** (אוֹת וּמוֹפֵת - "signs and wonders") were a dramatic form of prophetic communication in which the prophet not only spoke God’s word but embodied the message through symbolic actions. Isaiah’s three-year walk "naked and barefoot" (likely wearing only a loincloth, without the outer garment שַׂק) visualized the fate of war captives. Such sign-acts are also known from other prophets like Ezekiel and Jeremiah, and carried strong rhetorical power in an oral culture.

**Treatment of war captives** in the ancient Near East included systematic humiliation. Assyrian reliefs show captives being led away naked or semi-naked, often with hands bound and ropes around their necks. The expression "with exposed buttocks" (חֲשׂוּפַי שֵׁת) describes this extreme humiliation awaiting Egyptian and Cushite prisoners.

## Key Terms

**"The inhabitant of this coastland"** (יֹשֵׁב הָאִי הַזֶּה) in verse 6 is an unusual expression that likely refers to the coastal region or perhaps specifically to Judah as an "island" in a sea of dangers. The term אִי can mean both island and coastland, and here it is used rhetorically for those who placed their trust in Egypt as an ally against Assyria.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Sargon II’s own annals, found in his palace at Khorsabad (Dur-Sharrukin), confirm the conquest of Ashdod in 711 B.C. The inscriptions report that Azuri, king of Ashdod, was deposed and replaced by his brother Ahimiti, who later also was overthrown in a popular uprising. The new leader, Yamani, fled to Egypt but was later handed over to Assyria. This confirms the historical framework for Isaiah’s prophetic action and warning against relying on Egyptian help.
