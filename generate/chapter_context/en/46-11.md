# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 11

## Events and Customs

### Head Coverings and Hairstyles in Antiquity
The chapter addresses practices regarding head coverings during worship (vv. 4–16). In the Greco-Roman world, head covering was culturally complex. Roman men covered their heads during religious ceremonies, while Greek men usually did not. For women, the situation was more uniform: a respectable married woman (matrona) was expected to cover her head in public as a sign of modesty and her connection to her husband. Women who went with uncovered heads or loose hair could be associated with prostitutes, slaves, or women in ecstatic cults such as the cult of Dionysus and the cult of Isis, where loose hair was part of the ritual practice.

Shaved or short-cropped hair on women (vv. 5–6) was a sign of shame in antiquity. It could indicate that the woman had been punished for adultery, was a prostitute, or was in mourning. Paul's argument rests on the congregation understanding these cultural associations.

### The Common Meal and the Lord's Supper
The second main section of the chapter (vv. 17–34) concerns the congregation's practice regarding "the Lord's supper" (κυριακὸν δεῖπνον, v. 20). Early Christians celebrated communion as part of a full communal meal, often called the agape meal. This took place in private homes, where the homeowner served as host. The problem Paul addresses is that some (likely the wealthy who could arrive early and had plenty of food) ate until they were full before others (slaves and workers who arrived later) came, so that these went hungry.

This practice reflects Roman meal customs where guests of different social ranks could receive food of varying quality and quantity. Paul rejects such social distinctions having a place in the Christian assembly.

## Key Terms

### Κεφαλή (kephalē) – "head"
This word is used nine times in vv. 3–10 and has a double meaning that creates a play on words. Literally, it means the physical head, but it is also used metaphorically. The meaning of the metaphorical use is debated: it can mean "authority over" (as in Hebrew רֹאשׁ, rosh), "source/origin" (as in "the source of a river"), or "prominent part/representative." Paul's argument in v. 3 plays on both levels of meaning.

### Ἐξουσία (exousia) – "authority/power"
In v. 10, Paul says that the woman should have "exousía on her head." This is an unusual expression. Traditionally, it has been understood as meaning that the veil represents the man's authority over her, but grammatically, exousía here refers to the woman's own authority. Many scholars now understand this to mean that the head covering gives the woman authority to pray and prophesy in the assembly—it is a sign of authority, not submission.

### Διὰ τοὺς ἀγγέλους – "because of the angels"
This phrase in v. 10 is enigmatic. Possible interpretations include: (1) angels are present during worship and require proper conduct, (2) a reference to Genesis 6:1–4 where "the sons of God" saw the daughters of men, (3) angels as guardians of the created order, or (4) angels as examples of proper worship. The Qumran texts show that Jewish tradition assumed the presence of angels during worship services.

### Παράδοσις (paradosis) – "tradition/teaching"
Paul uses technical rabbinic terminology in vv. 2 and 23: he has "received" (παρέλαβον) and "handed on" (παρέδωκα). This corresponds to the Hebrew terms קִבֵּל (qibbel) and מָסַר (masar), which described the formal transmission of teaching traditions from teacher to student. Paul thus places the tradition of the Lord's Supper in an authoritative chain of transmission that goes back to the Lord Jesus himself (v. 23).

### Καινὴ διαθήκη – "the new covenant"
The expression in v. 25 connects directly to Jeremiah 31:31–34, where the prophet foretold a "new covenant" that would replace the Sinai covenant. By using this expression, Jesus (and Paul) interprets the death on the cross as the fulfillment of this prophecy and the establishment of the renewed covenant relationship between God and his people.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Corinth have uncovered several private houses (domus) from the Roman period that provide insight into the congregation's gatherings. The so-called "Anaploga villa" and other houses show typical triclinium rooms (dining rooms) that could accommodate 9–12 people in the dining room itself, while additional guests had to stay in the atrium (courtyard). This explains how social differences easily arose during communal meals: privileged guests reclined at tables in the triclinium with better food, while others stood or sat in the atrium.

Artistic representations from Corinth and surrounding areas, including reliefs and vase paintings, show both men and women wearing various head coverings and hairstyles. Terracotta figurines from the period depict respectable women with veils or head coverings, confirming that Paul's exhortations reflect real cultural expectations in the region.
