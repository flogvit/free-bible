## People

**Hiram (Huram) of Tyre** – The chapter presents King Hiram of Tyre as Solomon’s key trading partner in the temple-building project. The Phoenician king already had an established relationship with David, and this diplomatic bond continues. Hiram’s response (vv. 10–16) shows remarkable respect for Israel’s God, as he praises "the Lord, the God of Israel, who made heaven and earth" (v. 11). Phoenician kings are well documented in Assyrian and Egyptian sources from this period.

**Huram-Abi** (v. 13) – The master craftsman sent from Tyre is described as the son of a woman from the tribe of Dan and a Tyrian father. This mixed heritage is significant: it links him to Oholiab from Dan, who worked on the tabernacle (Exod 31:6), while also explaining his Phoenician expertise. The name "Huram-Abi" ("my father Hiram") may be an honorific title rather than a literal family relationship.

## Places

**Lebanon** – The Lebanon mountains were the ancient world’s premier source of quality timber. The cedar trees from there were sought after throughout the Middle East, and Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts confirm extensive timber trade from this region. The Phoenicians controlled this trade and had developed advanced techniques for logging and transport.

**Joppa (Jaffa)** – The port city of Joppa is mentioned as the receiving point for timber shipments (v. 15). This was the nearest natural harbor to Jerusalem, about 55–60 km away. The timber was floated as "rafts" along the coast from Lebanon—a transport method also confirmed in 1 Kings 5:9 and later in connection with the rebuilding under Ezra (Ezra 3:7).

## Events and Customs

**International treaties and trade** – The correspondence between Solomon and Hiram follows the pattern of ancient diplomatic agreements. Solomon offers payment in kind: 20,000 cors of wheat, 20,000 cors of barley, 20,000 baths of wine, and 20,000 baths of oil (v. 9). Such barter-trade agreements were standard in the Bronze Age and Iron Age, before coin-based economies became common.

**Use of foreign laborers** – The chapter concludes with a census of "foreigners" (gerim) in the land—153,600 people who were assigned to forced labor (vv. 16–17). This differs from the account in Kings by specifying that only non-Israelite inhabitants were used for forced labor. The Chronicler’s presentation emphasizes that Israelites were spared this burden.

## Key Terms

**"Algummim" wood** (אַלְגּוּמִּים, v. 7) – This type of wood, also known as "almugim" in 1 Kings 10:11, is difficult to identify with certainty. Possible candidates include sandalwood or a type of redwood. It was used for fine craftsmanship and musical instruments, suggesting it was a valuable imported product.

**"Cor" and "bath"** – Units of measure for dry goods (cor, about 220 liters) and liquids (bath, about 22 liters). The enormous quantities promised to the Phoenicians—equivalent to thousands of tons of grain and hundreds of thousands of liters of wine and oil—illustrate the massive scale of the project and Israel’s agricultural resources at that time.
