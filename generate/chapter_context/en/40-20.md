# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 20

## People

**The mother of the sons of Zebedee** (v. 20) is traditionally identified as Salome based on comparing the Gospel accounts (cf. Mark 15:40; Matt 27:56), although this is not explicitly confirmed. According to one traditional interpretation, she was Jesus' aunt, a sister of Mary, which may explain why she felt entitled to advocate for her sons. James and John, her sons, were fishermen from Galilee who belonged to Jesus' inner circle along with Peter.

**The two blind men near Jericho** (vv. 29–34) are unnamed in Matthew, while Mark names one of them as Bartimaeus. The fact that there were two blind men follows Matthew's tendency to double witnesses (cf. the two demon-possessed men in Matt 8:28). Blind beggars depended on charity and often sat by roads where pilgrims passed, especially near sanctuaries and along pilgrimage routes toward Jerusalem.

## Places

**Jericho** (v. 29) was actually two cities at that time: the old Jewish city and Herod the Great's new winter palace-city, built about 2 km south of the old one. Archaeological excavations have revealed Herod's impressive palace complex with gardens, pools, and a hippodrome. The city lay strategically on the important pilgrimage route from Galilee through the Jordan Valley to Jerusalem, about 24 km from the capital. The striking difference in elevation—Jericho is about 250 meters below sea level, while Jerusalem is about 750 meters above—meant that people literally "went up" (ἀναβαίνω) to Jerusalem.

**The Agora (the marketplace)** (v. 3) was the central market square in any city or village where day laborers gathered each morning hoping to be hired. This system is well documented in ancient sources and continued in the Middle East into modern times.

## Events and Customs

**Day labor in the vineyard** (vv. 1–16) reflects an authentic practice from first-century Palestine. Especially during harvest season, there was a great need for workers, and landowners (οἰκοδεσπότης) went to the marketplace several times daily to hire more hands. The Jewish workday was divided according to hours counted from sunrise: the third hour (about 9 a.m.), the sixth hour (about noon), the ninth hour (about 3 p.m.), and the eleventh hour (about 5 p.m.). Mosaic law required that day laborers receive their wages on the same day before sunset (Lev 19:13; Deut 24:15).

**Payment of wages through a steward** (v. 8) shows a typical system in which an ἐπίτροπος (steward or manager) administered the daily operations of larger estates on behalf of the owner. That those who arrived last were paid first was unusual and serves as a literary device to reveal the attitude of those who came first.

## Key Terms

**Denarius** (δηνάριον, vv. 2, 9, 10, 13) was a Roman silver coin that represented the standard wage for one day's work for a laborer or soldier. The coin bore the image and inscription of the emperor. One denarius corresponded roughly to the minimum amount a family needed to survive for one day, making the landowner's generosity toward those who arrived last remarkable.

**"The evil eye"** (ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, v. 15) is a Semitic idiom for envy or stinginess. In Jewish culture, "the good eye" represented generosity, while "the evil eye" represented envy of others' blessings. The expression was widely understood in the Middle East and also appears in Proverbs 28:22.

**Ransom** (λύτρον, v. 28) was a legal term for the price paid to free slaves or redeem prisoners of war. In the Old Testament, related terms are used concerning God's deliverance of Israel from Egypt. Jesus applies this term to his impending death—a statement central to New Testament atonement theology.

**"Son of David"** (υἱὸς Δαυίδ, vv. 30–31) was a messianic title expressing the expectation of a coming king from David's line. The fact that blind beggars used this title publicly is significant—they confess Jesus as the Messiah as he approaches Jerusalem, preparing for his entry in chapter 21.
