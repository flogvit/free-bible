# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 63

## Places

The superscription places this psalm in the **Wilderness of Judah** (מִדְבַּר יְהוּדָה), the barren, rugged landscape between the highlands of Jerusalem and the Dead Sea. This area extends approximately 25 kilometers from east to west and drops dramatically from about 1,000 meters above sea level down to the level of the Dead Sea (430 meters below sea level). The region is characterized by limestone cliffs, deep wadis (dry riverbeds), and minimal vegetation. Water scarcity was real and life-threatening; the few springs in the area (such as En-Gedi) were vital oases.

Traditionally, the psalm is linked to David’s flight from Saul (1 Sam 23–24) or from Absalom (2 Sam 15–17), both situations where David sought refuge in this inhospitable terrain. The reference to "the sanctuary" (בַּקֹּדֶשׁ, v. 3) suggests that the psalmist longs to return to a place of worship from which he is cut off.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **אֲשַׁחֲרֶךָּ** (v. 2) comes from the root שׁחר, meaning "dawn" or "to seek early." The word expresses both the timing of the seeking (at daybreak) and its intensity—an eager, purposeful longing. In the context of the wilderness, morning was the best time for activity before the heat of the day set in.

The expression **חֵלֶב וָדֶשֶׁן** (v. 6), "fat and richness," refers to the finest parts of a sacrificial meal—the fattest meat, considered the most nourishing and valuable portion. This was festive food, used here as a metaphor for spiritual satisfaction. The contrast with the hunger and thirst of the wilderness is striking.

## Events and Customs

The reference to **the night watches** (אַשְׁמֻרוֹת, v. 7) reflects the Israelite division of the night into three watches. Meditating on God (אֶהְגֶּה־בָּךְ) through these watches was a practice that combined vigilance and devotion. For a fugitive in the wilderness, staying awake at night also had a practical dimension—the need to remain alert against enemies.

The gesture of **lifting up hands** (אֶשָּׂא כַפָּי, v. 5) was a common posture for prayer in ancient Israel, where open hands turned upward expressed both praise and receptivity. This posture is attested in several biblical texts and in iconography from throughout the ancient Near East.
