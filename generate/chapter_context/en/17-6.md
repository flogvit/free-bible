# Historical and Cultural Context for Esther 6

## Events and Customs

**Royal Chronicles and Insomnia**: The chapter opens with the king unable to sleep (נָדְדָה שְׁנַת הַמֶּלֶךְ, "the king’s sleep fled") and ordering that "the book of records containing the events of the days" (סֵפֶר הַזִּכְרֹנוֹת דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים) be read aloud. Persian kings kept detailed archives of all events in the empire, including services rendered by subjects. Herodotus and other Greek historians confirm this practice. Maintaining such chronicles was both administratively important and served as a basis for rewarding loyalty.

**Public Honor**: Haman’s suggestion regarding how the king should honor a man (verses 7–9) reflects authentic Persian court customs. Being clothed in the king’s own robe and riding on the king’s horse were among the highest honors in the Persian Empire. Herodotus describes similar ceremonies in which the king honored deserving subjects. The proclamation, "This is how it is done for the man the king wishes to honor!" was intended to ensure public recognition and demonstrate royal favor.

## Key Terms

**Partemim (הַפַּרְתְּמִים)**: This word in verse 9 refers to Persian nobles or high officials. The term is likely borrowed from Persian (related to *fratama* = "first") and denotes the highest class of nobility at court. The use of this loanword confirms the story’s Persian setting.

**Covering the Head (וַחֲפוּי רֹאשׁ)**: When Haman goes home "with his head covered" (verse 12), this signals deep grief and shame in ancient Middle Eastern culture. This was a common gesture of mourning also found in other biblical texts (2 Sam 15:30; Jer 14:3–4).

## Characters

**Zeresh and Haman’s Advisors**: In verse 13, both Haman’s wife Zeresh and his "wise men" (חֲכָמָיו) are mentioned. The fact that Zeresh is named alongside the advisors shows that women in the Persian aristocracy could hold advisory roles. Their warning that Haman will "fall" before Mordecai "if he is of Jewish descent" suggests an acknowledgment of the Jews’ special status and divine protection—a remarkable statement from non-Jews within the narrative.
