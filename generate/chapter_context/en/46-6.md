# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 6

## Events and Customs

### The Legal System in Corinth
Paul's criticism of Christians who took legal disputes against one another to pagan courts (vv. 1-8) must be understood in light of the Roman legal system in Corinth. The city had a well-established forum where civil cases were handled. The large *bema* (judgment seat) on the agora illustrates where such cases were brought. Romans were known for their sophisticated judicial system, but legal proceedings were often marked by corruption, bribery, and favoritism toward the wealthy. Jewish communities traditionally had their own courts (*beth din*) based on Torah law, and Paul expects the Christian congregation to follow a similar principle of internal conflict resolution.

### Temple Prostitution and Sexual Morality
Corinth's reputation for sexual immorality was well known in antiquity. The expression "to Corinthianize" (κορινθιάζεσθαι) was used as a synonym for immoral conduct. According to Strabo (describing an earlier period), the temple of Aphrodite on Acrocorinth housed over a thousand temple slaves (*hierodouloi*). However, modern scholarship is skeptical about the extent of temple prostitution in Corinth during New Testament times. Regardless of this, the port city was full of brothels and prostitutes serving sailors and merchants. Archaeological findings of small shops (*tabernae*) along the main streets, some of which likely functioned as brothels based on graffiti and interior design, confirm the city's reputation. Paul's exhortation to "flee from sexual immorality" (φεύγετε τὴν πορνείαν, v. 18) was highly relevant in this context.

## Key Terms

### Ἄδικοι (adikoi) and ἅγιοι (hagioi)
The contrast between "the unrighteous" (τῶν ἀδίκων, v. 1) and "the saints" (τῶν ἁγίων, vv. 1-2) is fundamental to Paul's argument. *Adikoi* here refers to the pagan judges and the secular legal system, while *hagioi* designates believers as a set-apart community. Paul's point is that those who will judge the world and angels (vv. 2-3) should be able to resolve their own internal conflicts.

### Πόρνη (pornē) and πορνεία (porneia)
The Greek word *pornē* (prostitute, vv. 15-16) and *porneia* (sexual immorality, vv. 13, 18) cover a broader range than modern terms. *Porneia* included all forms of sexual activity outside marriage. In Greco-Roman culture, it was socially acceptable for men to visit prostitutes, and this was not considered adultery. Paul radically challenges this cultural norm by insisting that the believer's body belongs to Christ.

### Μαλακοί (malakoi) and ἀρσενοκοῖται (arsenokoitai)
These two terms in v. 9 are debated. *Malakoi* (literally "soft") was used for men who took the passive role in homosexual relationships, often young men or slaves. *Arsenokoitai* is a rare word, possibly coined by Paul based on the Septuagint's translation of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13, and refers to men who lie with men. Both terms must be understood within the ancient practices of pederasty and sexual exploitation of slaves.

### Ναός (naos) – Temple
When Paul describes the body as a "temple" for the Holy Spirit (v. 19), he uses *naos*, which specifically denotes the sanctuary itself—the innermost room where the deity dwelt—not the entire temple complex (*hieron*). For readers familiar with the many sanctuaries in Corinth, this metaphor was extremely powerful: the believer's body is as holy as the Most Holy Place.

### Ἠγοράσθητε τιμῆς (ēgorasthēte timēs) – Bought for a Price
The expression in v. 20 "you were bought with a price" alludes to slave trading, which was widespread in Corinth as a commercial hub. The freeing of slaves often occurred through the payment of a "ransom." Paul applies this image theologically: believers have been purchased out of slavery to sin but now belong to a new master—Christ.
