# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 5

## Places

The chapter opens with Jesus "going up on the mountain" (τὸ ὄρος). The traditional location is a hill on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee, near Capernaum, known today as the "Mount of Beatitudes" (Latin: Mons Beatitudinum). Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of a Byzantine church from the 400s at this site, testifying to an early Christian tradition connected with this location. Jesus teaching from a mountain carries clear symbolic meaning: it mirrors Moses receiving the law on Mount Sinai and thus presents Jesus as the new lawgiver.

Jerusalem is mentioned in verse 35 as "the city of the great King." In that time, Jerusalem was the religious and political center for the Jewish people, with the temple serving as the absolute focal point for worship and sacrifices. The reference to the altar (θυσιαστήριον) in verses 23–24 assumes that the temple still stood and that sacrificial practices were active.

## Events and Customs

The chapter presupposes familiarity with several Jewish customs. Temple sacrifice (vv. 23–24) was central to Jewish religious life. People traveled to Jerusalem to bring offerings, and Jesus emphasizes that reconciliation with others must precede ritual worship. The practice of divorce (vv. 31–32) refers to the regulation in Deuteronomy 24:1, where a man could give his wife a certificate of divorce (ἀποστάσιον). In Jesus’ time, rabbinic schools of Hillel and Shammai debated vigorously over valid grounds for divorce—Hillel allowed divorce for almost any reason, while Shammai restricted it to adultery.

Oath-taking (vv. 33–37) was widespread in Jewish culture; people swore by heaven, earth, Jerusalem, or their own head to avoid using God’s name directly. The practice of forced labor (ἀγγαρεύσει, v. 41) was a Persian system adopted by the Romans: soldiers could compel civilians to carry equipment for one mile (about 1.5 km). This served as a daily reminder of occupation and was deeply hated among Jews.

## Key Terms

**Μακάριοι** (makarioi, "blessed") opens each of the eight beatitudes. The word means more than "happy"—it describes a state of divine blessing and favor, independent of external circumstances. In Greco-Roman culture, gods and the wealthy were called "makarioi"; Jesus radically reverses this by attributing that condition to the poor, those who mourn, and the persecuted.

**Πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι** ("poor in spirit", v. 3) uses the strongest Greek word for poverty (πτωχός), describing a person entirely dependent on another’s grace—in contrast to πένης, which describes a working poor person. The expression has parallels in the Qumran texts, where similar formulations describe the pious remnant who humbly place their trust in God.

**Ῥακά** (v. 22) is an Aramaic insult (ריקא) meaning "empty-headed" or "fool," while **μωρέ** ("you fool") may have carried deeper connotations of moral and spiritual decay. Both words illustrate how Jesus radicalizes the command against murder to include hateful speech.

**Γέεννα** (Gehenna, vv. 22, 29, 30) refers to the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, where child sacrifices had taken place during the monarchy (2 Kings 23:10). By Jesus’ time, it had become a symbol of final judgment and punishment.

**Κοδράντην** (v. 26) is the smallest Roman coin, a quadrans, worth 1/64 of a denarius—a very small sum. The word is a Latin loanword in Greek and testifies to Roman economic influence.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological finds from Qumran by the Dead Sea have shed light on the language and thought world of the beatitudes. The text 4Q525 ("Beatitudes") contains similar formulations such as "Blessed is the one who speaks truth with a pure heart" and "Blessed is the one who seeks her [wisdom] with clean hands." This shows that the beatitude form was known in Jewish devotional literature, while Jesus’ version stands out due to its radical content and eschatological perspective.

Finds of oil lamps (λύχνος, v. 15) and lampstands (λυχνία) from the period confirm the everyday reality Jesus draws upon in his image of light. Typical houses in Galilee had one or a few lamps placed on a shelf or stand to illuminate the entire room. A modius (μόδιον) was a Roman grain measure of about 8.75 liters, used in every household—an everyday image that listeners would immediately recognize.
