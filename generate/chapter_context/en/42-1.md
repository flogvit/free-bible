# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 1

## People

**Zechariah and Elizabeth** were an elderly priestly couple who lived during the reign of Herod the Great (37–4 B.C.). Zechariah belonged to the division of Abijah, one of twenty-four priestly divisions that served in the temple on a rotating basis. Elizabeth was descended from Aaron, which gave their son John a double priestly inheritance. Their situation as an elderly childless couple reflects Old Testament narratives about Abraham and Sarah, as well as Elkanah and Hannah.

**Mary** (Greek: Μαριάμ) was a young woman from Nazareth, betrothed to Joseph of the line of David. Betrothal (*mnēsteuomai*) in Jewish culture was legally binding and typically lasted one year before the wedding. A betrothed woman was considered married in a legal sense, and adultery could be punished by death. Mary’s young age—likely her early teens—was typical for betrothal in this culture.

**The angel Gabriel** (Γαβριήλ) was known from the book of Daniel (Dan 8:16; 9:21) as a messenger who brought divine revelations. His name means "God’s strong man." In Jewish tradition, he was regarded as one of the four archangels who stood before God’s presence.

## Places

**The temple in Jerusalem**, where Zechariah served, was Herod’s magnificent reconstruction of the second temple. The altar of incense (*thysiastērion tou thymiamatos*) stood in the Holy Place, before the veil to the Most Holy Place. Being chosen by lot to offer incense was a rare honor that a priest might experience only once in his lifetime.

**Nazareth** (Ναζαρέθ) was a small village in Galilee with an estimated population of 400–500 people. Archaeological excavations have revealed simple dwellings carved into limestone hills, winepresses, and agricultural terraces. The town is not mentioned in the Old Testament or in the writings of Josephus, underscoring its insignificance at that time.

**The hill country of Judea** (τὴν ὀρεινήν), where Zechariah and Elizabeth lived, was likely located near Jerusalem. Traditionally, Ein Karem, about 8 km west of Jerusalem, has been identified as the place. The journey from Nazareth to this area would have taken 3–4 days on foot.

## Events and Customs

**Priestly divisions** (ἐφημερία) were organized according to the system King David established in 1 Chronicles 24. Each division served one week at a time, twice a year, plus during the major festivals. The division of Abijah was eighth in order. During their service week, priests lived in the temple and performed the daily sacrifices.

**The incense offering** was made twice daily—morning and evening—at the golden altar in the Holy Place. The priest chosen by lot entered alone while the people stood outside in prayer. The incense symbolized the prayers of the people rising to God. There were strict rules about how long a priest could remain inside, so the people’s anxiety over Zechariah’s delay was understandable.

**Circumcision on the eighth day** (ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ὀγδόῃ) was commanded in the Law of Moses and marked the boy’s inclusion in the covenant. It was customary to give the child a name at this occasion. The relatives’ expectation that the child would be named after his father reflects the widespread tradition of naming children after family members, even though directly naming a child after a living father was less common.

## Key Terms

**Σίκερα** ("strong drink") is a loanword from Hebrew *shekhar* and refers to fermented beverages made from fruit, grain, or honey—anything except grape wine. The prohibition against wine and strong drink (v. 15) links John to the Nazirite vow (Num 6:1–4), although the text does not explicitly call him a Nazirite.

**Ἀνατολὴ ἐξ ὕψους** ("sunrise from on high") in Zechariah’s song of praise (v. 78) is a messianic expression. The Greek *anatolē* can mean both "sunrise" and "branch/sprout," thus alluding to prophecies about the Messiah as the "Branch of David" (Jer 23:5; Zech 3:8) and as "light" for the nations.

## Archaeological Findings

An inscription found in Caesarea Maritima from the third–fourth century A.D. confirms the existence of the twenty-four priestly divisions and specifically notes that the division of Abijah was settled in an area that may possibly be identified with traditional sites in the hill country of Judea. Although the inscription is later than the New Testament, it confirms the continuity of the priestly organization described by Luke.
