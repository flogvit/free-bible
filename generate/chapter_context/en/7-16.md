# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 16

## People

**Samson (שִׁמְשׁוֹן)** appears in this chapter in the final phase of his life as a judge. His name is linked to the Hebrew word *shemesh* (שֶׁמֶשׁ, sun). He is identified as "a Nazirite from birth" (v. 17), a consecration that involved abstaining from wine, avoiding contact with dead bodies, and refraining from cutting his hair.

**Delilah (דְּלִילָה)** is introduced without any ethnic identification—the text states neither that she is an Israelite nor a Philistine. Her name may be connected to the Hebrew *laylah* (night) or the Arabic word for "coquette." She lives in the Valley of Sorek, a border region between Israelite and Philistine territory. The fact that the Philistine rulers offered her 1,100 silver pieces *each* (a total of 5,500 silver pieces) shows how highly they valued capturing Samson.

## Places

**Gaza (עַזָּה)** was the southernmost and one of the most important of the five Philistine city-states. It lay along the coastal road between Egypt and Mesopotamia and served as a major commercial center. The distance from Gaza to "the mountain that faces Hebron" (v. 3) is considerable, underscoring the magnitude of Samson's feat.

**The Valley of Sorek (נַחַל שֹׂרֵק)** was an important valley extending from the highlands of Judah down toward the Philistine coast. The name means "the valley of the red vine" and refers to the wine production in the area. The valley functioned as a natural corridor and border region between Israelite and Philistine territory.

## Events and Customs

**The Nazirite Vow (נְזִיר):** Samson reveals that his uncut hair was the sign of his Nazirite covenant from birth (v. 17, cf. chap. 13). The growth of his hair symbolized an unbroken connection with God, while shaving represented a breach of the covenant.

**The Temple Festival for Dagon** (vv. 23–25) provides insight into Philistine religious practice. Dagon was a central deity in the Philistine pantheon, likely associated with grain (from *dagan*). The temple festival included sacrifices, praise of the god, and entertainment—in this case, in the form of their humiliated enemy. That Samson was brought out to "entertain" (וִישַׂחֶק) them suggests a ritual humiliation of the defeated foe as part of religious celebration.

**Blinding of prisoners** (v. 21) was a common practice in the ancient Near East to neutralize dangerous captives without killing them. That Samson was set to grind grain in the prison house (בְּבֵית הָאֲסוּרִים) indicates the heavy labor normally performed by slaves or donkeys.

## Key Terms

**סַרְנֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים (sarnei felishtim)** – "the Philistine rulers" – use the distinctive word *seren* (plural *seranim*), which is applied only to Philistine leaders. The term reflects the non-Semitic background of the Philistines, who originally came from the Aegean region.

**מַחְלְפוֹת (machlefot)** – "braids" or "locks" – refers to Samson's seven locks of hair (vv. 13, 19). The number seven carries symbolic significance and underscores the completeness of his Nazirite consecration.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Tell es-Safi (identified as the Philistine city of Gath) and other Philistine cities have uncovered temples with characteristic architecture, including buildings supported by two central pillars placed close together. This construction style, in which the roof rested on two main pillars, remarkably matches the description of the temple of Dagon in this chapter. Such a structure would be vulnerable if the two supporting pillars were pushed down simultaneously, lending archaeological plausibility to the narrative.
