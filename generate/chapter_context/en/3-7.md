# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 7

## Events and Customs

Chapter 7 concludes the sacrifice instructions that began in chapter 1, with priest-specific additions from chapter 6. The chapter provides detailed regulations concerning the guilt offering (אָשָׁם, *asham*), the thank offering, and the peace offering (שְׁלָמִים, *shelamim*). Verses 37–38 summarize the entire sacrificial law from chapters 1–7.

A central element is the *tenufa* ritual (verse 30), in which the offerer themselves was to bring the fat lying on the breast with their own hands (יָדָיו תְּבִיאֶינָה), and the breast was to be waved as a wave offering (תְּנוּפָה) before the Lord. This was an action in which the offerer actively participated in the ritual process at the altar. The breast (*hazeh*) and the right thigh (*shoq*) were given to the priests as their portion (verses 31–34)—a provision that ensured the support of the priesthood.

The prohibition against eating fat (חֵלֶב, *helev*, verses 23–25) and blood (verses 26–27) was fundamental in Israelite religion. The internal fat was reserved for God alone and was to be burned on the altar. The penalty for violation—to be "cut off from one's people" (*karet*)—marks this as one of the most serious prohibitions in the law.

## Key Terms

**Piggul** (פִּגּוּל, verse 18) is an important term describing sacrificial meat that has become "abominable" or "rejected" because it was eaten on the third day. The word occurs only four times in the Old Testament and denotes something that has become ritually repulsive. The time limits for eating sacrificial meat had both practical and theological reasons: the thank offering had to be eaten on the same day (verse 15), while a vow offering (נֶדֶר) or a freewill offering (נְדָבָה) could be eaten on the day of sacrifice and the following day (verses 16–17). In the warm climate, meat began to spoil quickly, and the sacrificial meal was intended to be a solemn fellowship, not a means of stockpiling food.

**Terumah** (תְּרוּמָה, "heave offering/contribution," verses 14, 32) refers to the portion that was "lifted up" or set apart for the Lord and His servants. This concept became foundational for the support of the priests in Israel.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries at Israelite cult sites have uncovered altars and bone remains where fatty parts appear to have been treated separately, which may correspond with the regulations concerning the burning of internal fat (verses 3–5).
