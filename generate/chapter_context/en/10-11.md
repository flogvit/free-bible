# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 11

## People

**Bathsheba** (בַּת־שֶׁבַע, "daughter of an oath" or "seventh daughter") is introduced as the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah. Eliam was likely one of David's "thirty mighty men" (2 Sam 23:34), which places Bathsheba within the circle of the military elite. She was therefore not a stranger, but belonged to a family David knew well.

**Uriah the Hittite** (אוּרִיָּה הַחִתִּי) bears an Israelite theophoric name meaning "YHWH is my light," yet he is called "the Hittite." This indicates either Hittite origin or affiliation with the Hittite population that still lived in Canaan. Uriah is counted among David's "thirty mighty men" (2 Sam 23:39). Despite his non-Israelite background, he demonstrates exemplary loyalty to the covenant and the army—a sharp contrast to David's behavior.

## Places

**Rabbah** (רַבָּה) was the capital of Ammon, today's Amman in Jordan. The city was strategically located near the sources of the Jabbok River, about 35 km east of the Jordan. Archaeological excavations have uncovered fortifications from the Bronze and Iron Ages that explain the challenging combat conditions. The area controlled important trade routes, making its conquest significant.

**The king's roof in Jerusalem** was likely a rooftop terrace on the highest palace in the city. David's Jerusalem (the City of David) sat on a narrow ridge, and the palace at the highest point offered a view down over the surrounding houses. This explains how David could see Bathsheba bathing on her own roof.

## Events and Customs

"At the turn of the year when kings go out to war" (לִתְשׁוּבַת הַשָּׁנָה) refers to spring, after the rainy season, when roads became passable and military campaigns were possible. That David remained in Jerusalem broke with the convention that the king personally led his troops—a first hint that something was wrong.

Bathsheba's purification (מִתְקַדֶּשֶׁת מִטֻּמְאָתָהּ) refers to ritual cleansing after menstruation according to Lev 15:19-30. This detail is legally important: it confirms that Bathsheba was not already pregnant, and that the child was unquestionably David's.

Uriah's refusal to go home reflects holy war practices. Soldiers on active duty practiced sexual abstinence (cf. 1 Sam 21:5; Deut 23:9-14). The ark of the covenant was with the army, making the campaign a sacred act. Uriah's loyalty to this covenant stood in sharp contrast to David's breach of it.

## Key Terms

The expression "wash your feet" (רְחַץ רַגְלֶיךָ) was a euphemism that could mean either literal footwashing after travel or sexual intercourse within marriage. David's intention was clear: Uriah should sleep with his wife so the pregnancy could be attributed to him.

"The king's gift" (מַשְׂאַת הַמֶּלֶךְ) sent to Uriah was a token of honor consisting of food from the king's table—an attempt to lure him home and make the situation more tempting.
