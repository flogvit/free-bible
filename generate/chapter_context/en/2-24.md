# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 24

## People

This chapter mentions several key figures in addition to Moses. **Aaron** is Moses' brother and later the high priest. **Nadab and Abihu** are Aaron's two eldest sons, who later die when they offer "strange fire" before the Lord (Leviticus 10). **Joshua** is referred to here as Moses' "servant" (מְשָׁרְתוֹ, *mesharto*, v. 13) – a title indicating a trusted assistant and apprentice, not a slave. **Hur** is mentioned alongside Aaron as a leader in Moses' absence (v. 14); Jewish tradition identifies him as Caleb's son and Bezalel's grandfather (cf. Exodus 31:2; 1 Chronicles 2:19-20).

The **seventy of the elders of Israel** (שִׁבְעִים מִזִּקְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, v. 1, 9) represent Israel's leadership. The number seventy held symbolic significance in antiquity as a number of perfection (7 × 10), and this group forms the precursor to the later Sanhedrin.

## Places

**Mount Sinai** (הַר סִינַי, v. 16) is central to the chapter. The mountain is described with several levels of holiness: the people stand at the foot, the elders ascend to a middle level, and Moses alone goes to the top. This vertical structure reflects the gradation of holy space later formalized in the tabernacle and temple.

Moses builds an **altar at the foot of the mountain** (תַּחַת הָהָר, *tachat hahar*, v. 4) with **twelve stone pillars** (מַצֵּבָה, *matsevah*) representing the twelve tribes. Such stone pillars were common in Canaanite religion, but here they are used legitimately as covenant monuments – a practice later forbidden when associated with idolatry (Deuteronomy 16:22).

## Events and Customs

The **covenant ratification ritual** in this chapter follows a pattern known from ancient treaties. The blood is divided into two parts: half is thrown on the altar (representing God), and half on the people (vv. 6-8). This **blood ritual** (דַּם־הַבְּרִית, *dam habberit* – "the blood of the covenant") binds both parties in a life commitment. In Hebrew thought, blood represented life, and dividing the blood symbolized that the covenant partners now shared a common life.

The **covenant meal** where the elders "saw God, and they ate and drank" (v. 11) is remarkable. In the ancient Near East, it was customary for covenants to be confirmed with a shared meal. The fact that they saw God yet survived is highlighted as extraordinary – God's grace toward "the nobles of Israel" (אֲצִילֵי, *atsilei*).

**Moses' stay on the mountain** lasts forty days and forty nights (v. 18). The number forty appears frequently in the Bible as a period for testing, preparation, or transition (cf. the flood, the wilderness wandering, Jesus' fast).

## Key Terms

**"We will do and we will hear"** (נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע, *na'aseh venishma*, v. 7) is a famous formulation where action precedes understanding. Jewish tradition sees this as an expression of total devotion – Israel commits itself before fully grasping what it entails.

**"The Book of the Covenant"** (סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית, *sefer habberit*, v. 7) likely refers to the laws Moses has just written down (v. 4) and delivered. The expression shows that written documentation was central to covenant relationships, as it also was in Hittite and Mesopotamian treaties.

The **stone tablets** (לֻחֹת הָאֶבֶן, *luchot ha'even*, v. 12) which God promises to give Moses contain "the law and the commandment" that God Himself has written. This prepares the narrative for the Ten Commandments that Moses receives.

**A surface like lapis lazuli** (לִבְנַת הַסַּפִּיר, *livnat hassappir*, v. 10) describes the pavement under God's feet. The Hebrew סַפִּיר (*sappir*) probably refers to lapis lazuli, a deep blue gemstone that in the ancient Near East was associated with the divine and heaven.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Covenant documents from the second millennium BC, particularly Hittite vassal treaties, show striking parallels to the covenant ratification in this chapter: stipulations (laws), witnesses, and ritual confirmation with blood. This supports the text's historical credibility as an authentic covenant ceremony from the Bronze Age.

Archaeological discoveries of stone pillars (*massebot*) at several Canaanite cult sites, including Gezer and Hazor, confirm that such monuments were common covenant and memorial markers in the region.
