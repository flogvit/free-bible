# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 34

## People

**Zedekiah (צִדְקִיָּהוּ)** was the last king of Judah (597–586 B.C.), installed by Nebuchadnezzar after Jeconiah was taken into exile. His original name was Mattaniah, and he was the son of Josiah, making him Jeconiah’s uncle. The chapter portrays him in a desperate situation during the siege. The freeing of slaves may have been motivated both by religious considerations and practical reasons—freed slaves could be recruited to defend the city. The prophecy confirms that he would "see" the king of Babylon face to face and die in Babylon, which was later fulfilled when he was captured and taken away after his eyes were put out (2 Kings 25:7).

**Nebuchadnezzar (נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר)** is referred to here as the leader of a coalition of "all the kingdoms under his dominion." This reflects the structure of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, with vassal states required to contribute troops for military campaigns.

## Places

**Lachish and Azekah** (v. 7) are mentioned as the only remaining fortified cities in Judah besides Jerusalem. Both were strategically important fortress towns that protected access to Jerusalem from the southwest. Lachish was located about 45 km southwest of Jerusalem and controlled the road to Egypt, while Azekah lay in the Shephelah region.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Lachish Letters** are a collection of 21 ostraca (pottery shards with ink writing) discovered in 1935 and 1938 at Tell ed-Duweir (ancient Lachish). They date precisely to this period (588–586 B.C.) and contain military correspondence. Particularly relevant is ostracon number 4, which states: "We are watching for the signals from Lachish … for we cannot see Azekah." This has been interpreted as indicating that Azekah had already fallen, which aligns exactly with Jeremiah 34:7, which mentions these two cities as the last to hold out. The discovery provides remarkable extra-biblical confirmation of the historical situation described in the chapter.

## Events and Customs

**Manumission (דְּרוֹר - deror)** refers to the Mosaic law found in Exodus 21:2, Deuteronomy 15:12, and the Jubilee provision in Leviticus 25:10, which mandated the release of Hebrew slaves after six years of service. The word *deror* is related to the Akkadian *andurārum*, which denoted debt relief and manumission proclaimed by Mesopotamian kings at accessions. The use of this term connects the practice to a broader cultural custom in the ancient Near East, while in Israel it was rooted in the covenant with God.

**Covenant Making by Walking Between Animal Parts** (vv. 18–19) was an ancient ritual element associated with making covenants in the ancient Near East. By walking between the divided pieces of a sacrificial animal, the parties symbolically invoked the same fate upon themselves if they broke the covenant. This custom is also known from Genesis 15, where God makes a covenant with Abraham. The Hebrew expression "to cut a covenant" (כָּרַת בְּרִית) reflects precisely this ritual of cutting animals into pieces.

## Key Terms

**"Hail, O master!" (הוֹי אָדוֹן)** in verse 5 refers to the lament song that was to be sung at the funerals of kings. The verse also mentions "burnings" (מִשְׂרְפוֹת)—likely incense or ceremonial fires honoring the deceased—which were part of royal funeral ceremonies. The promise that Zedekiah would receive such a ceremony implied a peaceful, honorable death, as opposed to dying by the sword.

**"You have profaned my name" (וַתְּחַלְּלוּ אֶת־שְׁמִי)** in verse 16 expresses that the people’s breaking of their oath was not merely a violation against fellow humans, but an offense against God’s holiness. Since the covenant had been made in the temple "where my name is called" (v. 15), the breach became a direct insult to God Himself.
