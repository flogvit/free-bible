# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 17

## Places

The chapter mentions that Jesus traveled "through the region between Samaria and Galilee" (v. 11, Greek: διὰ μέσον Σαμαρείας καὶ Γαλιλαίας). This was an unusual route following the boundary line between the two regions eastward toward the Jordan Valley. The area was known for its mixed population, which explains how a group of ten lepers could include both Jews and a Samaritan. Normally these groups would not associate with one another, but the social stigma of disease created a fellowship across ethnic divides. The geography also emphasizes that Jesus is moving toward Jerusalem (explicitly mentioned in v. 11), which is a recurring theme in Luke's travel narrative.

## People

**The Samaritan leper** (v. 16) is described with the Greek word ἀλλογενής ("foreigner/outsider") in verse 18 – a word that otherwise appears only here in the New Testament. This same word was found on a stone inscription from the temple in Jerusalem warning non-Jews not to enter the inner courts. That it is precisely "the foreigner" who returns to give thanks, while the nine Jewish lepers do not, reflects a theme Luke often highlights: God's grace toward those on the outside.

## Events and Customs

**Leprosy and cleansing rituals:** The ten lepers "stood at a distance" (v. 12, πόρρωθεν) in accordance with the Mosaic Law (Leviticus 13:45-46), which required lepers to remain separated and call out their uncleanness. Jesus' command to show themselves to the priests (v. 14) refers to the cleansing procedure in Leviticus 14, where the priest was to inspect the healed person and perform a sacrifice. The healing occurred "while they were going" – that is, in faith before they had any visible proof. For the Samaritan, an interesting dilemma arose: Should he go to a Samaritan priest on Mount Gerizim or to Jerusalem? His return to Jesus suggests he recognized Jesus as the true authority.

**The duties of a slave** (vv. 7-10): The parable describes a δοῦλος (slave) working at plowing (ἀροτριῶντα) or shepherding (ποιμαίνοντα). In first-century Palestine, it was common for smaller households to have one slave who performed multiple tasks. After work in the fields, it was expected that the slave would first serve his master at the evening meal before eating himself. The point is that the slave does not deserve special thanks (χάρις) for doing his duty – a challenging teaching about the relationship between service and grace.

## Key Terms

**Σκάνδαλον** (vv. 1-2): The word originally means "stumbling block" or "trap" and is used of that which causes others to fall away from the faith. It was a powerful image in a culture where roads were often rocky and uneven.

**Λίθος μυλικός** (v. 2): A "millstone" – specifically the large upper stone of an ass-driven mill, significantly larger and heavier than hand mills. The image of being thrown into the sea with such a stone around one's neck was an extreme way to describe certain death, underscoring the seriousness of causing others to stumble.

**Συκάμινος** (v. 6): The mulberry tree (not to be confused with συκομορέα, the fig-mulberry) had an extremely deep root system and was known for being nearly impossible to uproot. Jesus' image is therefore deliberately hyperbolic.

**Ἐντὸς ὑμῶν** (v. 21): The expression "the kingdom of God is ἐντὸς ὑμῶν" can be translated either "within you" or "among you". The context – that Jesus is speaking to Pharisees – makes "among you" more likely: the kingdom of God is already present in Jesus' person and work.

## Chapter-Specific References

Jesus' references to the days of Noah and the flood (vv. 26-27) as well as Lot and Sodom (vv. 28-30) draw on well-known stories from Genesis. The brief command "Remember Lot's wife" (v. 32) assumes that listeners knew the story of how she became a pillar of salt when she looked back (Genesis 19:26). At the southern end of the Dead Sea, salt formations still exist which in tradition have been associated with this event. The image of two women who "are grinding together" (v. 35, ἀλήθουσαι) refers to the daily hand-milling of grain, usually carried out by women early in the morning – a scene of ordinary normalcy suddenly interrupted.
