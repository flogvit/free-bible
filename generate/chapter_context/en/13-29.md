# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 29

## People

**Jehiel the Gershonite** (verse 8) is mentioned as being responsible for the temple treasury. He belonged to the Gershonites, a Levitical clan descended from Gershom, the eldest son of Levi. The books of Chronicles show that the Levites had specialized roles, and the Gershonites were particularly responsible for managing the treasures and stores of the sanctuary (see 1 Chron 26:21–22).

**Zadok** (verse 22) was anointed as priest together with Solomon’s enthronement. This marked the formal confirmation of the Zadokite priestly line that would dominate temple service for centuries to come. Zadok’s anointing here legitimized his lineage as the rightful high priests.

## Events and Customs

**Double Enthronement**: Verse 22 notes that Solomon was made king "a second time." The first anointing occurred during a crisis when Adonijah attempted to seize power (1 Kings 1). This second, public ceremony was a solemn confirmation before the entire assembly of the people, which was important for securing broad legitimacy in ancient Israel.

**Voluntary Gifts for Temple Building**: The collection of gifts from the people and leaders follows a pattern known from the building of the tabernacle (Exod 35–36). The voluntary willingness to give (*hitnaddēb*) was a central principle—the sanctuary was to be built from gifts given "with a whole heart" (verse 9), not through forced taxation.

**Oath of Loyalty to the King**: The expression "gave their hand under" Solomon (verse 24) describes a formal gesture of submission that confirmed loyalty. This was an important political act that ensured a peaceful transfer of power.

## Key Terms

**Birāh** (בִּירָה, "citadel/palace"): This word in verses 1 and 19 is unusual in early texts and is usually used for Persian citadels (see Nehemiah, Esther). Here it describes the temple as God’s royal residence—a fortress for the heavenly King. The word emphasizes the temple as a majestic structure.

**Adarkonim** (אֲדַרְכֹנִים, "darics"): This monetary unit in verse 7 refers to Persian gold coins first minted under Darius I (around 515 B.C.). This is a clear anachronism showing that the author of Chronicles used the standards of his own time to make the sums understandable for his readers in the Persian period.

**"The throne of the Lord"** (verse 23): The description of Solomon’s throne as "the throne of the Lord" is theologically significant. It expresses the view in Chronicles that the Davidic kingdom was God’s representation on earth—the king sits as God’s representative.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Gold from Ophir** (verse 4): David’s gift included gold from Ophir, known in antiquity as the finest quality of gold. Gold from Ophir is mentioned in several biblical texts and has been confirmed by an ostracon from Tell Qasile near Jaffa, dated to the eighth century B.C., which mentions "gold of Ophir." This shows that trade with this exclusive gold was well established in the Iron Age. The exact location of Ophir is debated—proposed locations include southern Arabia, East Africa, or India.
