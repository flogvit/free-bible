# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 15

## People

**Miriam** is introduced here with the title "prophetess" (הַנְּבִיאָה, *hanneviah*) – the first woman in the Bible explicitly called this. She is called "Aaron's sister" (not Moses' sister), which may reflect Aaron's prominent role as priest or an older tradition. Miriam's leadership of the women's singing and dancing shows that women held important roles in Israelite worship. Her use of the tambourine (תֹּף, *tof*) and dance represents a common pattern in ancient Near Eastern victory celebrations.

## Places

**The Sea of Reeds** (יַם־סוּף, *Yam Suf*) literally means "Sea of Reeds" or "Reed Sea", not "Red Sea" as in later translations. The exact location is debated, but candidates include the Bitter Lakes or areas north of the Gulf of Suez.

**The Wilderness of Shur** (מִדְבַּר־שׁוּר) lay east of Egypt, along the northern Sinai Peninsula. This was a well-known trade route between Egypt and Canaan, often called "the Way of Shur".

**Marah** (מָרָה, "bitter") has been suggested to be located at Ain Hawarah, a brackish spring about 75 km southeast of Suez. **Elim**, with its twelve springs and seventy palm trees, has traditionally been identified with Wadi Gharandel, a fertile oasis in western Sinai.

## Events and Customs

**The Song of Victory** (שִׁירָה, *shirah*) in verses 1-18 represents one of the oldest poetic texts in the Bible, often called "the Song of Moses" or "the Song of the Sea". The genre "victory song" was widespread in the ancient Near East – Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Ugaritic texts contain similar hymns following military victories. Moses and the Israelites sing the main song, while Miriam then leads the women's responsive song with tambourines and dancing (vv. 20-21).

**Women's dance with tambourines** reflects an established tradition in which women celebrated military victories with music and dance (cf. Judg 11:34; 1 Sam 18:6-7). This was a recognized religious and social function for women in Israelite culture.

## Key Terms

**"The Lord is a warrior"** (יְהוָה אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, *YHWH ish milchamah*) – This expression presents God as a divine warrior, a concept central to ancient Near Eastern theology. Surrounding cultures had war gods (Baal, Marduk), but here it is Israel's God himself who fights.

**"Who is like you among the gods?"** (מִי־כָמֹכָה בָּאֵלִם, *mi kamocha ba'elim*) – The question proclaims YHWH's superiority. This has been interpreted in various ways – either as poetic rhetoric highlighting YHWH's incomparability, or as an expression of early Israelite theology where the existence of other gods is not explicitly denied.

**"Your holy dwelling"** (נְוֵה קָדְשֶׁךָ, *neveh qodshecha*) in verse 13, together with **"your inheritance mountain"** and **"sanctuary"** (מִקְּדָשׁ, *miqdash*) in verse 17, points forward to the land of Canaan and the future sanctuary that God will establish.

**"I am the Lord your healer"** (אֲנִי יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ, *ani YHWH rof'echa*) – God's self-presentation in verse 26 introduces an important theological theme: God as healer. This promise is linked to the people's obedience to God's commands and laws.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The poetic structure of the Song of Moses (vv. 1-18) shows archaic Hebrew features that many scholars consider signs of great age, but dating remains debated among researchers. Linguistic elements such as short verb forms and unusual word order have parallels in older Northwest Semitic poetry, including Ugaritic texts.

Egypt's use of chariots (מַרְכְּבֹת, *markevot*) and "picked officers" (שָׁלִשִׁים, *shalishim* – literally "third men", likely chariot warriors) is well documented in Egyptian sources from the New Kingdom (1550-1070 BC). The Hyksos period introduced chariots to Egypt, and they became central to Pharaoh's army.
