# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 7

## Characters

**Moses and Aaron** are presented here with their ages: Moses was 80 years old and Aaron 83 when they confronted Pharaoh (v. 7). In the ancient Near East, advanced age was associated with wisdom and authority. The ages also place the events within a concrete biographical framework. Moses' role is described using the Hebrew expression *elohim* (אֱלֹהִים, "as God") in relation to Pharaoh—this means that Moses was to function as God's representative with divine authority, while Aaron was to be his *navi* (prophet/spokesman).

**Pharaoh** is mentioned without a name. He surrounds himself with **hakhamim** (חֲכָמִים, wise men), **mekashfim** (מְכַשְּׁפִים, sorcerers/black magicians) and **hartummim** (חַרְטֻמִּים, enchanters/magicians). These categories reflect the religious hierarchy of the Egyptian court. Hartummim likely comes from the Egyptian *ḥry-tp*, a title for high priests who specialized in ritual magic and dream interpretation.

## Places

**The Nile** (*ye'or* יְאֹר in Hebrew, from Egyptian *itrw*) stands central to this chapter. The Nile was Egypt's lifeline—it provided water, food, and transportation. The river was personified as the god Hapi and was closely linked to Osiris. That Pharaoh went out to the water in the morning (v. 15) may refer to religious rituals where Pharaoh honored the Nile, or to practical purposes such as inspecting the water level.

## Events and Customs

**The first plague—blood** (vv. 14-25) strikes the Nile and all of Egypt's water sources. This was a direct attack on Egyptian religion: the Nile was worshipped as a life-giving deity, and water was essential for ritual purity. The fact that the hartummim could replicate the sign "with their secret arts" (*belahatehem* בְּלַהֲטֵיהֶם in v. 11, *belateihem* בְּלָטֵיהֶם in v. 22—two slightly different words in the original text) shows that Egyptian magic was recognized as real, but subordinate to God's power—as demonstrated when Aaron's staff swallowed up the magicians' staffs.

**The sign with the tannin** (vv. 9-12) has Egyptian resonance. Interestingly, v. 15 refers to the staff becoming "a snake" using the word *nahash* (נָחָשׁ), while vv. 9-10 use *tannin* (תַּנִּין). Pharaoh wore the uraeus cobra on his crown as a symbol of royal and divine power. That Aaron's staff became a *tannin* (great serpent/sea monster) and swallowed up the Egyptian magicians' staffs, symbolized Yahweh's superiority over Pharaoh's divine authority.

**The warning about the plague of frogs** (vv. 26-29) concludes the chapter with God's warning about the next plague if Pharaoh continues to refuse.

## Key Terms

**Tannin** (תַּנִּין) in verses 9-10 and 12 differs from *nahash* (נָחָשׁ, snake) used in 4:3 and referred to in 7:15. Tannin denotes a larger, more mythological creature—often translated "sea monster" or "dragon." In Egyptian and Canaanite mythology, such beings represented chaotic forces. The term emphasizes the cosmic dimension of the confrontation.

**Heart terminology** in this chapter: In verse 3 God says *aqsheh et-lev par'oh* ("I will harden Pharaoh's heart," from the verb *qashah* קשה, meaning to make hard/stiff). In verses 13 and 22, *chazaq* (חזק, "became strong/hard") is used regarding Pharaoh's heart. In verse 14, Pharaoh's heart is described as *kaved* (כָּבֵד, "heavy"). In Egyptian thought, the heart (*ib*) was the seat of will and intellect, and was weighed against the feather of truth in the judgment of the dead. A "heavy" heart was morally burdened.

**Mofet** (מוֹפֵת)—"sign" or "wonder"—was in the ancient Near East proof of legitimacy for divine authority. Pharaoh's demand for a sign (v. 9) followed standard diplomatic practice where envoys had to prove their divine backing.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian papyri describe magicians (*ḥry-tp*) who performed "secret arts" at the court. The Westcar Papyrus (c. 1600 BCE) tells of magicians performing wonders for Pharaoh, including transformations of objects. This confirms that the role of the Egyptian magicians in the text reflects actual Egyptian court practice.

The Ipuwer Papyrus (possibly from the Second Intermediate Period) contains a passage stating: "Truly, the river is blood. If one drinks from it, one rejects it as human and thirsts for water." Although the dating is disputed and a direct connection to Exodus is uncertain, the text shows that disasters related to the Nile's contamination were known in Egyptian literature. Red sediment from Ethiopian highlands may have periodically colored the Nile red, but the biblical description presents this as a supernatural divine sign.
