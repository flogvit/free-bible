# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 23

## Events and Customs

### Legal Practice and Witness Testimony
The opening laws of the chapter concerning justice in legal cases (vv. 1–8) reflect a developed legal culture in the ancient Near East. The prohibition against "bearing false report" (*שֵׁמַע שָׁוְא*, shema shav) and taking bribes has parallels in Egyptian wisdom texts and Mesopotamian law codes. The Laws of Hammurabi contain similar provisions regarding the responsibility of witnesses and penalties for false accusation. What is unique in Israelite law is its social dimension: protection of the poor (*dal* and *evjon*) in legal proceedings, and the command to help even an enemy’s animal.

### The Three Festivals
The three annual pilgrimage festivals (*שָׁלֹשׁ רְגָלִים*, shalosh regalim) described in verses 14–17 followed the agricultural cycle of Canaan’s climate. "The Festival of Unleavened Bread" (*chag hamatzot*) fell in the month of Aviv (March–April) at the barley harvest, "the Festival of Harvest" (*chag hakatzir*) came seven weeks later at the wheat harvest, and "the Festival of Ingathering" (*chag ha'asif*) marked the conclusion of the harvest in autumn. All neighboring peoples had harvest festivals, but Israel connected them to salvation history—especially the Exodus from Egypt.

### The Sabbath Year and Weekly Sabbath
The command to let the land rest every seventh year (vv. 10–11) is unique to Israel. While other cultures practiced fallow rotation for practical reasons, the Israelite *shemittah* system provided a theological and social rationale: the poor and wild animals were to eat what grew on its own. The social dimension of the Sabbath (v. 12) explicitly includes the ox, the donkey, the slave’s son, and the foreigner—a remarkable expansion of the principle of rest.

## Key Terms

### Ger – The Foreigner
The word *גֵּר* (ger) in verses 9 and 12 denotes a non-Israelite living permanently among the people. The reminder that "you know the soul of the foreigner, for you were foreigners in Egypt" uses the Hebrew *נֶפֶשׁ* (nefesh)—which encompasses the whole person’s inner life and experience. This empathetic argument for justice based on historical experience is characteristic of Israelite ethics.

### Cooking a Kid in Its Mother’s Milk
The prohibition in verse 19 (*לֹֽא־תְבַשֵּׁ֥ל גְּדִ֖י בַּחֲלֵ֥ב אִמּֽוֹ*) has been the subject of much discussion. Some scholars have viewed it as a reaction against a Canaanite fertility rite, others as an expression of respect for natural relationships. A Ugaritic text mentions preparing a kid in milk in a cultic context, which may support interpreting this as a command against idolatry. This prohibition was later developed in rabbinic tradition into the comprehensive ban on mixing meat and milk.

### Matsevot – Sacred Pillars
The *מַצֵּבֹת* (matsevot) that were to be broken down (v. 24) were standing stones used in Canaanite worship. Archaeological excavations at Hazor, Gezer, and other sites have uncovered such cultic pillars, often arranged in rows near sanctuaries. They were associated with Baal worship and other Canaanite deities.

## Locations

### The Promised Boundaries
Verse 31 describes the ideal boundaries of the promised land: "from the Sea of Reeds to the sea of the Philistines, and from the wilderness to the river." The Sea of Reeds (*yam suf*) is traditionally identified with the Red Sea or the Gulf of Aqaba in the south; "the sea of the Philistines" refers to the Mediterranean Sea in the west; the wilderness is the Negev/Sinai desert; and "the river" (*ha-nahar*) refers to the Euphrates in the northeast. This represents the maximum extent of the promised territory, which was partially realized during the reigns of David and Solomon.

### The Peoples of Canaan
The six peoples mentioned in verse 23—the Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Canaanites, Hivites, and Jebusites—represent the ethnic mosaic of Bronze Age Canaan. Archaeology confirms a composite population with diverse cultural traditions. The Hittites are connected to the great Hittite empire in Anatolia, while the Jebusites controlled Jerusalem until David’s conquest.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

### The Gezer Calendar and the Agricultural Cycle
The Gezer calendar, dated to approximately 925 BCE, is one of the oldest known Hebrew inscriptions and lists the activities of the agricultural year: olive harvest, sowing, late sowing, flax harvest, barley harvest, wheat harvest, vine pruning, and summer fruit. This calendar directly illuminates the three festivals in Exodus 23, all of which are tied to the agricultural cycle in the Israelite highlands.
