# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Peter 2

## People

The chapter mentions several Old Testament figures as examples. **Noah** (v. 5) is referred to as "a preacher of righteousness" (δικαιοσύνης κήρυκα), a tradition not found explicitly in Genesis but widespread in Jewish literature (cf. Josephus, Antiquities 1.74). The expression "the eighth" (ὄγδοον) refers to the fact that Noah was one of eight people who were saved.

**Lot** (vv. 7–8) is portrayed as a "righteous" (δίκαιον) man who was distressed by the depraved lifestyle of the lawless. This explicitly positive assessment of Lot goes beyond the more ambivalent portrait in the Genesis narrative and reflects Jewish tradition from the intertestamental period (cf. Wisdom of Solomon 10:6). **Balaam son of Beor** (vv. 15–16) is used as a typical example of a false prophet motivated by greed. The place name "Bozrah" (Βοσόρ) differs from "Beor" (the name of Balaam’s father in Numbers 22:5, LXX: Βεώρ). This may be due to a textual variant, an Aramaic form, or possibly a deliberate wordplay on the Hebrew *basar* (flesh/meat).

## Places

**Sodom and Gomorrah** (v. 6) serve as the classic example of God’s judgment upon ungodliness. The cities were located in the southern part of the Jordan Valley, likely in the area now covered by the southern basin of the Dead Sea. In Jewish and Christian tradition, the destruction of these cities became the foremost symbol of God’s judgment against sin.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes false teachers as "feasting together with" the congregation (συνευωχούμενοι, v. 13), probably a reference to the church’s **love feasts**. These were communal meals celebrated by early Christians, often in connection with the Lord’s Supper. Called *agape*, these meals were social gatherings where rich and poor were meant to eat together as equals. The "depraved conduct" of the false teachers during such meals represented a serious violation of the holy character of the community.

The mention of "turning aside from the right way" (v. 15) plays on the widespread use of "the Way" as a metaphor for Christian faith and practice in the early church. The first Christians were called "those who belonged to the Way" (Acts 9:2), and this chapter’s references to "the way of truth" (v. 2) and "the way of righteousness" (v. 21) connect with this self-understanding.

## Key Terms

**Tartarus** (ταρταρώσας, v. 4) is the only place in the Bible where this Greek term occurs. In Greek mythology, Tartarus was the deepest part of the underworld, a prison for the Titans and the place of eternal punishment. Peter’s use of this term for the dwelling place of the fallen angels shows how early Christian writers could employ well-known Hellenistic terminology to communicate theological points to a Greek-speaking audience. The parallel with 1 Enoch 10:4–6 suggests that Peter draws upon widespread Jewish traditions concerning the fall of the angels.

**Haireses** (αἱρέσεις, v. 1), translated "false doctrines" or "heresies", originally meant simply "choices" or "schools of thought" (such as philosophical schools). In a Christian context, the word gradually acquired a negative meaning denoting divisive, deviant teachings. The phrase "destructive heresies" (αἱρέσεις ἀπωλείας) underscores the serious consequences of such teaching.

**Pleonexia** (πλεονεξία, vv. 3, 14), "greed" or "covetousness", was a vice strongly condemned in both Jewish and Greco-Roman moral philosophy. The term implies not only material greed but also a general attitude of wanting more than one’s rightful share, often at the expense of others.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The proverb about the dog returning to its own vomit (v. 22) quotes Proverbs 26:11, while the proverb about the sow wallowing in the mud is not found in the Old Testament. This latter proverb is known from Syriac wisdom tradition (the proverbs of Ahikar) and was widespread in the Hellenistic world. The combination of a biblical and a non-biblical proverb shows how New Testament authors could draw upon broader wisdom traditions. Both animals—the dog and the sow—were considered unclean in Jewish context, which strengthens the rhetorical effect of the comparison.
