# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 34

## People

**Josiah** (Heb. יֹאשִׁיָּהוּ, "the Lord supports") became king of Judah at the age of eight after his father Amon was murdered in a conspiracy. This chapter describes his religious reform, which began in his eighth year as king and culminated with the discovery of the Book of the Law in his eighteenth year.

**Hilkiah** (חִלְקִיָּהוּ, "the Lord's portion") was the high priest and played a key role in finding the Book of the Law. He belonged to a priestly line that can be traced back to Zadok. **Shaphan** served as the scribe (סּוֹפֵר) and was an influential official; his family remained important during Judah's final decade. **Huldah** is one of the few female prophets mentioned in the Old Testament. The fact that the king sent a delegation to her shows she held significant authority as a prophetic voice in Jerusalem.

The **overseers** mentioned in verse 12 include Jahath and Obadiah from the Merari clan, and Zechariah and Meshullam from the Kohathite clan. These Levites were responsible for leading the restoration work on the temple.

## Places

**Mishneh** (הַמִּשְׁנֶה, "the second [quarter]") where Huldah lived refers to a newer district in Jerusalem, likely the western expansion of the city built during King Hezekiah's reign to house refugees from the north after Samaria fell in 722 B.C. Archaeological excavations have confirmed significant urban expansion during this period.

The chapter also mentions Josiah's reform activities in **Manasseh, Ephraim, Simeon, and Naphtali**—areas that had belonged to the northern kingdom of Israel. The phrase "in their ruins" (בְּחַרְבֹתֵיהֶם) in verse 6 suggests these areas still bore the marks of destruction from the Assyrian conquest more than a century earlier.

## Events and Customs

**The discovery of the Book of the Law** in the temple (v. 14) is one of the most discussed events in the Old Testament. Many scholars identify this book with a form of Deuteronomy, based on the reform's focus aligning with Deuteronomy's emphasis on centralized worship. The book's "loss" can be explained by the neglect of the temple during Manasseh and Amon's periods of idolatry. The custom of storing important documents in sanctuaries was common in ancient times.

**The renewal of the covenant** (vv. 29–32) followed a pattern known from Old Testament tradition: solemn reading of the law followed by the people's commitment. The king's position "on his place" (עַל־עָמְדוֹ) likely refers to a special platform or pillar at the temple entrance where the king stood on official occasions, also mentioned in connection with Joash (2 Chron 23:13).

**Tearing of garments** (v. 19) was a traditional expression of grief, horror, or repentance in Israelite culture. Josiah's reaction shows he understood the seriousness of the message about God's judgment.

## Key Terms

**Hammim** (הַֽחַמָּנִים) in verses 4 and 7 were incense altars, possibly linked to sun worship. The word is derived from "chamah" (heat/sun). These are distinct from ordinary altars and represent Canaanite-Phoenician cultic practices that had infiltrated Israelite religion.

**Asherahs** (אֲשֵׁרִים) were cultic poles or stylized trees associated with the fertility goddess Asherah. Archaeological finds, including inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud mentioning "Yahweh and his Asherah," show that Asherah worship was widespread in Israel and Judah, even among those who worshipped Yahweh.

**Torat YHWH** (תּוֹרַת־יְהוָה, "the law/instruction of the Lord") in verse 14 describes the book that was found. The phrase "by the hand of Moses" (בְּיַד־מֹשֶׁה) emphasizes the book's authority as Mosaic.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Bullae (clay seals) bearing the names of individuals mentioned in Josiah's administration have been found in Jerusalem, including a seal attributed to "Gemariah son of Shaphan" (Shaphan's son mentioned in Jer 36:10). This confirms the historicity of the officials mentioned in the narrative.
