## Places

**Ai (העי)** was a Canaanite city east of Bethel, whose name means "ruin mound" in Hebrew. The city was strategically located on the ascent from the Jordan Valley to the central highlands. Archaeologically, Ai is traditionally identified with et-Tell, about 3 kilometers southeast of Bethel. Excavations at et-Tell have revealed that the site was uninhabited for much of the Bronze Age, leading to debate over its exact location. Alternative suggestions include Khirbet el-Maqatir. The chapter describes the city as relatively small compared with Jericho, with a population of 12,000 (v. 25).

**Bethel (בית־אל)** is mentioned as a reference point for the position of the ambush force (vv. 9, 12, 17). The city, whose name means "house of God," had deep patriarchal significance from Jacob's experience (Gen 28). It lay west of Ai, and the two cities appear to have had close ties, since the men of Bethel also participated in pursuing Israel (v. 17).

**Mount Ebal (הר עיבל)** and **Mount Gerizim (הר גריזים)** are located near Shechem, about 30–35 kilometers north of Ai. These mountains form a natural amphitheater-like formation with the valley of Shechem between them. Here Joshua built an altar and carried out the renewal of the covenant (vv. 30–35). Archaeological investigations on Mount Ebal (in the 1980s, led by Adam Zertal) uncovered a stone structure from the early Iron Age that some scholars have suggested could be Joshua's altar, but the interpretation remains debated.

## Events and Customs

**Military strategy and ambush** were recognized military tactics in the ancient Near East. Unlike the conquest of Jericho, which was purely miraculous, the battle at Ai involved human strategy approved by God. A false retreat to draw out the enemy was a known tactic in ancient warfare, documented in Egyptian and Mesopotamian sources.

**Hanging on a tree** (v. 29) was a form of public disgrace inflicted upon enemies. According to Deut 21:22–23, the body had to be taken down before sunset to avoid defiling the land. Joshua's adherence to this shows respect for the Mosaic law. The pile of stones over the king's body served as a lasting memorial of defeat.

**Renewal of the covenant at Ebal and Gerizim** (vv. 30–35) fulfilled Moses' command from Deut 27. The ceremony included building an altar of uncut stones, offering burnt offerings and peace offerings, inscribing the law on stones, and reading aloud the blessings and curses. This ritual confirmed Israel's covenant relationship with God upon entering the promised land.

## Key Terms

**Kidon (כידון)** (vv. 18, 26) is often translated as "spear" or "javelin," but can also mean "sabre" or a type of short sword. Joshua's raised weapon served as a signal to the ambush force and as a symbol of God's leadership in battle, parallel to Moses' raised hands/staff during the fight against the Amalekites (Exod 17:11–12).

**Herem (החרים)** (v. 26) refers to "devotion" or total destruction dedicated to God. At Ai, the Israelites were allowed to keep the plunder and livestock (vv. 2, 27), in contrast to Jericho where everything was to be destroyed. This shows that the principle of herem could vary according to God's specific instructions.

**Tel olam (תל עולם)** – "everlasting ruin mound" (v. 28) – describes the result of the destruction. The term *tel* was used for artificial mounds formed by repeated layers of settlement, and Ai became a permanent monument of God's judgment.
