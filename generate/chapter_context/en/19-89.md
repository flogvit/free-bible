# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 89

## People

**Ethan the Ezrahite** (הָאֶזְרָחִי) is mentioned in the superscription as the author of this psalm. According to 1 Kings 5:11, Ethan was one of the wise men whose wisdom was surpassed by Solomon’s. He likely belonged to a Levitical family of singers, and 1 Chronicles 15:19 mentions an Ethan among the Levites whom David appointed to lead worship with cymbals. The designation "Ezrahite" may link him to the lineage of Zerah from the tribe of Judah (1 Chron 2:6), or it could be a variant connecting him to a Levitical singing family.

**David** stands at the center of the psalm as the recipient of God’s covenant and promises (vv. 4–5, 21, 36, 50). The psalm refers to his anointing with holy oil and God’s promise of an eternal dynasty. The current king is referred to as "your anointed" (meshiakh), showing how the Davidic covenant was understood as applying to each successive king in David’s line.

## Places

**Tabor and Hermon** are mentioned in verse 13 as mountains that rejoice over God’s name. Tabor lies in the northern part of the Jezreel Valley and rises 575 meters above sea level as a distinctive dome-shaped hill. Hermon is the highest mountain in the region (2,814 m) and is located far north in Israel at the border with Lebanon and Syria. By mentioning these two mountains—one along the south-north axis of the land and one at the extreme north—the psalmist represents the entire geography of the land as testifying to God’s greatness.

**The sea and the rivers** (v. 26) refer to God’s promise that the anointed king’s dominion will extend from the Mediterranean Sea in the west to the rivers (likely the Euphrates) in the east, describing the ideal boundaries of Israel’s kingdom as promised to David and Solomon.

## Events and Customs

The psalm likely reflects a national catastrophe in which the Davidic dynasty suffered a serious defeat. The description of ruined walls, plundering, and the king’s disgrace (vv. 39–46) fits either the Babylonian invasions of the sixth century B.C.E. or an earlier military defeat. The dramatic contrast between God’s eternal promises (vv. 4–38) and the present reality (vv. 39–52) makes this psalm a lament that reminds God of his covenant obligations.

**The royal crown** (Hebrew *nezer*, v. 40) was a visible symbol of royal dignity and God’s choice. That the crown is "profaned to the ground" describes the total humiliation of the anointed king, and thus an apparent overturning of God’s promises.

## Key Terms

**Khesed** (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love/faithful love") and **emunah** (אֱמוּנָה, "faithfulness") are the dominant theological terms in this psalm. Khesed appears seven times (vv. 2, 3, 15, 25, 29, 34, 50), while emunah appears six times (vv. 2, 3, 6, 9, 34, 50). These words describe God’s covenantal loyalty and reliability, and the entire psalm revolves around the tension between these divine attributes and the present crisis.

**Rahab** (רָהַב, v. 11) here refers to a mythological sea monster symbolizing chaotic forces. In ancient Near Eastern thought, the sea and its monsters represented the chaotic powers that threatened creation. By declaring that God has crushed Rahab, the psalmist confesses God’s sovereign power over all chaotic forces—a confession that makes the present crisis even more incomprehensible.

**Sod kedoshim** (סוֹד־קְדֹשִׁים, "the council of the holy ones," v. 8) refers to the heavenly assembly of angels and spiritual beings surrounding God’s throne. This idea of a heavenly council is also found in Job 1–2 and Isaiah 6.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The so-called **Tel Dan inscription** from the ninth century B.C.E., discovered in 1993–94, contains the expression "House of David" (*bytdwd*) and confirms the existence of a dynasty linked to David. This is relevant for Psalm 89 because it shows that the Davidic dynasty was a historical reality recognized even by neighboring peoples. The inscription, erected by an Aramean king to celebrate victory over Israel, illustrates precisely the kind of humiliation against which the psalm laments.
