## Places

In verse 19, two place names are mentioned: **Tema** (תֵּמָא) and **Saba** (שְׁבָא). Tema was an oasis and trading post in northern Arabia, known as a crossroads for caravans crossing the desert. The area is mentioned several times in the Old Testament and was inhabited by descendants of Ishmael (Gen 25:15). Saba (Sheba) refers to southern Arabia, present-day Yemen, famous for its wealth from trade in incense, gold, and precious stones. Job’s reference to caravans from these places illustrates how travelers could be disappointed when expected water sources failed, reinforcing the book’s setting in the eastern, non-Israelite world.

## Events and Customs

The image of **wadis** (seasonal riverbeds) in verses 15–20 was immediately recognizable to people in the Middle East. In a desert climate, a wadi could be full of water after winter rains but completely dry in the summer heat. Travelers and caravans planning their routes based on the expectation of water could die if the wadi was empty when they arrived. Job uses this image to describe the unreliability of friends—they are available when all is well, but disappear when one truly needs them. The custom of offering **chesed** (חָסֶד, "steadfast love" or "loyalty") to a friend in distress was a fundamental social obligation in the ancient Middle East, and Job’s complaint in verse 14 shows that his friends have broken this deeply rooted cultural expectation.

## Key Terms

The word **חַלָּמוּת** (challamut) in verse 6, often translated as "egg white," "plant slime," or "mallow juice," is a rare Hebrew word that appears only here in the Bible. Its meaning is uncertain, but the context suggests something tasteless and unappetizing. Job’s point is that suffering without meaning is just as impossible to swallow as food without flavor.

The phrase **בִּעוּתֵי אֱלוֹהַּ** (bi'utei eloah, "terrors of God") in verse 4 describes an experience of divine terrorizing. In ancient Near Eastern thought, deities could send disease and disaster as punishment or testing. Job experiences his suffering as divine "arrows" and "terrors"—war imagery that portrays a hostile God rather than a protective one.

The expression **אִמְרֵי קָדוֹשׁ** (imrei qadosh, "the words of the Holy One") in verse 10 refers to God’s revealed will and commands. Job claims that he has not hidden or denied these words, which underscores his innocence and makes his suffering even more incomprehensible within the traditional framework of retribution.
