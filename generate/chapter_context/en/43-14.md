# Historical and Cultural Context for John 14

## People

**Thomas** (v. 5) appears here as the disciple who dares to ask the direct question that the others may also be thinking. His question about the way reveals a literal understanding typical of the dialogues in the Gospel of John.

**Philip** (vv. 8–9) receives a gentle rebuke from Jesus. His request to see the Father reflects a Jewish longing for a theophany, similar to Moses' desire in Exodus 33:18. Jesus' response – "Τοσούτῳ χρόνῳ μεθʼ ὑμῶν εἰμι" (so long a time I have been with you) – emphasizes that the time spent together should have been sufficient to understand his relationship with the Father.

**Judas, not Iscariot** (v. 22) is likely identical to "Thaddaeus" or "Lebbeus" in the synoptic lists of apostles. John specifies "οὐχ ὁ Ἰσκαριώτης" (not Iscariot) to distinguish him from the traitor who had left the room earlier that evening (13:30). His question reflects the widespread Jewish expectation of a Messiah who would reveal himself to the whole world in power and glory.

## Events and Customs

The chapter takes place during the last meal, which in John's chronology occurs on the evening before Passover (cf. 13:1; 18:28). The intimate setting with reclining at the table created an atmosphere for deep conversation. Jesus uses this setting for his longest continuous teaching in the Gospel.

The expression "ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ τοῦ πατρός μου" (in my Father's house, v. 2) with "many rooms" (μοναὶ πολλαί) may have evoked associations with the temple, which is also called "my Father's house" in John 2:16. At the same time, Jewish apocalyptic literature used the image of heavenly dwellings for the righteous. Jesus combines familiar images in a new way.

## Key Terms

**Παράκλητος** (parakletos, vv. 16, 26) is a central term that is difficult to translate with a single word. The term comes from legal language and denotes one who is called to someone's side to help – an advocate, spokesperson, or defender. In v. 16, Jesus calls the Spirit "another παράκλητος," implying that Jesus himself has been the first. In the Gospel of John, the term is used only in the farewell discourse (14:16, 26; 15:26; 16:7), while 1 John 2:1 uses it to refer to Jesus himself. The Holy Spirit is presented as the one who will continue Jesus' presence and work among the disciples.

**Εἰρήνη** (eirene, v. 27) corresponds to the Hebrew "shalom" and was the common Jewish greeting. But Jesus clarifies that his peace is qualitatively different from the world's ("not as the world gives"). In Jewish thought, shalom included not only the absence of conflict but complete well-being, harmony, and right relationship with God. Jesus' peace is rooted in the relationship with the Father and remains independent of external circumstances.

**Ὁ τοῦ κόσμου ἄρχων** (the ruler of this world, v. 30) is a designation for Satan that reflects contemporary Jewish demonology. In intertestamental literature and the Qumran texts, we find similar ideas about an evil spiritual power ruling over the present world. Jesus acknowledges the existence of this power but declares its powerlessness against himself.
