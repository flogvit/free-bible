# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 6

## People

**Elisha’s servant** is mentioned in verses 15–17 without a name. Tradition sometimes identifies him with Gehazi, but this is problematic given that Gehazi was struck with leprosy in chapter 5:27. The servant represents the ordinary believer who needs spiritual insight to see God’s protection.

**Ben-Hadad, king of Aram** (verse 24) was likely Ben-Hadad II or possibly Ben-Hadad III, who reigned in Damascus. The name "Ben-Hadad" (בֶּן־הֲדַד, "son of Hadad") was a dynastic title linked to the Aramean storm god Hadad. Assyrian inscriptions from this period mention several conflicts with Aramean kings.

**The sons of the prophets** (בְנֵי־הַנְּבִיאִים, "sons of the prophets") mentioned at the beginning were a group living in community under Elisha’s leadership. Their need for larger living quarters testifies to the growth of their movement.

## Places

**The Jordan Valley** (verses 2–4) was covered with dense forest in biblical times, containing many tree species suitable for construction. The area was significantly more wooded than it is today, making it natural to obtain building materials there.

**Dothan** (דֹתָן, verse 13) lay about 15 kilometers north of Samaria along an important trade route. Tell Dothan has been archaeologically excavated and shows continuous settlement from the Bronze Age. The site is located in a fertile valley surrounded by hills, which explains the military strategy of encirclement.

**Samaria** (שֹׁמְרוֹן) was the capital of the Northern Kingdom, founded by Omri around 880 B.C. The city was heavily fortified and situated on a hill that made it difficult to capture. Archaeological excavations at Tell el-Sultan/Sebastiye have uncovered massive city walls and royal structures from this period.

## Events and Customs

**Borrowing tools** (verse 5) was common practice. That the axe head was "borrowed" (שָׁאוּל) explains the servant’s despair—he was responsible for replacing the lost tool, which could have been financially devastating for a poor prophet’s disciple.

**Siege warfare**, described from verse 24 onward, was standard military practice in ancient times. The goal was to starve the population into surrender. The grotesque prices mentioned in verse 25 illustrate the desperation: a donkey’s head (normally considered unclean) cost eighty shekels of silver, and a quarter kab of "dove droppings" (חֲרִיּוֹנִים/דִּבְיוֹנִים – the ketiv/qere variant דִּבְיוֹנִים may refer to a type of legume or onion, alternatively literally droppings used as fuel) cost five shekels.

**Cannibalism during siege** (verses 28–29) was the ultimate consequence of extreme famine and is documented in other ancient texts. Moses had warned about this as a curse for disobedience (Deuteronomy 28:53–57), giving the event theological significance.

**Sackcloth beneath garments** (verse 30) shows that the king was already practicing mourning and repentance rituals in secret, even though he publicly appeared capable of action.

## Key Terms

**Terror** (סַנְוֵרִים, verse 18) is translated as "blindness" and occurs only here and in Genesis 19:11. The word probably does not describe total blindness but rather a disorientation or confusion that prevented the soldiers from recognizing their surroundings and Elisha.

**"Open his eyes"** (פְּקַח־נָא אֶת־עֵינָיו, verse 17) refers to spiritual sight that reveals invisible reality—chariots of fire and horses symbolize God’s heavenly armies.

**"Son of a murderer"** (בֶּן־הַֽמְרַצֵּחַ, verse 32) is Elisha’s description of the king (likely Joram), probably referring to the crimes of his parents Ahab and Jezebel.
