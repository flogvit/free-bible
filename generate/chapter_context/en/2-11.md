# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 11

## People

Moses is described in verse 3 as "very great in the land of Egypt" (גָּדוֹל מְאֹד בְּאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם). This expression indicates that he had attained a position of significant influence and respect, both among Pharaoh's servants and the Egyptian people in general. In Egyptian court tradition, such recognition of a foreigner was unusual, and the text highlights this as a result of God's intervention. Pharaoh remains unnamed in the text, which may reflect a literary style that emphasizes his role rather than his identity.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the Israelites' request for silver and gold items from the Egyptians (verse 2). The Hebrew verb in the form וְיִשְׁאֲלוּ (from the root שָׁאַל, sha'al) primarily means "to ask" or "to inquire," indicating a request rather than a loan with an expectation of return. This practice can be understood in light of ancient Near Eastern tradition, where slaves or servants who were freed had the right to compensation or gifts from their masters.

The concept of the firstborn (בְּכוֹר, bekhor) held great significance in the ancient Middle East. The firstborn son inherited a double portion and carried the family's honor. In Egyptian royal ideology, Pharaoh's firstborn son was the heir to the throne and divine status. That the plague strikes "from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne, to the firstborn of the female slave who is behind the handmill" (verse 5), shows a complete social spectrum—from the highest to the lowest rank in society.

## Key Terms

The word נֶגַע (nega') in verse 1, translated "plague," has a broader meaning than just disease. It denotes a blow or touch from God—a divine intervention with a punitive character. The word is later used concerning leprosy and other conditions requiring ritual attention.

The expression כַּחֲצֹת הַלַּיְלָה (kachatsot halayla) in verse 4, "at midnight," is significant. The word חֲצֹת is derived from the root חצה meaning "to divide," and refers literally to the midpoint of the night. Midnight was in Egyptian and Mesopotamian tradition a time when supernatural forces were especially active.

The expression לֹא יֶחֱרַץ־כֶּלֶב לְשֹׁנוֹ (verse 7), "not even a dog shall sharpen its tongue," is an idiom for complete silence and peace. Dogs were common in Egyptian settlements and reacted to disturbances. That not even the dogs would show aggression toward the Israelites underscores the total difference between Egypt's chaos and Israel's protection.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian texts confirm that midnight and night in general were considered dangerous times when chaotic forces threatened the world order. Several Egyptian hymns and incantations focus on protection through the night. This cultural background makes the timing of the tenth plague especially meaningful—Israel's God demonstrates power precisely when Egyptians feared chaotic forces most.
