# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 41

## Key Terms

Psalm 41 contains several Hebrew expressions that require cultural explanation. The word **"maskil"** (מַשְׂכִּיל) in verse 2, a participle form of the verb "sakal" meaning "to understand, to act wisely," denotes more than merely noticing the poor—it implies insightful, practical action. The term **"dal"** (דָּל) refers to the weak, helpless, or poor, a category of people who stood under special divine protection in Israelite law and ethics.

The expression **"debar-beliyya'al"** (דְּבַר־בְּלִיַּעַל) in verse 9, often translated as "a deadly disease" or "an evil thing," literally means "a word/cause of Belial"—something destructive and wicked. This expression is otherwise used concerning evil and moral corruption. The enemies claim that the psalmist has been struck by an irreparable curse.

## Events and Customs

The psalm reflects a practice where illness was interpreted as possible divine punishment for sin, something the psalmist himself acknowledges as a possibility in verse 5. At the same time, verses 7–8 describe the custom of "visiting the sick"—going to see the ill person—but reveal how such visits could be misused to gather gossip and wait for the sick person's death. The Hebrew expression "to eat someone's bread" (verse 10) was a strong cultural symbol of covenant fellowship and loyalty, making the betrayal by a trusted friend especially serious.

## Literary Features

Verse 14 marks the conclusion of the first book of Psalms (Psalms 1–41) with a doxology—a formula of praise. The double "amen" (אָמֵן וְאָמֵן) serves as liturgical confirmation and congregational response at the end of the book.
