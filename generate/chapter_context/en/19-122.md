## Places

### Jerusalem

The psalm describes Jerusalem as the destination of pilgrims. The description of the city as "built together as one" (verse 3) uses the Hebrew expression *she-chubberah-lah yachdav*, which suggests a tightly knit, cohesive city where houses and walls form a unified whole. This reflects Jerusalem's topography on the ridge where the City of David was located, with concentrated and compact settlement.

The city gates (verse 2) were central meeting places for trade, legal proceedings, and social interaction. Pilgrims who "stood at the gates" experienced the transition from journey to arrival right there.

## Events and Customs

### The Pilgrimage and the Gathering of the Tribes

Verse 4 describes how "the tribes go up" (*alu shevatim*) to Jerusalem. This refers to the required pilgrimage festivals three times a year (Exodus 23:17; Deuteronomy 16:16), where Israelite men were to "appear before the Lord." The expression "testimony for Israel" (*edut le-yisrael*) points to how this collective worship served as visible evidence of the people's covenant relationship with God. The gathering of the tribes strengthened national unity and religious identity.

## Key Concepts

### Shalom (שָׁלוֹם)

The word *shalom* appears four times in the psalm (verses 6–8), creating a clear wordplay with the city name *Yerushalayim*. The name Jerusalem (*Yerushalayim*) has traditionally been interpreted as "foundation of peace" or "city of peace," although its etymological origin is debated. *Shalom* encompasses more than the absence of war—it includes prosperity, wholeness, security, and harmonious relationships. The exhortation to "pray for the peace of Jerusalem" (*sha'alu shalom*) uses a verb that also means "to ask after someone's welfare," indicating active care for the well-being of the city.

### David's House (בֵּית דָּוִיד)

"David's house" (verse 5) refers to the Davidic dynasty and its judicial authority. The courts in Jerusalem represented the highest court of appeal, and the king or his appointees served as supreme judges. This links religious worship with civil justice—both had their seat in Jerusalem.
