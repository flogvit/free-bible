# Historical and Cultural Context for Amos 2

## Places

**Moab and Kerijoth (vv. 1–3):** Moab was a kingdom east of the Dead Sea, on the high plateau that today lies in Jordan. Kerijoth (הַקְּרִיּוֹת) can be understood either as a specific place name or as a common noun meaning "the cities" (with the definite article). If it is a place name, it was likely one of Moab's towns. The Mesha Stele (ca. 840 B.C.), discovered in 1868, mentions several Moabite cities and confirms the historical existence of this kingdom. The Moabites were related to the Israelites through Lot (Gen 19:37) and had a long history of both conflict and interaction with Israel.

**Jerusalem (v. 5):** The capital city of the kingdom of Judah is mentioned as the target of God's judgment. In Amos' time, Jerusalem was an important religious and political center, although it was less wealthy than the cities of the northern kingdom.

## Events and Customs

**Burning bones to lime (v. 1):** The accusation against Moab concerns their burning of Edom's king's bones to lime (לַשִּׂיד). In the ancient Near East, respectful burial of the dead was of utmost importance. Digging up and burning a king's remains until they turned to dust was an extreme act of desecration, intended to completely erase the person's memory and prevent any form of honor after death. This custom of dishonoring enemies' graves is documented in several ancient texts.

**Debt slavery and pledging of clothing (vv. 6–8):** Amos describes how the poor were sold for trivial sums—even for a pair of sandals. This reflects the development of a debt-ridden society in Israel, where farmers unable to pay their debts were forced to sell themselves or family members into slavery. The law in Exodus 22:25–26 forbade keeping a poor person's cloak overnight as a pledge, since it was his only source of warmth. That people lay on pledged garments at the altar (v. 8) shows how religious worship had become mixed with social injustice.

**Sexual immorality and desecration (v. 7):** The expression "a man and his father go to the same young woman" (הַנַּעֲרָה) may refer to sexual exploitation of servant girls or possibly to cultic prostitution associated with Canaanite fertility worship. Regardless of interpretation, this dishonored God's holy name by combining sexual immorality with religious practice.

## Key Terms

**Nazirites (נְזִירִים, vv. 11–12):** The Nazirites were individuals who had taken a special vow to God, described in Numbers 6. They were to abstain from wine and all grape products, not cut their hair, and avoid contact with dead bodies. Samson and Samuel are well-known examples. That the Israelites gave Nazirites wine to drink represented a direct attack on this holy institution and showed contempt for God's ordinances.

**Amorites (הָאֱמֹרִי, vv. 9–10):** The Amorites are used here as a collective term for the pre-Israelite population of Canaan. Their description as tall as cedar trees and strong as oaks reflects the tradition concerning the giant peoples of Canaan (Num 13:32–33). By reminding Israel how God destroyed this mighty people, Amos emphasizes that Israel's conquest of the land was God's work, not their own merit.

**Torah and statutes (תּוֹרַת יְהוָה וְחֻקָּיו, v. 4):** The accusation against Judah uses technical terms for God's revealed will. "Torah" denotes God's instruction and law, while "huqqim" (statutes) refers to the established decrees. This shows that Judah had a codified legal system they were obligated to follow.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from Samaria, including ostraca and ivory inlays from the 8th century B.C., confirm the economic prosperity among the upper class that enabled the social injustice described in this chapter—where the poor were sold for as little as a pair of sandals (v. 6).
