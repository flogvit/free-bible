## Places

**Zion and the holy mountain** (vv. 1, 15, 23) refers to Jerusalem and the temple mount as the center of the nation's spiritual life and the place where the people were to gather for sacred assemblies. The blowing of the shofar "on Zion" underscores the central significance of this location.

**"Between the porch and the altar"** (v. 17) describes a specific area within the temple complex—the space between the entrance of the temple building (אוּלָם) and the bronze altar in the courtyard. This was the priests' sacred work area and a natural place for intercession on behalf of the people. The location emphasizes the solemn and official character of prayer.

## Events and Customs

**Blowing the shofar** (vv. 1, 15) was a central part of Israel's religious and civil life. Made from a ram's horn, the shofar was used to signal danger, call people to war, inaugurate festivals, and proclaim sacred assemblies. Here it is used to summon the people to repentance and to declare a national day of fasting and prayer.

**A holy fast and assembly** (vv. 15–16) describes a formal, ritual gathering in which the entire community—from elders to infants—participated. The expression "consecrate a fast" (קַדְּשׁוּ־צוֹם) indicates that fasting was a formal, ritual act requiring preparation and dedication. The fact that even newlyweds were to leave their bridal chambers (v. 16) shows the seriousness of the crisis—normally, newly married couples were exempt from public obligations during the first year (cf. Deut 24:5).

**Tearing clothes** (v. 13) was a common expression of grief in the ancient Near East. Joel urges people to "tear your hearts" instead of their garments, marking a contrast between outward rituals and inner repentance. This reflects the prophets' repeated criticism of empty formalism.

## Key Terms

**"The day of the Lord"** (יוֹם־יְהוָה, vv. 1, 11) is a central concept in this chapter. It describes a time when God intervenes directly in history—both to judge enemies and injustice, and to bring salvation to His people. The day is presented as near and awe-inspiring, yet offering the possibility of grace through repentance.

**"The northern one"** (הַצְּפוֹנִי, v. 20) is an unusual expression that has been interpreted in various ways. It may refer to locust swarms that often came from the north, or to hostile armies—since Israel's historical enemies frequently invaded from the north. The image of the enemy being driven toward "the eastern sea" (הַיָּם הַקַּדֹּמִי, the Dead Sea) and "the western sea" (הַיָּם הָאַחֲרוֹן, the Mediterranean Sea) describes complete destruction.

**"The early rain and the latter rain"** (גֶּשֶׁם מוֹרֶה וּמַלְקוֹשׁ, v. 23) were crucial for agriculture in Israel. The early rain came in autumn (October–November), softening the ground for plowing and sowing. The latter rain came in spring (March–April), ensuring the ripening of crops. The promise of both rainy seasons symbolizes the full restoration of the land's fertility.

**"My great army"** (חֵילִי הַגָּדוֹל, v. 25) is used to describe the locusts, showing that Joel understands this natural disaster as God's instrument of judgment. The four names for locusts in this verse (אַרְבֶּה, יֶלֶק, חָסִיל, גָּזָם)—the same list found in 1:4 but in a different order—emphasize the total extent of the destruction.
