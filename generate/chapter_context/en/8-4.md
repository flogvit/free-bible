## Places

The city gate (הַשַּׁעַר, *hashsha'ar*) in verse 1 was the central location for legal and commercial transactions in ancient Israel. The city gate was not merely an entrance but a complex with chambers and seating areas where the city elders gathered to handle lawsuits, make contracts, and witness agreements. Archaeological excavations at several Israelite cities, including Dan, Beersheba, and Lachish, have uncovered city gate halls with stone benches along the walls—likely seats for the elders. In Bethlehem, the city gate would have been the natural place for Boaz to carry out the legal process described in this chapter.

## Events and Customs

**The Right of Redemption (גְּאֻלָּה, *ge'ullah*)**: This chapter describes the legal procedure for redeeming family land. According to Leviticus 25:25, relatives were obligated to buy back land that a poor family member had been forced to sell. The nearest relative had first rights but could transfer those rights to the next in line. Boaz presented the matter strategically by first mentioning the land and then the obligation to marry Ruth—a combination that caused the nearer kinsman to withdraw.

**The Sandal Ritual (verses 7–8)**: The text itself explains that this was "an old custom in Israel"—that one would remove a sandal and give it to another to confirm an agreement concerning redemption or exchange. This differs from Deuteronomy 25:9, where a widow removes the sandal of a brother-in-law who refuses levirate marriage and spits before him. Here, however, the action is a peaceful transfer of rights without any shame involved. The fact that the text explains this custom to its readers suggests it had fallen out of use by the time the book was written.

**The Ten Witnesses (verse 2)**: The choice of ten men as witnesses reflects an established legal practice. Later, ten men were also considered the minimum required for a *minyan* (quorum) in Jewish tradition. These witnesses gave the transaction public validity and legal force.

## Key Terms

**"Pelon Almoni" (פְּלֹנִ֣י אַלְמֹנִ֑י)** in verse 1 is a Hebrew expression equivalent to "a certain man" or "so-and-so." This term is used when the narrator deliberately omits the person's real name. This may be a literary strategy to contrast the unnamed kinsman—who would not carry on the family name—with Boaz, whose name and legacy are established through his actions.

**"To raise up the name of the dead" (לְהָקִ֥ים שֵׁם־הַמֵּ֖ת)** in verses 5 and 10 refers to the purpose of both the right of redemption and levirate marriage: to ensure that the deceased's name and inheritance continued through descendants who legally belonged to the dead. The child Obed was thus regarded as Mahlon's legal heir, even though Boaz was his biological father.

**"Better than seven sons" (verse 15)**: The number seven symbolized completeness and wholeness in Hebrew thought. To say that Ruth was worth more than seven sons—the ideal number of male heirs—was the highest possible praise of her value and loyalty.

## People

**Obed (עוֹבֵד)** means "servant" or "worshiper" and is introduced as the link between the story of Ruth and the royal line of David. He is referred to as the grandfather of King David. The genealogy in verses 18–22 traces this lineage back to Perez, son of Judah and Tamar—another story about an outsider woman who secured the family line through unusual circumstances (Genesis 38).

**Rachel and Leah (verse 11)** are mentioned in the people's blessing as the matriarchs who "built the house of Israel." This reference places Ruth among the matriarchs and gives her a honored place in Israel's history, despite her Moabite origins.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The genealogy concluding this chapter (verses 18–22) also appears in a partially parallel form in 1 Chronicles 2:5–15. Such genealogies were important in the ancient Near East for legitimizing kingdoms and establishing dynastic authority. David's descent from the line of Perez through Boaz gave his royal house a documented connection to the tribe of Judah. The placement of this genealogy here transforms what could have been a simple family story into a theological document about God's guidance across generations.
