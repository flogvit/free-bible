# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 14

## Places

**Iconium** (vv. 1–6) was a prosperous city in the southern part of the Roman province of Galatia, located at a fertile oasis along the trade route between east and west. The city had a significant Jewish population with its own synagogue, as well as a large Greek-speaking population. Archaeological findings show that the city had an active religious life with several temples.

**Lystra** (vv. 6–20) was located about 30 kilometers south of Iconium and was a Roman colony founded by Augustus in 6 B.C. as a military outpost. The city had fewer Jews than Iconium, and the population consisted mainly of native Lycaonians who spoke their own language (Λυκαονιστί, v. 11). Inscriptions found in the area confirm the worship of Zeus and Hermes, and an altar dedicated to "Zeus before the city" has been discovered near the presumed site of the ancient city.

**Derbe** (vv. 6, 20–21) was located farthest southeast on the route, near the border of Cilicia. The city was identified in 1956 through an inscription at Kerti Höyük. It was the last city Paul and Barnabas visited before turning back, and it lay strategically at the border of Roman territory.

## People

**The lame man in Lystra** (vv. 8–10) had been lame from birth and had never walked. Paul saw that he had faith to be healed, and the healing triggered the crowd’s reaction that gods had come down.

**Priest of Zeus** (v. 13) represented the official cult of Zeus in Lystra. His quick response with bringing oxen and garlands shows that worship of visiting deities was an established part of the city’s religious practice.

## Events and Customs

**Worship of Zeus and Hermes** (vv. 11–13) has a specific local background. The Roman poet Ovid tells in *Metamorphoses* (8.611–724) the legend of Philemon and Baucis, an elderly couple from exactly the Phrygia/Lycaonia area who received Zeus and Hermes disguised as travelers, while everyone else rejected them. The gods rewarded them and punished the inhospitable. This myth explains why the people in Lystra reacted so strongly—they did not want to repeat their ancestors’ mistake by rejecting gods in human form. That Barnabas was identified as Zeus (the supreme god) and Paul as Hermes (the messenger of the gods) "because he was the one speaking" (ἡγούμενος τοῦ λόγου), fits with Hermes’ role as the god of speech and eloquence.

**Preparations for sacrifice** (v. 13) with oxen and garlands (ταύρους καὶ στέμματα) were standard practice in Greco-Roman sacrifices. The oxen were decorated with wool garlands, and the priest from the temple of Zeus "before the city" (πρὸ τῆς πόλεως)—a common location for main temples—led the ceremony at the city gates. This was a significant public ritual that required an immediate response from the apostles.

**Tearing clothes** (v. 14, διαρρήξαντες τὰ ἱμάτια) was a Jewish practice expressing deep grief or disgust over blasphemy. By doing this, Paul and Barnabas clearly demonstrated their rejection of the worship.

## Key Terms

**Λυκαονιστί** (lykaonisti, v. 11) refers to the local Lycaonian language, which belonged to the Anatolian languages and was different from Greek. That the crowd spontaneously switched to their mother tongue in their excitement shows that Greek was a second language for many in the area. This also explains why Paul and Barnabas first understood what was happening when they saw the concrete preparations for sacrifice.

**Χειροτονήσαντες** (cheirotonēsantes, v. 23) literally means "to stretch out the hand" and was used concerning the appointment or election of leaders. In Greek city-states, the word was used about voting by raising hands. Here it describes the ordination of elders (πρεσβύτεροι) in each congregation—a practice that combined Jewish synagogue structure with the emerging Christian church order.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations and epigraphic findings from the Lycaonia region confirm Luke’s accuracy. An inscription from the 2nd century A.D. found at Sedasa near Lystra mentions "priests of Zeus" and shows the continuing significance of the cult of Zeus in the area. Another inscription from the same region is dedicated to "Zeus before the city" (corresponding to Greek πρὸ τῆς πόλεως), which directly confirms Luke’s description of the temple of Zeus "before the city" in verse 13. These findings show that Acts reflects authentic local religious conditions in first-century Lycaonia.
