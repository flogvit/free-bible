# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 6

## People

**Herod Antipas** (v. 14-29) was the son of Herod the Great and served as tetrarch over Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39. Mark refers to him as "βασιλεύς" (king), even though his official title was tetrarch—a lower rank. This may reflect common speech in Galilee. The Jewish historian Josephus confirms both Herod's marriage to Herodias and the execution of John the Baptist, although Josephus cites political motives rather than the personal ones described by Mark (Antiquitates 18.116-119).

**Herodias** was a granddaughter of Herod the Great and had first been married to her uncle Herod Philip (another son of Herod the Great, not the tetrarch Philip). According to Josephus, her daughter, who danced at the banquet, was named Salome and later married the tetrarch Philip. The marriage between Herod Antipas and Herodias violated Jewish law on two counts: it was a marriage to a brother's wife while the brother was still living, and Herodias was Herod's niece.

**Τέκτων** (v. 3): The designation of Jesus as "tektōn" (carpenter/craftsman) is the only direct reference in the Gospels to his occupation. In antiquity, this was a respectable but humble trade that placed the family in the working class.

## Places

**Nazareth** ("πατρίς" – hometown, v. 1) was a small village in Galilee with an estimated few hundred inhabitants during Jesus' time. Archaeological excavations have revealed simple dwellings carved into limestone hills, confirming the town's humble character. The synagogue where Jesus spoke was likely a simple assembly hall.

**Bethsaida** (v. 45) was located at the northern end of the Sea of Galilee and was the hometown of Peter, Andrew, and Philip. The city was upgraded to a "polis" by Philip the tetrarch and renamed Julias. Archaeological excavations at et-Tell (and possibly el-Araj) have uncovered remains of a fishing village with fishing gear and anchors.

**Gennesaret** (v. 53) was a fertile plain along the northwestern shore of the lake, known in antiquity for its exceptional fertility. Josephus describes the area as a paradise with diverse fruit production (Bellum 3.516-521).

## Events and Customs

**Anointing with oil** (v. 13): The disciples' use of oil in healing ("ἤλειφον ἐλαίῳ") reflects a widespread practice in antiquity. Olive oil was used both medicinally and ritually. In Jewish tradition, oil was associated with the work of the Holy Spirit and prophetic anointing.

**Birthday celebration** (v. 21): The Greek word "γενέσια" denotes a birthday celebration, a custom more common in Greco-Roman culture than in Jewish tradition. The feast included a "δεῖπνον" (banquet) for "μεγιστᾶσιν" (nobles), "χιλιάρχοις" (military commanders of a thousand men), and "πρώτοις" (leading citizens)—typical of a Hellenistic court.

**Dancing at the banquet** (v. 22): For a princess to dance for the guests was highly unusual and scandalous in both Jewish and Greco-Roman contexts. Normally, such entertainment was performed by professional dancers or slaves. This underscores the moral corruption of Herod's court.

**Shaking off dust** (v. 11): The custom of shaking dust from one's feet was a Jewish practice upon returning from pagan territory—a symbolic act of cleansing. Jesus instructed his disciples to use this gesture against Jewish towns that rejected their message, which was a powerful symbolic action.

## Key Terms

**Σπεκουλάτωρ** (v. 27) is a Latin loanword (speculator) denoting a bodyguard or executioner in military service. The use of this Roman military term suggests that Mark's readers were familiar with Roman affairs and that Herod's court was organized according to Roman patterns.

**Συμπόσια συμπόσια** and **πρασιαί πρασιαί** (v. 39-40): The double form is a Semitism expressing distribution—"group by group" and "row by row". The image of the people arranged in rows on the green grass (χλωρῷ χόρτῳ) strongly echoes Psalm 23 and the shepherd motif in v. 34.

**Τετάρτη φυλακή** (v. 48): The fourth watch of the night (about 3-6 a.m.) follows the Roman system of four night watches, as opposed to the Jewish system of three. This is yet another sign of Roman influence in the Gospel of Mark.

**Κόφινος** (v. 43): The twelve baskets that were gathered up were "kofinos"—a type of woven basket particularly associated with Jews. Juvenal (Satire 3.14) mocks Jews for carrying such baskets. The number twelve corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel.
