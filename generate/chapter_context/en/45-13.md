# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 13

## Events and Customs

Romans 13:1-7 can be understood in light of a relevant historical event: In the year 49 C.E., Emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome, possibly due to disturbances related to "Chrestus" (likely a Latin distortion of "Christus," according to Suetonius). When Paul wrote his letter (around 56-57 C.E.), the Jews had been allowed to return under Emperor Nero, who during this early period of his reign pursued a relatively mild policy. The congregation in Rome was therefore in a vulnerable position and needed to demonstrate itself as peaceful citizens to avoid new expulsion or persecution.

The issue of taxation that Paul addresses (vv. 6-7) was particularly timely. The Greek word "φόρος" (foros) refers to direct tax as tribute, while "τέλος" (telos) denotes indirect taxes such as customs duties. At this time, there was growing discontent with the tax burden throughout the empire, and only a few years later (in 58 C.E.) there nearly arose an uprising in Rome over the practices of tax collectors. For Jews and Christians, who were already suspected of disloyalty, it was crucial not to be associated with such resistance.

## Key Terms

The expression "τὴν μάχαιραν φορεῖ" (bears the sword, v. 4) is a technical term referring to *ius gladii* – the Roman authority's right to carry out capital punishment. This sword was a symbol of the governing power's legal authority and was carried by Roman magistrates and their representatives as a sign of their authority to maintain law and order.

The word "λειτουργοί" (leitourgoi, v. 6) is remarkable because in Greek context it denoted persons who performed public service – originally wealthy citizens who financed public goods. In the Septuagint, the word is used for priestly service. By calling the governing authorities "God's servants" with this term, Paul places secular authority within a theological framework where even pagan rulers serve God's purposes.

The concept of "συνείδησις" (syneidesis, conscience, v. 5) was known in Greco-Roman philosophy, including Stoic thought, and was well familiar in the Greco-Roman world. Paul expands the motivation for obedience from mere fear of punishment to also include an inner moral conviction – something that would resonate with educated Roman citizens.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Inscriptions from the first century confirm the complex tax system Paul refers to. Papyrus documents from Egypt show how tax collection worked in practice, with detailed receipts for both direct taxes (tributum) and indirect fees (portoria). These sources illuminate the concrete everyday reality behind Paul's exhortations to pay what is owed to the governing authorities.

Archaeological finds of coins from Nero's early reign (54-62 C.E.) bear inscriptions highlighting peace and justice, reflecting the positive propaganda of the period when Paul wrote. This so-called "quinquennium Neronis" (Nero's good five years) was characterized by relatively good governance under the guidance of the philosopher Seneca, making Paul's positive description of governing authorities understandable in its contemporary context.
