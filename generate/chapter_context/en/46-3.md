# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 3

## People

**Apollos** is prominently mentioned in this chapter as a central figure in the church. According to Acts 18:24–28, Apollos was a Jewish scholar from Alexandria, described as eloquent and knowledgeable about the Scriptures. His Alexandrian background and rhetorical skills may have led some in the church to prefer him over Paul, who, according to 2 Corinthians 10:10, was criticized for having weak presence in speech.

**Cephas** (Peter) is mentioned in verse 22 as one of the leaders with whom some in Corinth identified. The Greek Κηφᾶς is a transcription of the Aramaic "Kefa" (rock). Whether Peter actually visited Corinth is uncertain, but his apostolic authority gave him considerable status among those who identified with him in the church.

## Events and Customs

Paul uses the image of a **master builder** (ἀρχιτέκτων) in verse 10. In the Greco-Roman world, an architect was not merely a designer but a highly respected professional who oversaw the entire construction process. The term implied technical expertise and responsibility for laying a solid foundation. Corinth was marked by extensive building activity following its reconstruction in 44 BC, so the image of construction work would have been very familiar to readers.

The **agricultural metaphor** in verses 6–9 (planting, watering, growth) was well known in both Greek and Jewish tradition. The Isthmian region around Corinth was fertile farmland, and the image of workers cooperating on the same field would have resonated with readers.

## Key Terms

**Σαρκίνοις/σαρκικοί** (sarkinos/sarkikos) – "fleshly" – are key words in verses 1–3. Paul distinguishes between σαρκίνοις (verse 1), which describes the natural state of human weakness, and σαρκικοί (verse 3), which suggests a moral failure where believers live *as if* they still lacked the Spirit. This wordplay marks the distinction between immaturity and spiritual backsliding.

**Ναὸς θεοῦ** (naos theou) – "God’s temple" (verses 16–17) – uses ναός, which specifically denotes the inner holy place, the sanctuary itself, in contrast to ἱερόν, which refers to the entire temple complex. This underscores the church’s status as the most holy place where God’s Spirit dwells. In a city full of temples—including the Temple of Apollo and the Sanctuary of Aphrodite—this was a radical claim about the church’s holy identity.

**Συνεργοί** (synergoi) – "co-workers" (verse 9) – was a term known from both craft guilds and philosophical schools in antiquity. Paul’s use of the term to describe God’s co-workers was bold and emphasized shared service rather than competition among leaders.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Corinth have uncovered extensive building remains from the first century, including foundations of varying quality—from expensive worked stone to simple materials. This provides concrete background for Paul’s metaphor of building with "gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, or straw" (verse 12). Inscriptions found in Corinth also show that the city had an active construction environment with architects and craftsmen, making the building metaphor immediately understandable to members of the church.
