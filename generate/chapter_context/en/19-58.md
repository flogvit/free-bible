# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 58

## Key Terms

The psalm opens with the Hebrew expression **אֵלֶם** (*elem*), which is debated among scholars. The word may mean "silence" or "dumbness," but some interpret it as a variant of **אֵלִם** (*elim*, "gods" or "mighty ones"), possibly referring to heavenly beings or earthly rulers. In this second interpretation, the psalm directs criticism against divine council members or earthly judges who fail in their duty—a motif known from Mesopotamian and Canaanite literature where gods hold counsel.

The phrase **חוֹבֵר חֲבָרִים** (*chover chavarim*, v. 6) literally means "one who binds spells" and refers to a snake charmer—a widespread profession in the ancient Near East. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, such specialists used formulas and music to control venomous snakes. The image of a deaf snake that will not be charmed (כְּמוֹ־פֶתֶן חֵרֵשׁ יַאְטֵם אָזְנוֹ, v. 5) illustrates the wicked's deliberate resistance to all influence. Snake charming is well documented archaeologically; Egyptian reliefs show charmers at work, and texts from Ugarit contain spells against snake bites.

## Events and Customs

The psalm contains powerful curse formulas that have parallels in Old Testament and surrounding cultures. The image of the enemy melting "like a snail" (**שַׁבְּלוּל תֶּמֶס**, *shablul temes*, v. 9) and the comparison to stillborn children (**נֵפֶל אֵשֶׁת**, *nefel eshet*) reflect a worldview in which curses held real power. Such expressions are also found in Akkadian and Egyptian texts.

The expression "wash one's feet in the blood of the wicked" (**פְּעָמָיו יִרְחַץ בְּדַם הָרָשָׁע**, v. 11) is shocking to modern readers but represents conventional imagery for total victory in Old Testament poetry. It reflects the triumphant warrior-king and God's righteous judgment, not literal blood revenge.
