# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 106

## Events and Customs

Psalm 106 is a historical psalm of confession that reviews Israel's history of sin from the exodus from Egypt to settlement in Canaan. It refers to several specific events from the books of Moses: the rebellion at the Red Sea (Exodus 14), the demand for meat in the wilderness (Numbers 11), Korah's rebellion against Moses and Aaron (Numbers 16), the golden calf at Horeb (Exodus 32), the spies' unbelief (Numbers 13–14), the worship of Baal-Peor (Numbers 25), and the incident at Meribah (Numbers 20). This listing of sins follows a pattern known from other confession texts in the Old Testament, such as Nehemiah 9, and was likely used in communal rituals of repentance.

Verses 37–38 mention child sacrifice to "shedim" (evil spirits/demons) and to Canaanite idols. Archaeological finds from Carthage (Phoenician: Qart-Hadasht) and other Phoenician colonies have uncovered "tophet" sanctuaries containing remains of child sacrifices. In Canaan itself, traces of similar practices have been found, confirming the biblical portrayal of this abhorrent custom that Israel adopted from neighboring peoples.

## People

**Moses** (vv. 16, 23, 32–33) is presented as God's chosen servant and intercessor. In verse 23 he is described as one who "stood in the breach" to turn away God's wrath after the sin of the golden calf—an image drawn from someone defending a city wall against intruders. At Meribah (vv. 32–33) he "spoke rashly" because of the people's rebellion, which cost him entry into the promised land.

**Phinehas** (vv. 30–31) was Aaron's grandson who intervened during the Baal-Peor crisis by killing an Israelite man and his Midianite woman (Numbers 25:7–13). His action "was counted to him as righteousness"—an expression otherwise used primarily concerning Abraham (Genesis 15:6). This established Phinehas's lineage as a priestly line with the promise of an everlasting priesthood.

**Dathan and Abiram** (v. 17) were Reubenites who, together with the Levite Korah, led a rebellion against the leadership of Moses and Aaron. The psalm focuses on these two because they represented the political rebellion, while Korah represented the religious one. The earth that "opened its mouth and swallowed" them became a lasting warning image in Israelite tradition.

## Places

**Horeb** (v. 19) is an alternative name for Mount Sinai, especially used in the Deuteronomic tradition. It was here that Israel made the golden calf while Moses received the law. **Baal-Peor** (v. 28) was a local Baal sanctuary in Moab, at Mount Peor, where the Israelites engaged in idolatry and sexual immorality just before entering the land. **Meribah** (v. 32) means "quarrel" and refers to the place where the people demanded water and Moses struck the rock in anger.

## Key Terms

The expression "zivcḥe metim" (זִבְחֵי מֵתִים, "sacrifices to the dead", v. 28) has been interpreted in several ways: either sacrifices to dead gods (lifeless idols), sacrifices connected with a cult of the dead, or sacrifices in which the sacrificial animals were completely destroyed. The term "shedim" (שֵׁדִים, v. 37) is a rare Hebrew word, possibly borrowed from Akkadian "šēdu" (a type of protective spirit). In biblical context it is used polemically to describe idols as spiritual powers in contrast to the living God. It occurs only here and in Deuteronomy 32:17 in the Old Testament.
