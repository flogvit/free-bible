# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 18

## People

**Jonathan** appears in this chapter as Saul’s firstborn son and heir to the throne. His making of a covenant with David is remarkable because, as the crown prince, he voluntarily binds himself to David in a binding alliance. The handing over of his robe, weapons, and belt (v. 4) was not merely gifts of friendship but symbolic acts with political significance—he transferred insignia that marked royal status.

**Merab and Michal**, Saul’s daughters, play central roles in the marriage negotiations. Merab had been promised to David, but instead was given to Adriel from Meholah. Michal is the only woman in the Old Testament explicitly described as loving a man (v. 20), an unusual narrative detail that underscores David’s popularity.

## Events and Customs

**The Women’s Victory Song** (vv. 6–7) represents a well-known custom in the ancient Near East where women greeted returning warriors with song, dance, and music. The instruments mentioned—hand drums (tuppim) and shalishim (possibly a stringed or percussion instrument)—were typical for such celebrations. The antiphonal form of singing with alternating responses ("Saul has struck down his thousands, David his tens of thousands") follows an established poetic pattern, but the numerical comparison became politically explosive.

**Bride-price (mohar)** was a fixed custom in Israelite society. Saul’s demand for one hundred Philistine foreskins as bride-price was unheard of—normally the mohar consisted of silver, livestock, or service (cf. Jacob’s seven years for Rachel). David exceeded the requirement by delivering two hundred foreskins (v. 27), demonstrating his superior skill. The request for foreskins was specifically directed at the Philistines, who, unlike the Israelites, did not practice circumcision. Collecting body parts from enemies as proof of victory is documented in Egyptian sources and other ancient texts.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **"nefesh niqsherah"** (נֶפֶשׁ... נִקְשְׁרָה, "the soul was bound") in verse 1 describes a deep spiritual bond. The word is used elsewhere concerning Jacob and Benjamin (Gen 44:30) and denotes an inseparable connection.

**Berit** (בְּרִית, "covenant") in verse 3 is a heavily loaded term. The covenant between Jonathan and David was a formal, binding agreement, likely with witnesses and perhaps involving a sacrifice—not merely an informal promise of friendship.

The verb **"maskil"** (מַשְׂכִּיל) in verses 5, 14, 15, and 30 describes David as one who "acts wisely" or "prosper[s]." The word encompasses both practical wisdom and divine blessing—his success is not merely human ability but a sign that the Lord was with him.
