# Historical and Cultural Context for Malachi 1

## People

**Jacob and Esau** are mentioned in verses 2–3 as representatives of Israel and Edom respectively. In this context, the names serve as designations for the nations descended from these twin brothers in Genesis. Edom, the descendants of Esau, settled in the mountainous regions south and east of the Dead Sea. The relationship between Israel and Edom was marked by conflict throughout Old Testament history, and the Edomites' mockery of Judah during Jerusalem's fall in 586 B.C.E. is strongly condemned in several prophetic books (see Obadiah; Psalm 137:7).

**The priests** (הַכֹּהֲנִים, *hakkohanim*) are the primary recipients of criticism in verses 6–14. After returning from exile, the priesthood had been restored, but this chapter reveals that they had deteriorated into indifference and dishonesty in their service.

## Places

**Edom** and its desolation (verses 3–4) reflect historical events. In the sixth century B.C.E., the Edomites were gradually driven from their traditional territory by the Nabateans, an Arab people. Archaeological investigations in southern Jordan (ancient Edom) show signs of population decline and the decay of cities such as Bozrah during this period. The Edomites moved westward into southern Judah (later called Idumea), but their original homeland remained desolate—just as the prophecy describes.

## Events and Customs

**The sacrificial practice** described in verses 7–8 and 13–14 was regulated by detailed instructions in Leviticus. The law required that sacrificial animals be without defect (Lev 22:17–25). A blind, lame, or sick animal was explicitly forbidden. The term לֶחֶם מְגֹאָל (*lechem megoa'l*, "unclean food/bread") in verse 7 refers to offerings that were ritually unclean and therefore unacceptable. The priests failed in their responsibility as guardians of holiness standards.

**The reference to the governor** (פֶּחָה, *pechah*) in verse 8 reflects the Persian administrative structure in the province of Yehud. The governor was the highest Persian official in the region. The argument is rhetorically effective: no one would dare give a defective gift to a human governor, yet the priests considered it acceptable to offer such things to God.

## Key Terms

**"The Lord's table"** (שֻׁלְחַן יְהוָה, *shulchan YHWH*) in verses 7 and 12 is a designation for the altar. The expression emphasizes that the offering was God's meal, a form of fellowship between God and his people. By profaning the table, the priests showed contempt for the very fellowship with God.

**"From the rising of the sun to its setting"** (verse 11) is a poetic expression for the entire world. This verse has been the subject of much theological discussion—the prophet looks forward to a time when God's name will be honored universally, in contrast to the dishonor shown him by the priests in Jerusalem.
