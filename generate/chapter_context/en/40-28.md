# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 28

## People

**Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary"** (Μαριὰμ ἡ Μαγδαληνή καὶ ἡ ἄλλη Μαρία) are the first witnesses to the resurrection. Mary Magdalene was from the town of Magdala by the Sea of Galilee. "The other Mary" is usually identified as Mary, the mother of James and Joseph (see Matt 27:56, 61). The fact that women were the first witnesses is remarkable, since a woman's testimony had limited legal value under Jewish law. This supports the authenticity of the account—a fabricated story would likely have used male witnesses.

**The guard unit** (κουστωδία, *koustōdia*) consisted either of Roman soldiers or temple police under the command of the chief priests. The term is a Latin loanword in Greek, suggesting a Roman military presence. The fact that they could be bribed and that the Sanhedrin promised to protect them before the governor indicates they were Roman soldiers who risked execution for falling asleep on duty.

## Places

**The tomb**, according to tradition, was a rock-cut tomb of the type common among wealthy Jews in the first century. Such tombs had a rolling stone (λίθος, *lithos*) in front of the entrance that could weigh several hundred kilograms. Archaeological excavations in the Jerusalem area have uncovered many such tombs with grooves for rolling stones, confirming the gospel description.

**The mountain in Galilee** (v. 16) is not identified further, but it is characteristic of Matthew to place important events on mountains (see the Sermon on the Mount in ch. 5–7, and the transfiguration in ch. 17). Galilee held great symbolic significance as the place where Jesus' ministry began and where many of the disciples came from.

## Events and Customs

**The visit to the tomb at dawn** on the first day of the week follows Jewish custom. The Sabbath lasted from sunset on Friday until sunset on Saturday, so the women had to wait until the Sabbath ended before they could complete the burial rites. The expression "ὀψὲ σαββάτων" (after the Sabbath) combined with "τῇ ἐπιφωσκούσῃ εἰς μίαν σαββάτων" (as it began to dawn toward the first day of the week) places the event early on Sunday morning.

**The bribery of the guards** (vv. 12–15) reflects an early Jewish-Christian polemic. Matthew notes that this story "has been spread among the Jews until this day" (μέχρι τῆς σήμερον ἡμέρας), indicating that the evangelist was writing at a time when competing explanations for the empty tomb existed. This indirectly confirms that both Christians and non-Christian Jews agreed that the tomb was indeed empty—the disagreement concerned the cause.

## Key Terms

**"All authority in heaven and on earth"** (πᾶσα ἐξουσία ἐν οὐρανῷ καὶ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς) is an expression linked to Daniel 7:14, where "one like a son of man" receives dominion over all peoples and nations. Jesus applies this to himself, thereby legitimizing the mission command that follows.

**"Make disciples"** (μαθητεύσατε) is the main verb in the Great Commission and stands in the aorist imperative. The accompanying participles "βαπτίζοντες" (baptizing) and "διδάσκοντες" (teaching) describe how disciple-making should take place. It involves more than just preaching—it is about shaping followers who learn to obey everything Jesus commanded. "All nations" (πάντα τὰ ἔθνη) marks an expansion from Jesus' earlier focus on "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matt 10:6) to a universal mission.

**The Trinitarian baptismal formula** "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (εἰς τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ πατρὸς καὶ τοῦ υἱοῦ καὶ τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος) is unique to Matthew and represents the earliest known formulation of this kind. The book of Acts refers to baptism "in Jesus' name," which has led to discussions about the origin and development of the formula in the early church.

**"I am with you always"** (ἐγὼ μεθʼ ὑμῶν εἰμι πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας) concludes the gospel with a promise of Jesus' enduring presence "to the end of the age" (ἕως τῆς συντελείας τοῦ αἰῶνος). This echoes the Immanuel theme from Matt 1:23 ("God with us") and forms an inclusion that frames the entire gospel.
