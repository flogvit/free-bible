# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 21

## Places

**Bethphage and the Mount of Olives** (v. 1): Bethphage was a small village on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, about 1 km from Jerusalem. The name likely means "place of the fig house" in Aramaic. The Mount of Olives (Τὸ Ὄρος τῶν Ἐλαιῶν) lies east of Jerusalem with a view overlooking the temple and held special significance in Jewish eschatology. According to Zechariah 14:4, the Lord would stand on this mountain on the last day. A king coming from that direction would thus stir messianic expectations.

**Bethany** (v. 17): The village was located about 3 km east of Jerusalem on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives. Jesus used it as a base during his final days, likely because he had friends there (Lazarus and his sisters).

**The Temple** (vv. 12–23): The trade took place in the "Court of the Gentiles" – the outer temple area where non-Jews were allowed to enter. Archaeological finds include stone blocks with inscriptions warning Gentiles not to go further into the temple.

## Events and Customs

**The Entry on a Donkey** (vv. 1–11): In ancient times, kings rode horses in war but donkeys in peacetime. Jesus' choice of a donkey signaled a peaceful, humble king in contrast to military expectations. Laying cloaks on the road was a royal honor known from the Old Testament (2 Kings 9:13). Palm branches and leafy boughs were associated with Jewish festivals, especially the Feast of Tabernacles, and carried national-political symbolism after the Maccabean period.

**Temple Trade** (vv. 12–13): Money changers (κολλυβιστῶν) were necessary because the temple tax had to be paid in Tyrian currency, and pilgrims needed to exchange Roman coins. Dove sellers offered sacrificial animals for those too poor to afford larger animals. This trade was itself legitimate, but the criticism likely concerned its location, prices, and how it hindered Gentile worship. The high priest Annas and his family controlled much of this trade and made large profits from it.

**The Fig Tree** (vv. 18–22): Fig trees in Israel produce edible "early figs" (paggîm) in March-April along with the leaves. A tree with leaves but without such early figs would indicate barrenness. The fig tree was a well-known symbol of Israel in prophetic literature (Jeremiah 8:13; Hosea 9:10; Micah 7:1).

## Key Terms

**"Hosanna"** (Ὡσαννά, vv. 9, 15): Greek transliteration of the Hebrew *hôšîʿâ nāʾ* ("save now!") from Psalm 118:25. Originally a cry for help, by Jesus' time it had developed into a liturgical acclamation, especially used during the Feast of Tabernacles. The addition "in the highest" (ἐν τοῖς ὑψίστοις) directs the prayer toward heaven.

**"Son of David"** (τῷ υἱῷ Δαυίδ, vv. 9, 15): A messianic title based on the promises to David in 2 Samuel 7. In Jewish expectation, the Messiah was to be a Davidic king who would restore Israel's glory. The high priests' reaction (v. 15) shows how provocative this title was in the temple context.

**"Tax collectors and sinners"** (οἱ τελῶναι καὶ αἱ πόρναι, vv. 31–32): In Jesus' parable, tax collectors and prostitutes represent those who initially rejected but later repented and believed John's message – in contrast to the religious leaders. That these would enter God's kingdom ahead of the high priests and elders was an extreme provocation.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological finds from the temple area include stone blocks with Hebrew inscriptions warning Gentiles against entering the inner courts. These illustrate the ethnic and religious segregation that characterized the temple. Coin finds from the period show the Tyrian shekels used for the temple tax, as well as the Roman coins pilgrims had to exchange – shedding light on the role of money changers. In addition, excavations along the southern temple wall have uncovered stairs and ritual baths (mikvaot) that pilgrims used before ascending to the temple.
