## Events and Customs

The chapter warns against *porneia* (sexual immorality), *akatharsia* (impurity), and *pleonexia* (greed) – sins that were widespread in cosmopolitan Ephesus. The warning against getting "drunk with wine, which leads to debauchery" (v. 18) reflects a concrete challenge: Greco-Roman symposia (drinking parties) were often characterized by excessive alcohol consumption and immoral behavior. Paul contrasts this with being "filled with the Spirit" and singing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs – an alternative form of community for Christians.

The reference to "what is done in secret" (v. 12) may point to the mystery cults that flourished in Ephesus, including the worship of Dionysus, where secret rituals often involved intoxication and immorality. The hymnic fragment in verse 14 ("Ἔγειρε, ὁ καθεύδων, καὶ ἀνάστα ἐκ τῶν νεκρῶν" – "Wake up, you who sleep, and rise from the dead") is believed by many scholars to be a quotation from an early Christian baptismal hymn, sung when new converts rose out of the baptismal water – a powerful image of the transition from darkness to light.

## Key Terms

The expression *osmē euōdias* ("a fragrant aroma", v. 2) is sacrificial language drawn from the Old Testament (cf. Lev 1:9,13,17; 2:2,9), where it describes offerings that are pleasing to God. By applying this to Christ’s self-giving, his death on the cross is presented as the ultimate sacrifice.

The word *kephalē* ("head", v. 23) in the context of marriage has been the subject of much discussion. In Greek, it could mean both "authority/leader" and "source/origin". In the Greco-Roman world, *patria potestas* (the father’s authority over the household) was a legal reality, but Paul radically transforms this by commanding husbands to love "as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" – a self-sacrificing love that was foreign to the patriarchal understanding of marriage in that time.

The term *mystērion* ("mystery", v. 32) had strong resonance in Ephesus, where mystery cults promised insight into hidden truths. Paul reinterprets the term christologically: the great mystery is the relationship between Christ and the church, revealed through the image of marriage.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Inscriptions from Ephesus confirm that the *haustafel* structure (household codes in vv. 22–33) draws upon a well-known social reality. Archaeological discoveries of marriage contracts from the eastern Mediterranean region from this period show that women often had limited legal status in practice. The revolutionary aspect of Paul’s teaching was not the structure itself, but its content: the call for mutual submission (v. 21) and the husband’s self-sacrificing love were countercultural. Discoveries of early Christian inscriptions from burial sites in Asia Minor show that the Christian understanding of marriage gradually influenced practice, with increasing emphasis on mutual respect between spouses.
