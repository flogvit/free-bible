# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 29

## Key Concepts

Several Hebrew expressions in this chapter require closer explanation. The expression **מַקְשֶׁה־עֹרֶף** (*maqsheh-oref*, verse 1) literally means "to make the neck stiff" and refers to a stubborn ox that refuses to bow under the yoke. This became a fixed idiom for obstinacy and resistance to correction, used several times in the Old Testament regarding Israel's disobedience. The expression **אִישׁ תְּרוּמוֹת** (*ish terumot*, verse 4) describes someone who demands or accepts "gifts" – a euphemism for bribes and corrupt extortion that undermined the legal system.

The word **חָזוֹן** (*chazon*, verse 18) means prophetic vision or revelation, not merely ordinary sight. This verse links societal stability directly to access to divine guidance through prophets. The verb **יִפָּרַע** (*yippara*), which describes what happens without such vision, can mean "to run wild," "become unrestrained," or "perish." The rare word **מָנוֹן** (*manon*, verse 21) occurs only here in the entire Bible, and its meaning is uncertain – possible interpretations include "heir," "ungrateful," or "rebellious."

## Events and Customs

Verse 24 mentions a specific legal situation: **אָלָה יִשְׁמַע וְלֹא יַגִּיד** – "he hears the oath/curse, but does not report it." This refers to the public oath proclaimed when witnesses were summoned in a court case (cf. Lev 5:1). When a judge or official pronounced a solemn curse upon anyone withholding testimony, anyone with relevant information was obligated to come forward. One who conspired with a thief and then remained silent under such an oath invoked the curse upon themselves – they "hate their own life."

The chapter's repeated focus on the king's justice (verses 4, 12, 14, 26) reflects the Old Testament understanding of the king as supreme judge and guarantor of social justice. In contrast to surrounding cultures where the king stood above the law, wisdom literature emphasizes that even the king was bound by divine law and would be held accountable for how he treated the poor and weak.
