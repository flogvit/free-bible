# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 42

## People

In the epilogue, Job’s three daughters are named by name: **Jemima** (יְמִימָה, "dove" or "day"), **Keziah** (קְצִיעָה, "cassia bark") and **Keren-Happuch** (קֶרֶן הַפּוּךְ, "horn of antimony" or "horn with eye paint"). That the daughters are named while the sons remain anonymous is unusual in biblical texts and highlights their significance. The names are linked to beauty and precious items—the dove symbolized grace, cassia was a valuable spice used in perfume and anointing oil, and "horn of antimony" refers to containers for the black eye makeup (kohl) that was widespread in the ancient Near East.

## Events and Customs

The **burnt offering** (עוֹלָה) that the three friends are commanded to bring—seven bulls and seven rams—represents a significant and costly sacrifice of atonement. The number seven symbolizes perfection and completeness in Semitic culture. That Job acts as an **intercessor** (יִתְפַּלֵּל עֲלֵיכֶם) for his friends places him in a role reminiscent of the patriarchs and later prophets who prayed on behalf of others (cf. Gen 20:7 regarding Abraham).

**Inheritance rights for daughters** (verse 15) were unusual in the ancient Near East, where property normally passed to sons. This reflects either a very old custom from before the Mosaic laws were established, or a special honor that underscores Job’s righteousness and generosity.

## Key Terms

The expression **אֶמְאַס וְנִחַמְתִּי** ("I reject and repent/am comforted") in verse 6 is debated among Hebrew scholars, since the verb מאס lacks an explicit object. The verb נחם can mean both "repent" and "be comforted/find comfort." The phrase "in dust and ashes" (עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר) may indicate that Job sits in mourning and repentance, or it can be understood as his setting aside his earlier complaint about his earthly, perishable condition.

**Keziah** (קְשִׂיטָה) in verse 11 is an ancient unit of currency that otherwise appears only in Gen 33:19 and Josh 24:32, both in connection with the patriarchal period. This supports the book’s placement in an early, pre-monarchic context and suggests that value was measured by weight of precious metal rather than by coins.

**"Old and full of days"** (זָקֵן וּשְׂבַע יָמִים) in verse 17 is a title of honor describing a complete and blessed life, used concerning the patriarchs Abraham (Gen 25:8) and Isaac (Gen 35:29), as well as King David (1 Chron 29:28). The expression places Job in the same category as these great figures of faith.
