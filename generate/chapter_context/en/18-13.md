# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 13

## Key Terms

The chapter contains several Hebrew expressions with deep cultural resonance. The phrase "טֹפְלֵי־שָׁקֶר" (toflei shaqer) in verse 4, translated as "those who plaster lies," uses a craftsman's term for applying mortar or plaster to a wall. This image suggests that the friends' arguments are like a thin, cosmetic layer over a fragile structure—they cover up the truth with false explanations.

The expression "רֹפְאֵי אֱלִיל" (rof'ei elil) – "worthless physicians" – is particularly sharp. The word "elil" (אֱלִיל) is used elsewhere to refer to idols and empty things, implying that the friends' "comfort" is as ineffective as idol worship.

In verse 15 we find the famous "הֵן יִקְטְלֵנִי" (hen yiqt'leni). The Hebrew text has a textual critical variant between ketiv (לא – "not") and qere (לוֹ – "him"). This gives two possible readings: "Though he slay me, yet will I not wait" (ketiv) or "Though he slay me, yet will I hope in him" (qere). This ambiguity has been the subject of Jewish and Christian interpretation for millennia.

## Events and Customs

Job's desire to "plead his case" before God (verses 3, 18–22) reflects Old Testament legal tradition. The phrase "עָרַכְתִּי מִשְׁפָּט" (arakti mishpat) – "I have prepared my case" – is legal terminology. In the ancient Near East, the city gate was where legal cases were heard, and both parties presented their arguments before the elders. Job uses this familiar image but elevates it to a cosmic level: he wants a trial with the Creator himself.

Verse 27 mentions "סַד" (sad), a stock or footblock used to restrict the movements of prisoners. Archaeological finds from Egypt and Mesopotamia show various types of such restraints. Job uses this as an image of how he feels trapped and watched by God.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The concept of "putting one's life in one's hand" (verse 14: "נַפְשִׁי אָשִׂים בְּכַפִּי") is an idiomatic expression also found elsewhere in the Old Testament (Judges 12:3; 1 Samuel 19:5; 28:21). It means to risk one's life, to stake everything on a single outcome. This expression testifies to a shared linguistic and cultural heritage in ancient Israel, where courage and desperation were described with concrete bodily images.
