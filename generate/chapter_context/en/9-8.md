## People

**Joel and Abijah** (verse 2) were Samuel's two sons, appointed as judges in Beersheba. The name Joel (יוֹאֵל) means "YHWH is God," while Abijah (אֲבִיָּה) means "my father is YHWH." Despite these pious names, they did not follow their father's path. Their corruption—taking bribes and perverting justice—stands in contrast to Samuel's integrity and recalls how Eli's sons also failed (1 Sam 2:12-17).

**The elders of Israel** (זִקְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, verse 4) represented the leadership of the tribes. The council of elders was the traditional form of governance in Israel. Their gathering to present a unified demand shows coordinated political action from the entire people.

## Places

**Beersheba** (verse 2) lay at Israel's southernmost border, about 80 km south of Ramah. The city traditionally marked the southern boundary of the kingdom in the expression "from Dan to Beersheba." That Samuel's sons were placed there, far from their father's oversight in Ramah, may have contributed to their corruption.

**Ramah** (הָרָמָתָה, verse 4) was Samuel's hometown and served as his administrative center. It was here that the elders came to present their demand for a king.

## Events and Customs

**The demand for a king "like all the nations"** (כְּכָל־הַגּוֹיִם, verses 5, 20) reflects the people's desire to imitate the political structure of surrounding nations. The justification in verse 20 is threefold: they want a king who will "judge them," "go out before them," and "fight their battles."

**"The rights of the king"** (מִשְׁפַּט הַמֶּלֶךְ, verses 9-17) describes typical features of Eastern monarchy. The description covers chariots and cavalry (verse 11), forced labor, confiscation of property, and tithes for royal officials.

## Key Terms

The expression **מִשְׁפַּט הַמֶּלֶךְ** (verses 9, 11) can be translated both as "the king's right/custom" and "the king's judgment/manner." The word *mishpat* is ambiguous—it can mean legitimate legal practice, but also simply "how the king will behave." Samuel likely uses this ambiguity deliberately to warn: this is not ideal justice, but a realistic description of royal practice.

The verb **מָאַס** ("reject," verse 7) is theologically charged. God interprets the people's demand as a rejection of his own kingship over Israel, not merely as criticism of Samuel.

**Tithes** (יַעְשֹׂר, verses 15, 17) to the king come in addition to the religious tithe given to the Lord. This represents a new economic burden that the people must bear.
