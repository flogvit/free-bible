# Historical and Cultural Context for Jonah 4

## Places

Jonah settled "east of the city" (v. 5), which geographically places him on the highlands east of Nineveh. This area would have provided a view over the city and the plains around the Tigris River. That he waited "to see what would happen to the city" may suggest that he expected destruction within the forty-day deadline he had proclaimed (3:4). The region east of Nineveh was known for its hot climate, especially in summer, which makes the "scorching east wind" (רוּחַ קָדִים חֲרִישִׁית) in verse 8 geographically credible. The east wind from the Mesopotamian desert was notorious for its intense, drying heat.

## Key Terms

The expression in verse 2 where Jonah describes God as "אֵל־חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וְרַב־חֶסֶד" (a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in love) is an almost word-for-word rendering of God’s self-revelation in Exodus 34:6–7. This is one of the most central confessions in the Old Testament, often called the formula of God’s grace or, in Hebrew, linked to the concept of "midot" (attributes). The irony is that Jonah uses this lofty theology as the basis for his complaint.

The plant "קִיקָיוֹן" (qiqayon) in verse 6 is disputed. It is often identified as castor bean (Ricinus communis), known for its rapid growth and large leaves that provide good shade, or alternatively as a type of gourd plant. Regardless of the species, the point of the text emphasizes the plant’s fleeting nature—it came "in one night" and perished "in one night" (v. 10).

## Events and Customs

Jonah’s construction of a "סֻכָּה" (sukkah) in verse 5 reflects a common practice in the ancient Near East. Such temporary shelters were used by farmers, shepherds, and travelers to provide shade. The structure was typically made of branches and leaves. This is the same word used for the booths in the Feast of Tabernacles, which may give a subtle theological undertone about transience and dependence on God’s provision.

God’s reference to the people of Nineveh as those "who do not know their right hand from their left" (v. 11) is an expression usually referring to young children. The number "one hundred twenty thousand" (120,000) may refer either to the entire population of the city or specifically to children below the age of accountability. This rhetorical device underscores the Ninevites’ moral ignorance and God’s compassion toward the vulnerable.
