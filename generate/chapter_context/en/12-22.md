# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 22

## People

**Josiah** (יֹאשִׁיָּהוּ) became king at the age of eight and reigned for thirty-one years in Jerusalem. His mother, Jedidah (יְדִידָה), was the daughter of Adaiah from Bozkath (v. 1). The text emphasizes that he "did what was right in the eyes of the Lord" and followed the path of his ancestor David without turning to the right or to the left (v. 2).

**Hilkiah** (חִלְקִיָּהוּ), the high priest, played a key role in the discovery of the book. A seal with the inscription "belonging to Hilkiahhu, son of [...], the priest" has been found in the Jerusalem area, possibly belonging to him or a close relative. **Shaphan** (שָׁפָן) was the royal scribe (סֹּפֵר), a high-ranking official responsible for documents and finances.

**The five messengers** (vv. 12–14): Hilkiah the priest, Ahikam son of Shaphan, Achbor son of Micaiah, Shaphan the scribe, and Asaiah the king's servant—were sent to seek the will of the Lord.

**Huldah** (חֻלְדָּה) is one of the few female prophets mentioned in the Old Testament. The fact that the king's delegation sought her out shows that female prophets held recognized authority. She was married to Shallum, "keeper of the wardrobe" (שֹׁמֵר הַבְּגָדִים)—likely responsible for royal or temple ceremonial garments.

## Places

**Mishneh** (בַּמִּשְׁנֶה), where Huldah lived (v. 14), means "the second [quarter]" and refers to an expansion of Jerusalem. This quarter was likely located in the area west of the Temple Mount. Archaeological excavations have confirmed that this district existed during Josiah's time.

**Bozkath** (בָּצְקַת), Jedidah's hometown, was located in the Shephelah region (the lowlands of Judah) and is identified with the modern Eglon area.

## Events and Customs

**Temple repair and funding** (vv. 4–7) reveal an established practice for temple maintenance. The "doorkeepers" (שֹׁמְרֵי הַסַּף) collected contributions from the people. The fact that the craftsmen did not need to provide accounts because they worked "in faithfulness" (בֶאֱמוּנָה) reflects a trust-based economy within the temple context.

**Tearing one's clothes** (וַיִּקְרַע אֶת־בְּגָדָיו, v. 11) was the standardized expression for grief, shock, or repentance in ancient Near Eastern culture. Josiah's reaction shows that the content of the book was understood as a serious judgment against the people's practices.

## Key Terms

**"The Book of the Law"** (סֵפֶר הַתּוֹרָה) found (v. 8) has been the subject of academic debate regarding which text it refers to. The term "found" (מָצָאתִי) could mean either a literal discovery of a forgotten scroll or the rediscovery of a text that had fallen out of use.

**"To seek the Lord"** (דִרְשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה, v. 13) was the technical term for consulting a prophet or oracle to receive divine guidance. This was standard practice when kings faced important decisions.

**Huldah's double prophecy** (vv. 15–20): She proclaimed both judgment upon Jerusalem and the people (vv. 16–17) and personal mercy for Josiah because of his humble heart (vv. 19–20). The expression "your heart was tender" (רַךְ־לְבָבְךָ) and "you humbled yourself" (וַתִּכָּנַע) describe the attitude that led to Josiah being granted a peaceful death.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Bullae (clay seal impressions) from Josiah's time have been found in Jerusalem. One seal belongs to "Asaiah, the king's servant" (עֲשָׂיָה עֶבֶד הַמֶּלֶךְ), possibly identical with Asaiah mentioned in verse 12. These findings from the City of David confirm that the individuals in the narrative represent historical officials in the late Judean court.
