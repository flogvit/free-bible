## People

In this chapter, the **Servant of the Lord** (עֶבֶד, vv. 3, 5, 6, 7) is introduced in a new and expanded way. While the servant in verse 3 is identified as "Israel," the mission in verses 5–6 is extended to include not only Israel but also the nations. This has led to extensive discussion about the identity of the servant—whether it refers to the nation of Israel, a remnant of the people, the prophet himself, or a future messianic figure. The dual identification (both as Israel and as one who will bring Israel back) reflects a complex theological figure characteristic of Isaiah's Servant Songs.

## Places

The **personification of Zion/Jerusalem** (vv. 14–21) is central to the chapter. Zion is portrayed as a childless, forsaken woman who will have her children returned to her. This literary device was common in ancient Near Eastern poetry, where cities were personified as women or mothers.

The **"land of Sinim"** (אֶרֶץ סִינִים) in verse 12 has been the subject of much debate. Traditionally, this has been identified with Syene (modern-day Aswan in Egypt), or by some with China. The Dead Sea Scrolls (1QIsaᵃ) read "Syenites" (סוניים), which supports an Egyptian location at the southern border. Archaeological findings from Elephantine Island near Aswan confirm that a Jewish community existed there from the 5th century BCE.

## Key Terms

The expression **"light for the nations"** (אוֹר גּוֹיִם, v. 6) is a key term that expands the servant's mission universally. This marks a clear extension from a national to a global perspective in Isaiah's message.

**"I have engraved you on the palms of my hands"** (הֵן עַל־כַּפַּיִם חַקֹּתִיךְ, v. 16) uses the verb חָקַק, meaning to engrave or carve permanently. This image may allude to the practice of tattooing or marking the body with names or symbols—a practice known in antiquity, although usually forbidden under Israelite law (Lev 19:28). Here, the image is used to emphasize God's unforgettable love for Zion.

## Events and Customs

The chapter uses the image of **kings as foster fathers and their princesses as nurses** (מְלָכִים אֹמְנַיִךְ וְשָׂרוֹתֵיהֶם מֵינִקֹתַיִךְ, v. 23). In the ancient Near East, royal foster families were an established institution. Children of high rank were often raised in foreign courts as part of diplomatic alliances. Here, the image is reversed: foreign kings will serve Israel.

**Licking the dust from your feet** (וַעֲפַר רַגְלַיִךְ יְלַחֵכוּ, v. 23) was an extreme gesture of submission known from Assyrian and Babylonian texts, where defeated enemies showed total obedience to their conqueror. Isaiah applies this image to describe the future submission of the nations to Zion.
