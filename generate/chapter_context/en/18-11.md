## People

Zophar the Naamathite (צֹפַר הַנַּעֲמָתִי) speaks for the first time in this chapter. He is the third of Job’s friends to speak in this first round of speeches. The name "Zophar" (צֹפַר) has an uncertain meaning, but may be related to the root צפר, which is connected with birds or dawn. His hometown, Naamah, was likely a city in northwestern Arabia or possibly in the lowlands of Judah. There is a city called Naamah mentioned in Joshua 15:41, but the connection is uncertain. In this chapter, Zophar appears direct and confrontational, with a sharper tone than his friends have shown before.

## Key Terms

**תּוּשִׁיָּה (tushiyyah)** – verse 6 uses this Hebrew word translated as "wisdom" or "understanding." It is a rare word in the Old Testament that denotes a deeper, practical wisdom or sound judgment. Zophar claims that God’s wisdom is "twofold" (כִּפְלַיִם), meaning far beyond human comprehension.

**נָבוּב (navuv)** – in verse 12 this word means "hollow" or "empty inside." The expression "a hollow man" (אִישׁ נָבוּב) describes a person without understanding. The comparison with a wild donkey’s colt (עַיִר פֶּרֶא) was in ancient Near Eastern culture a strong image of uncontrolled, untamed behavior—the wild donkey symbolized freedom from all discipline, but also a lack of wisdom.

## Events and Customs

Zophar’s description in verses 13–14 of "spreading out your hands" (וּפָרַשְׂתָּ כַּפֶּךָ) reflects a common posture of prayer in the ancient Middle East. Stretching out open hands toward heaven was a gesture expressing both submission and readiness to receive from God. The call to remove "iniquity from your tent" (verse 14) refers to the head of the family’s responsibility in patriarchal culture for the moral purity of the entire household—the tent represented not only the home, but one’s whole sphere of life and household.
