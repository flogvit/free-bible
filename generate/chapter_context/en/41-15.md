# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 15

## People

**Pontius Pilate** was the Roman prefect of Judea (c. 26–36 AD). In this chapter, he appears reluctant to condemn Jesus – he recognizes the chief priests' envy (v. 10), attempts to release Jesus (vv. 9-14), and asks, "What evil has he done?" (v. 14). Nevertheless, he yields to the crowd (v. 15). His question, "Are you the king of the Jews?" (v. 2) reflects the political charge against Jesus.

**Barabbas** (Greek: Βαραββᾶς, from Aramaic *bar-abba*, "son of the father") is described as a στασιαστής (rebel) who had committed murder during an uprising (v. 7). This places him among the anti-Roman resistance fighters active in this period.

**Simon of Cyrene** (v. 21) was forced (ἀγγαρεύουσιν – a Persian loanword for compulsory labor) to carry Jesus' cross. He came from Cyrene in Libya, where there was a significant Jewish diaspora. The fact that his sons Alexander and Rufus are named by name suggests they were known to Mark's readers.

**Joseph of Arimathea** (v. 43) is described as an εὐσχήμων βουλευτής – a respected member of the council, likely in the Sanhedrin. He was waiting for the kingdom of God and dared to go to Pilate to ask for Jesus' body. Arimathea is often identified with Ramathaim-Zophim.

**The women at the cross** (vv. 40-41) – Mary Magdalene, Mary (the mother of James the younger and Joses), and Salome – had followed Jesus from Galilee and served him. They observed the crucifixion from a distance and noted where Jesus was buried (v. 47), which becomes important for the resurrection account in chapter 16.

**The centurion** (κεντυρίων, v. 39) was the Roman officer who supervised the crucifixion. His confession, "Truly this man was the Son of God", forms a climax in the Gospel of Mark, where a pagan soldier recognizes what the disciples themselves struggled to understand.

## Places

**The Praetorium** (πραιτώριον, v. 16) was the Roman governor's residence in Jerusalem, where the soldiers gathered the entire cohort (σπεῖρα) to mock Jesus. Scholars debate whether this was Herod's palace on the western side of the city or the Antonia Fortress near the temple area.

**Golgotha** (Γολγοθᾶ, v. 22) is Aramaic for "skull" (Greek: Κρανίου Τόπος). The name may refer to the shape of the place or its function as an execution site. Traditionally, it is located in the area where the Church of the Holy Sepulchre stands today. Archaeological investigations have confirmed that this area was outside the city walls during Jesus' time.

**The rock-hewn tomb** (v. 46) – Joseph bought a linen cloth (σινδών), took down Jesus' body, and laid him in a tomb cut out of the rock, with a rolling stone placed before the entrance. Such tombs were typical for wealthy Jews in this period.

## Events and Customs

**Time references** – Mark uses Jewish timekeeping: The third hour (v. 25, c. 9 AM) indicates the time of crucifixion; the sixth hour (v. 33, c. noon) marks the beginning of darkness; and the ninth hour (v. 34, c. 3 PM) is the time of Jesus' death.

**The Passover release** (v. 6) – the custom of releasing a prisoner during the festival is not documented in other sources outside the Gospels. The practice may have been a local arrangement to ease tensions during the politically charged Passover celebration.

**Flogging before crucifixion** (φραγελλώσας, v. 15) was standard Roman practice. The Roman whip (*flagrum*) was equipped with bone or metal pieces that tore the skin, intended to weaken the condemned and shorten their suffering on the cross.

**Crucifixion** was Rome's most feared method of execution, reserved for slaves, rebels, and non-citizens. The condemned normally carried the crossbeam (*patibulum*) to the place of execution. The execution was designed for maximum shame and prolonged suffering.

**The day of Preparation** (παρασκευή, v. 42) was the day before the Sabbath, which explains Joseph's haste in burying Jesus before sunset.

## Key Terms

**"You say so"** (Σὺ λέγεις, v. 2) is Jesus' only direct answer to Pilate – an ambiguous response that neither confirms nor denies directly, but places responsibility back on the questioner. Jesus' subsequent silence (vv. 3-5) amazed Pilate.

**The purple robe** (πορφύρα, v. 17) and **the crown of thorns** were the soldiers' parody of royal attire. Purple was the color of emperors and kings, and the crown parodied Hellenistic diadems.

**"Eloi, Eloi, lema sabaktani"** (v. 34) is Aramaic and quotes Psalm 22:2 ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"). The confusion with a call to Elijah (v. 35) stems from the acoustic similarity between "Eloi" (my God) and "Elija" (Elijah).

**The curtain** (καταπέτασμα, v. 38) likely refers to the inner curtain that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place in the temple. Its tearing in two symbolizes open access to God's presence.

**Wine mixed with myrrh** (ἐσμυρνισμένον οἶνον, v. 23) was offered to Jesus as a numbing drink, but he refused to accept it. The vinegar drink later (v. 36) was different – sour wine that was common soldier's fare.

## Archaeological Finds

**Remains of crucified individuals** were found in 1968 in a tomb at Giv'at ha-Mivtar north of Jerusalem. The skeleton belonged to a man named Jehohanan, with an iron nail through his heel bone. This confirms that crucified people sometimes received proper burial, as the Gospels describe for Jesus.

**Rolling stone tombs** from the first century are well documented archaeologically in the Jerusalem area, featuring the type of entrance and rolling mechanism described in verse 46.
