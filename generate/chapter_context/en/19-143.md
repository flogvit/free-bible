# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 143

## People

Psalm 143 is attributed to David (לְדָוִד) and is regarded as the last of the seven traditional "penitential psalms" in Christian liturgical tradition. The content of the psalm—with references to persecution from enemies and a feeling of being "crushed to the ground"—has traditionally been linked to periods in David's life when he was on the run, either from King Saul or during Absalom's rebellion. The deep experience of spiritual distress and the need for divine guidance aligns with several critical moments in David's life as described in the books of Samuel.

## Key Terms

The expression **"כְּמֵתֵי עוֹלָם"** (v. 3, "like those long dead") describes a state in which the psalmist feels as though he has been in the realm of the dead for a long time. This reflects the Old Testament concept of Sheol as a dark, silent place where the dead exist in a shadowy existence, cut off from the world of the living and from praising God.

The term **"אֶרֶץ מִישׁוֹר"** (v. 10, "level land" or "plain") is a rich image that combines geographical and moral meaning. In a literal sense, it refers to flat terrain without obstacles, in contrast to the rugged landscape of Judea. Figuratively, it signifies a life characterized by moral uprightness and God's guidance—a path free from spiritual stumbling blocks.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's description of **stretching out hands** toward God (v. 6, "פֵּרַשְׂתִּי יָדַי אֵלֶיךָ") testifies to an ancient posture of prayer in the Middle East. This gesture—with open hands lifted toward heaven—symbolized both submission and receptivity. It was common in Israelite worship and is depicted in Egyptian and Mesopotamian reliefs. The image of the soul as "exhausted earth" thirsting for rain reflects the intense dependence on God that characterizes the tradition of Israelite piety.
