# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 144

## People

The psalm is attributed to David (לְדָוִד) and presents him as a warrior and king. Verse 10 mentions "David your servant" in the third person, which has led some scholars to suggest that the psalm may have been written by a later Davidic king identifying with his ancestor. The double reference to David—both as author and as one delivered from "the sword of evil"—reflects the Davidic dynastic tradition in which successors saw themselves as heirs to God's promises to David.

## Events and Customs

The expression "right hand that swears falsely" (יְמִין שָׁקֶר) in verses 8 and 11 refers to the ancient custom of raising the right hand when taking an oath. In the ancient Near East, an oath was a sacred act invoking divine witness. A false oath taken with the right hand was therefore a particularly serious offense—both against people and against God. This phrasing suggests that the psalmist faces enemies who break treaties or covenant promises.

The image of playing on "the ten-stringed lyre" (בְּנֵבֶל עָשׂוֹר) in verse 9 points to temple music. This type of harp was one of the most important instruments in Levitical worship and was especially associated with praise and thanksgiving.

## Key Terms

The word **צוּר** (tsur, "rock") in verse 1 is a central divine epithet in the Psalms that portrays God as an impregnable fortress and stable foundation. The term draws its power from Israel's mountainous landscape, where rocks provided protection against enemies.

The expression **בְּנֵי נֵכָר** ("foreigners' sons") in verses 7 and 11 denotes not merely foreigners in general, but specifically hostile nations threatening Israel. The wording emphasizes their alienation from the covenant community and Israel's God.

The description of daughters as **כְזָוִיֹּת** ("like corner pillars") in verse 12 uses architectural terminology from temple and palace buildings. These carved corner posts were both structurally important and decorative, creating an image of daughters playing a role that is both supportive and beautifying within the family.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

The combination of war language and agricultural blessings (verses 12–14) reflects the idealized picture of royal prosperity known from Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions. Descriptions of full storehouses, numerous flocks, and peaceful cities were standard elements in royal rhetoric in the ancient Near East. The psalm's use of similar motifs—but with emphasis that such blessing comes from Yahweh—represents a theological reworking of this material.
