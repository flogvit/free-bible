# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 7

## People

**Titus** plays a central role in this chapter as Paul’s envoy to Corinth (vv. 6–7, 13–15). Titus was a Gentile Christian coworker whom Paul deeply trusted. His successful mission in Corinth demonstrates that he possessed considerable diplomatic skills and could handle a difficult situation with wisdom.

## Places

**Macedonia** is mentioned in verse 5 as the place where Paul was when he received news from Titus. This was the Roman province north of Achaia (where Corinth was located) and included cities such as Philippi and Thessalonica. Paul describes the situation there as marked by "struggles without, fears within" (v. 5), suggesting opposition both from outsiders and inner concerns for the churches.

## Events and Customs

The chapter refers to a previous letter Paul had sent to the Corinthians that had caused them grief (v. 8). This is often called the "tearful letter" and is probably not preserved, although some scholars believe it may be identical with 2 Cor 10–13. The letter addressed a specific conflict in which someone had "done wrong" (v. 12), possibly a person who had wronged Paul or challenged his apostolic authority. The church had now taken action against that individual, demonstrating their loyalty.

The expression "with fear and trembling" (v. 15) was a common phrase in antiquity to describe reverent respect, especially toward authorities or divine representatives. Here it describes how the Corinthians received Titus as Paul’s official envoy.

## Key Terms

**Μετάνοια (metanoia)** – "repentance/change of mind" (vv. 9–10) – is a central concept in this chapter. The word literally means "change of mind" and involves a fundamental shift in thinking and direction of life. Paul distinguishes between "godly grief" (κατὰ θεὸν λύπη), which leads to repentance, and "worldly grief", which leads to death. This distinction was important in early Christian ethics and separated Christian repentance from mere guilt or fear of consequences.

**Παρρησία (parrēsia)** – "boldness" (v. 4) – was an important concept in Greek democratic culture, where it denoted the right of free speech belonging to citizens. In a relational context, it described open and trusting communication between friends. Paul uses it here to show that the relationship with the Corinthians has now been restored and is characterized by mutual trust.
