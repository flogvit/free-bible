## Events and Customs

### Graduated Offerings with Accompaniments
The chapter presents a detailed system in which grain, oil, and wine offerings (מִנְחָה and נֶסֶךְ) must accompany animal sacrifices in proportional amounts based on the size of the animal. For a lamb, one-tenth of an ephah of flour mixed with one-quarter of a hin of oil is required, along with one-quarter of a hin of wine as a drink offering; for a ram, two-tenths of an ephah of flour with one-third of a hin of oil and wine; for cattle, three-tenths of an ephah of flour with one-half of a hin of oil and wine.

### The Challah Offering
The command to give "the first portion of the dough" (רֵאשִׁית עֲרִסֹתֵכֶם) establishes an offering connected to bread baking, not merely the grain harvest. The Hebrew word עֲרִסָה refers to dough during kneading or fermentation. This offering, later called "challah" in Jewish tradition, was to be given to the priests and represented acknowledgment that even daily food comes from God.

### The Sabbath-Breaker Incident
The account of the man gathering wood (מְקֹשֵׁשׁ עֵצִים, vv. 32–36) illustrates how specific legal decisions were formulated. He was placed "in custody" (בַּמִּשְׁמָר) because the penalty had not been specified beforehand—the Sabbath command forbade work but did not detail the consequence. Stoning outside the camp was the standard punishment for serious violations and served both as a penalty and as ritual purification of the community. The expression "with a high hand" (בְּיָד רָמָה) in v. 30 describes deliberate, defiant sin as opposed to unintentional sin.

## Key Terms

### Ezrach and Ger (אֶזְרָח / גֵּר)
The chapter repeatedly emphasizes that the same law applies to "the native" (אֶזְרָח) and "the foreigner residing among you" (גֵּר). The term גֵּר does not refer to a casual visitor, but to a non-Israelite who had joined the community permanently and accepted its religious obligations. This legal equality was unusual in the ancient world, where foreigners typically had fewer rights.

### Tsitsit (צִיצִת)
The tassels that were to be attached to the corners of garments, with a blue (תְּכֵלֶת) thread, served as physical reminders of the commandments. The blue dye likely came from the murex snail, making the color valuable and symbolically significant. Tassels or fringes on clothing were common in the ancient Near East, but Israel gave them a distinct theological meaning as visual reminders of covenant obligations.

### Shegagah (שְׁגָגָה)
This term for "unintentional sin" or "error" is central to the chapter’s sin offering system. The distinction between שְׁגָגָה and deliberate sin (בְּיָד רָמָה) was crucial: unintentional violations could be atoned for through offerings, while defiant sin led to being "cut off from one’s people" (כָּרֵת).
