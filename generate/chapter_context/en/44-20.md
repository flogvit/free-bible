# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 20

## People

Chapter 20 names seven of Paul’s traveling companions who represented various congregations: **Sopater, son of Pyrrhus**, from Berea; **Aristarchus** and **Secundus** from Thessalonica; **Gaius** from Derbe; **Timothy**; as well as **Tychicus** and **Trophimus** from the province of Asia. These men likely served as representatives of their congregations, accompanying the collection for Jerusalem (cf. 1 Cor 16:3–4). The fact that they represented Macedonian, Galatian, and Asian churches underscores the ecumenical scope of this solidarity project. **Eutychus** (Greek: "fortunate" or "lucky") was a young man in Troas; his name was common among slaves and freedmen in the Greco-Roman period.

## Places

**Troas** (vv. 5–12) was an important port city on the Aegean Sea in northwest Asia Minor, fully named Alexandria Troas. The city was a Roman colony and a key hub for travel between Asia and Macedonia. The room on the third floor (τριστέγου, "third loft/floor") where the congregation met corresponded to typical apartment buildings (insulae) in Roman cities, where upper floors were often used for gatherings.

**Miletus** (vv. 15–17; meeting vv. 17–38) was an ancient Ionian port city about 50 km south of Ephesus. The city had a famous theater seating 15,000 spectators and was known as a center of trade and culture. Archaeological excavations have uncovered the harbor area where Paul likely met with the elders from Ephesus, as well as an inscription in the theater reserving seats for "Jews and God-fearers"—testimony to Jewish presence in the city.

## Events and Customs

The time references "the days of unleavened bread" (v. 6) and "Pentecost" (v. 16) show that Paul still oriented himself according to the Jewish festival calendar, even while traveling among Gentile regions. The expression "on the first day of the week" (τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων, v. 7) is the earliest clear testimony in Acts that Christians gathered on Sunday. That they assembled "to break bread" (κλάσαι ἄρτον) refers to the Lord’s Supper combined with a fellowship meal, as practiced by the first Christians.

Paul’s action when he leaned over Eutychus and "embraced him" (συμπεριλαβών, v. 10) recalls the resurrections performed by Elijah and Elisha in the Old Testament (1 Kgs 17:21; 2 Kgs 4:34), something the first readers would have recognized as a prophetic parallel.

## Key Terms

The speech at Miletus introduces important leadership terms used for the same individuals: **presbyteroi** (πρεσβύτεροι, "elders," v. 17) and **episkopoi** (ἐπίσκοποι, "overseers," v. 28). This shows that in this early period these terms were functionally overlapping, not separate hierarchical offices. The task is described as to "shepherd" (ποιμαίνειν) the congregation—a shepherd image with deep roots in the Old Testament.

The expression "bound by the Spirit" (δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι, v. 22) is an unusual phrase conveying an irresistible inner compulsion from the Holy Spirit, directed toward Jerusalem despite known dangers.

The saying "it is more blessed to give than to receive" (v. 35) is quoted as a word of Jesus not found in the Gospels. This is an example of so-called "agrapha"—unwritten words of Jesus preserved in oral tradition.
