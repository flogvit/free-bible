## People

**Jephthah** concludes his role in this chapter. He is described as one who "placed my life in my hand" (v. 3), a Hebrew idiom expressing willingness to risk one’s life. His judgeship of six years was relatively short, and he was buried "in the cities of Gilead"—an unusual phrasing that may indicate his grave was not tied to any single city.

**The three "lesser" judges**—Ibzan from Bethlehem, Elon from Zebulun, and Abdon from Pirathon—are presented with brief, formulaic descriptions. Their large families and wealth (Ibzan with 60 children; Abdon with 40 sons and 30 grandsons who rode on 70 donkeys) indicate high social status. Donkeys were status symbols in this period, and the high number demonstrated political influence and prosperity.

## Places

**The fords of the Jordan** played a strategic role in the conflict between Gilead and Ephraim. These crossing points were crucial for communication and trade between areas east and west of the Jordan, and whoever controlled them controlled the movement of people and goods.

**Bethlehem** (vv. 8, 10), from where Ibzan came, may refer either to the well-known city in Judah or to a lesser-known Bethlehem in Zebulun (cf. Josh 19:15). **Pirathon** (vv. 13, 15) lay in the hill country of Ephraim and is commonly identified with today’s Fer'ata, about 10 km southwest of Shechem. The place is described as lying "in the mountains of Amalek," which may refer to an older name for the region.

## Events and Customs

**The Shibboleth test** (v. 6) is one of the earliest documented examples of using linguistic markers to identify enemies. The Ephraimite dialect apparently lacked the shin sound (שׁ), so they pronounced the word as "sibboleth" instead of "shibboleth." The word means either "ear of grain" or "stream/river" in Hebrew. This test revealed that even within Israel there were significant dialect differences among the tribes, reflecting a decentralized social structure during the period of the judges.

**The tribal conflict** between Ephraim and Gilead (vv. 1–6) follows a pattern also seen in Judges 8:1–3, where Ephraim complained about not being called to battle. While Gideon resolved the conflict diplomatically, here it resulted in civil war with 42,000 dead. Ephraim’s aggressive threat to burn down Jephthah’s house reflects the tribal rivalry and pride that characterized this period.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **"פְּלִיטֵי אֶפְרַיִם"** (*pelitei Efrayim*, "fugitives of Ephraim") in verse 4 refers to the Ephraimites’ insult toward the Gileadites, calling them "Ephraimite fugitives"—that is, homeless defectors who had settled between Ephraim and Manasseh. After the victory, the Gileadites turned this label back on the fleeing Ephraimites in verse 5.

The expression **"placed my life in my hand"** (v. 3, Hebrew: *va'asimah nafshi bekhapi*) is an idiom meaning to expose oneself to mortal danger and also appears elsewhere in the Old Testament (1 Sam 19:5; 28:21).
