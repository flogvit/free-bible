## People

**Abner ben Ner** was Saul’s army commander and close relative (likely an uncle or cousin, cf. 1 Sam 14:50–51). He was the most powerful man in the northern kingdom. His role as a "kingmaker" is highlighted by his act of "strengthening his position" in Saul’s house (v. 6). Abner’s decision to switch sides to David was triggered by a conflict with Ish-Bosheth concerning Rizpah, Saul’s concubine. In ancient Near Eastern culture, taking a king’s concubine was a sign of claiming the throne—the accusation was therefore politically charged, not merely moral.

**Rizpah bat Aiah** was Saul’s פִּלֶגֶשׁ (pilegesh, "concubine"). A pilegesh had lower status than a primary wife but was not a concubine in the modern sense—she had legal rights, and her children could inherit. Rizpah is mentioned later in 2 Samuel 21 in connection with the execution of her sons, where her loyalty and grief become a moving image.

**Michal bat Saul** was David’s first wife, given to him as bride-price for 100 Philistine foreskins (1 Sam 18:25–27). Saul later gave her to Paltiel ben Laish (1 Sam 25:44). David’s demand to have her back was both personal and political—as Saul’s son-in-law, he strengthened his legitimate claim to the throne.

**Joab ben Zeruiah** was David’s nephew and army commander. His killing of Abner was revenge for his brother Asahel (2 Sam 2:23), but possibly also motivated by fear of losing his position. The murder took place "בַּשֶּׁלִי" (v. 27)—an expression that can mean "in peace" or "by treachery"—under false pretenses while Abner was in Hebron for peace negotiations. David strongly condemned the act.

## Places

**Hebron** served as David’s capital for seven and a half years. The city is located about 30 km south of Jerusalem in the Judean highlands. As one of the six cities of refuge (Joshua 20:7), Hebron had special legal status, although this protection applied only to unintentional killing. Joab’s murder of Abner "in the middle of the gate" (v. 27) was still a grave act because it occurred during diplomatic peace talks.

**Bahurim** (v. 16), where Paltiel was sent back, lay on the border between Judah and Benjamin, near Jerusalem. The place is mentioned several times as a boundary area with symbolic significance for the relationship between David and Saul’s house.

**Bor-Hassira** ("Sira’s Well," v. 26) is otherwise an unknown location, probably north of Hebron on the road toward the northern territory.

## Events and Customs

**David’s sons in Hebron** (vv. 2–5) represent his growing harem, which was a sign of royal power and alliance-building. His marriage to Maacah, daughter of the king of Geshur (an Aramean kingdom northeast of Galilee), was a diplomatic alliance. Polygamy among kings served political purposes by forging ties with powerful families and neighboring states.

**The mourning ritual for Abner** (vv. 31–35) illustrates public grief in ancient Israel: tearing clothes (קִרְעוּ בִגְדֵיכֶם), putting on sackcloth (שַׂקִּים), and walking in a funeral procession behind the bier. David’s fasting until sunset demonstrated his innocence and honored the dead. The lament (קִינָה) in verses 33–34 opens with the rhetorical question, "Should Abner die as a fool dies?" (הַכְּמוֹת נָבָל יָמוּת אַבְנֵר)—a powerful expression that Abner deserved an honorable death, not treachery.

**The meal after the burial** (v. 35) was a common custom called לְהַבְרוֹת ("to bring bread"). That David refused to eat was unusual and emphasized his deep grief—or his political need to distance himself from the murder.

## Key Terms

The expression **"from Dan to Beersheba"** (v. 10) was the fixed formula for describing all of Israel’s territory, from its northernmost to its southernmost city. This represented Abner’s promise to give David dominion over the whole land.

**"Am I a dog’s head that belongs to Judah?"** (הֲרֹאשׁ כֶּלֶב אָנֹכִי אֲשֶׁר לִיהוּדָה, v. 8) is a powerful insult. "Dog" was one of the strongest terms of abuse in Semitic culture, and Abner sarcastically asks whether he is being treated as a worthless traitor to Judah.

David’s declaration that he is **"weak"** (רַךְ) even though he is anointed king (v. 39) shows his political situation—he was dependent on Zeruiah’s sons (Joab and Abishai) for military power, even though their actions were "too harsh" (קָשִׁים) for him to control.
