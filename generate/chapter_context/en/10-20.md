## People

**Sheba son of Bichri** is introduced as "a worthless man" (אִישׁ בְּלִיַּעַל) from the tribe of Benjamin. His tribal affiliation is significant—as a Benjamite, he belonged to the same tribe as the deposed king Saul. His rebellion thus represents not only political discontent but possibly also a remnant of Saulide resistance against Davidic rule. His slogan "We have no portion in David" (v. 1) is repeated almost verbatim at the division of the kingdom under Rehoboam (1 Kings 12:16), showing deep and enduring tensions between north and south.

**Amasa** was David's nephew and had recently been appointed commander-in-chief in place of Joab (2 Samuel 19:14). His killer, Joab, therefore had both personal and professional motives. Amasa's delay in gathering the men of Judah (v. 5) may indicate either logistical problems or a lack of loyalty within the army, which contributed to his vulnerable position.

**The wise woman from Abel** represents a tradition of female advisors and negotiators in Israelite culture. Her reference to the city's reputation as a place where people sought counsel (v. 18) suggests that certain cities functioned as centers of wisdom.

## Places

**Abel-beth-maacah** (אָבֵלָה בֵּית מַעֲכָה) was located in the far north of Israel, near Dan, at the foot of Mount Hermon. The city is identified with Tell Abil el-Qamh in present-day northern Israel. Archaeological excavations have revealed significant fortifications from the Iron Age. Its location along important trade routes to Aram and Phoenicia gave it strategic importance. The designation "a mother in Israel" (v. 19) suggests that it was an important regional capital.

**Gibeon** (v. 8), where Joab met Amasa, was an ancient cultic site marked by "the great stone." This place had already been the scene of bloody conflict between David's and Saul's men (2 Samuel 2:12–17).

## Events and Customs

**The fate of the concubines** (v. 3) sheds light on a difficult aspect of ancient Near Eastern royal practice. David's ten concubines, whom Absalom had publicly dishonored (16:22), were placed under lifelong house arrest—living as "widows." They could neither be separated from the king nor live normally as his wives, since sexual relations with them were now taboo. This reflects that royal women were tied to issues of legitimacy and inheritance.

**Joab's murder of Amasa** (vv. 9–10) follows the same pattern as the killing of Abner (2 Samuel 3:27): a seemingly friendly meeting with a kiss serving as cover for a fatal sword thrust in the belly. The custom of grasping the beard for a friendly kiss left the victim defenseless. Joab's "loose" sword that fell from its sheath was likely a deliberate trap.

## Key Terms

The expression **"every man to his tents"** (אִישׁ לְאֹהָלָיו, vv. 1 and 22) is an ancient mobilization cry used to disband army gatherings. It points back to the time when Israelites lived in tents and served as a signal that the common military effort had ended.

**"To swallow up the inheritance of the Lord"** (תְבַלַּע נַחֲלַת יְהוָה, v. 19) uses the image of devouring or destroying something that belongs to God Himself—namely, the people and land of Israel. This expression emphasizes that the destruction of an Israelite city was an attack on God's own property.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The closing list of officials in this chapter (vv. 23–26) offers a rare glimpse into Davidic state administration. The list mentions "the Cherethites and Pelethites"—likely royal bodyguards of foreign (possibly Cretan or Philistine) origin. The title "Adoniram over the forced labor" testifies to an established practice of compulsory service that later became unpopular (1 Kings 12:18). That Ira the Jairite is called "priest for David" suggests that the king had a personal court chaplain alongside the chief priests Zadok and Abiathar.
