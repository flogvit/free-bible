## People

**Solomon, son of David, king of Israel** (v. 1): The chapter heading identifies Solomon as the author of Proverbs. The three-part title establishes his authority as a teacher of wisdom through his royal position and lineage back to David.

**Father and mother** (v. 8): The young man is urged to listen to his father's instruction (mûsār) and not forsake his mother's teaching (tôrâ). Both parents are presented as transmitters of wisdom, reflecting the central role of the home in upbringing.

## Key Terms

**Māšāl** (מָשָׁל, "proverb/parable", v. 1, 6): The word encompasses parables, comparisons, and wise sayings, deriving from a root suggesting comparison or likeness. In v. 6 it is used together with mǝlîṣâ (riddle/dark saying) and ḥîdôt (riddles) to describe the wisdom literature the reader is to learn to understand.

**Ḥokmâ/Ḥokmôt** (חָכְמָה/חָכְמוֹת, "wisdom", v. 2, 7, 20): In v. 20 the plural form ḥokmôt is used, which either functions as an intensive ("supreme wisdom") or suggests the many facets of wisdom. Here wisdom is personified as a woman crying out publicly – in sharp contrast to the secret invitation of sinners (v. 11-14).

**Pǝtāʾîm** (פְּתָאיִם, "the simple/inexperienced", v. 4, 22, 32): This describes not foolishness but immaturity – young people who have not yet formed their character and remain open to influence in either direction. In v. 32 it is warned that their "turning away" (mǝšûbâ) will kill them.

**Yirʾat YHWH** (יִרְאַת יְהוָה, "fear of the Lord", v. 7, 29): This chapter presents this as the "beginning" (rēʾšît) of knowledge – either in the sense of "starting point" or "most important thing." In v. 29 rejection of this fear of God is directly linked to the judgment that wisdom warns about.

## Events and Customs

**Wisdom in the square** (v. 20-21): The description of wisdom crying out at the city gates (pǝtāḥê šǝʿārîm) and on busy streets reflects important aspects of urban life in ancient Israel. City gates were centers for legal decisions, commerce, and public debate. Placing wisdom here emphasizes that it is not esoteric or hidden but accessible and insistent – it leaves no one without responsibility.

**The band of robbers' invitation** (v. 11-14): The warnings against joining violent gangs who lie in ambush for the innocent (nǝṣappǝnâ lǝnāqî ḥinnām – "let us lie in wait for the innocent without cause") reflect real dangers. The invitation to "cast your lot among us" (gôrālǝkā tappîl bəṯōkēnû) and have "one purse together" (kîs ʾeḥād) is an invitation to full membership in the group – with all its consequences.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The chapter's structure**: The chapter has three main parts: (1) The book's purpose and motto (v. 1-7), (2) The father's warning against sinners (v. 8-19), (3) The public speech of personified wisdom (v. 20-33). This structure sets up a fundamental contrast: the secret, seductive invitation of sinners versus the public, confronting appeal of wisdom.

**The bird metaphor** (v. 17): The proverb that "in vain is the net spread in the sight of any bird" is debated. It may mean that birds are wise enough to avoid visible traps, while these sinners are not – they set traps for themselves (v. 18).
