# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 12

## Places

The chapter repeatedly mentions "the place that the Lord will choose" (הַמָּקוֹם אֲשֶׁר־יִבְחַר יְהוָה) without naming it directly. This concept of a central worship site is central to the chapter and points forward toward Jerusalem and the temple, even though these are not explicitly mentioned. The chapter also describes the Canaanite places of worship on "the high mountains, the hills, and under every green tree" (verse 2). Archaeological surveys in Canaan have uncovered numerous "high places" (במות) from the Bronze Age on mountain tops and elevations, confirming the biblical description of Canaanite religious practice.

## Events and Customs

The chapter gives detailed instructions for the destruction of Canaanite cultic items: altars (מִזְבְּחֹת), stone pillars (מַצֵּבוֹת), Asherah poles (אֲשֵׁרִים), and carved images (פְּסִילִים). Stone pillars were upright stones that marked sacred sites, and Asherah poles were wooden posts or images associated with the fertility goddess Asherah. Archaeological finds from sites such as Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom have uncovered inscriptions and cult objects showing that such practices also occurred in an Israelite context.

The chapter introduces an important distinction between sacrificial slaughter at the central sanctuary and ordinary slaughter for food in hometowns. Later rabbinic tradition refers to this as "secular slaughter" (זְבִיחַת חֻלִּין). Previously, all slaughter had to be performed at an altar, but due to the distance from the central worship site, ordinary meat consumption is now permitted locally—compared to eating game such as gazelle and deer (verses 15, 22). The prohibition against eating blood (verses 16, 23–25) is justified by the statement that "the blood is the life" (הַדָּם הוּא הַנָּפֶשׁ), a concept with deep roots in ancient Near Eastern thought.

## Key Terms

The expression לָשׂוּם אֶת־שְׁמוֹ שָׁם ("to set his name there," verse 5) represents the theological concept of God's "name" as His presence and ownership. This stands in contrast to the idea that God Himself dwells in the sanctuary, instead emphasizing God's transcendence. Parallels are found in Akkadian royal inscriptions where rulers "set their name" (šuma šakānu) on territories they have conquered, as an expression of sovereignty and ownership.

The terms "rest" (מְנוּחָה) and "inheritance" (נַחֲלָה) in verse 9 refer to the future state in the land after conquest—both physical rest from enemies and spiritual rest in proper worship.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verse 31 notes that the Canaanites "burn their sons and daughters in the fire for their gods." Child sacrifice was a documented practice in the Canaanite cultural sphere, especially associated with the god Molech. Archaeological finds from Carthage (Punic culture with Canaanite roots) have uncovered so-called "tophet" sanctuaries containing urns with burned children's bones, confirming that such practices occurred. Phoenician and Punic inscriptions also refer to this type of sacrifice as a "molk" offering.
