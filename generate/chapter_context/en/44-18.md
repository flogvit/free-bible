# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 18

## People

**Aquila and Priscilla** (Ἀκύλας, Πρίσκιλλα) were a Jewish couple who became key co-workers with Paul. Aquila originally came from Pontus by the Black Sea. The couple had recently arrived in Corinth after Emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome. The Roman historian Suetonius mentions this expulsion and links it to disturbances "because of Chrestus" – likely a reference to conflicts within the Jewish colony over Jesus as the Messiah. The expulsion is usually dated to 49 AD, providing an important anchor for the chronology in Acts.

**Gallio** was proconsul (ἀνθύπατος) of the province of Achaia. He was the brother of the philosopher Seneca and uncle of the poet Lucan. An inscription found in Delphi (the so-called "Gallio Inscription") dates his term of office to approximately 51–52 AD. This is one of the most secure chronological anchors in the entire New Testament. Gallio's refusal to hear the case against Paul established an important precedent: Romans regarded Christianity as an internal Jewish religious dispute, not a threat to Roman law.

**Crispus** (Κρίσπος) was the synagogue ruler (ἀρχισυνάγωγος) in Corinth. Paul mentions in 1 Corinthians 1:14 that he personally baptized Crispus. **Sosthenes** (Σωσθένης), who succeeded Crispus as synagogue ruler, was beaten before the judge's seat. Many scholars identify him with the Sosthenes mentioned as a co-sender in 1 Corinthians 1:1, which may indicate that he too later came to faith.

**Titius Justus** (Τιτίου Ἰούστου, with textual variation in the manuscripts) was a God-fearing man whose house stood next door to the synagogue. After Paul turned away from the synagogue, this house became the starting point for his ministry.

**Apollos** (Ἀπολλῶς) was a Jewish scholar from Alexandria in Egypt. Alexandria was an important center of Jewish learning, known for allegorical interpretation of Scripture (represented by Philo). Luke describes him as "learned" (λόγιος) and "mighty in the Scriptures" – terms pointing to thorough rhetorical and theological training. His knowledge limited only to "John's baptism" suggests that there were groups who knew about Jesus' movement through John the Baptist's preaching, but without full knowledge of the Pentecost event and the gift of the Holy Spirit.

## Places

**Corinth** was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia and one of the most significant cities of antiquity. The city was strategically located on the isthmus between mainland Greece and the Peloponnese, with harbors facing both east (Cenchreae) and west (Lechaeum). This made Corinth a vital hub for trade between East and West. The city was notorious in antiquity for immorality – the verb "korinthiazesthai" (to live like a Corinthian) meant to live immorally.

**Βῆμα** (the judge's seat/bench) where Paul was brought before Gallio has been identified archaeologically. It was a large, raised platform in the center of the forum in Corinth, built of white and blue marble. Here Roman officials delivered public speeches and pronounced judgments. This structure is well preserved and can still be seen today.

**Cenchreae** (Κεγχρεαῖς) was Corinth's eastern port city facing the Aegean Sea, about 11 km from the city itself. Here Paul cut his hair because of a vow. Romans 16:1 mentions a church in Cenchreae with Phoebe as deacon, showing that Christianity spread quickly to this port town.

**Ephesus** was the provincial capital of Asia and one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire. The city was known for the Temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of antiquity. Paul's brief visit here (vv. 19–21) laid the foundation for his later, longer stay (chapter 19).

## Events and Customs

**Tentmaking** (σκηνοποιοί), which Paul shared with Aquila and Priscilla, involved working with leather and textiles – making tents, awnings, and similar products. It was common in Jewish tradition for learned rabbis also to have a trade. This gave Paul financial independence and the ability to preach without being accused of exploiting his listeners. In Corinth there was great demand for such products due to the large markets and the Isthmian Games held every two years.

**Shaking the dust from one's clothes** (ἐκτιναξάμενος τὰ ἱμάτια) was a symbolic act expressing that one disclaimed responsibility for another's fate. This symbolic action was known from Jewish practice where people shook dust from their feet when leaving pagan territory. Paul's words "Your blood be on your own heads" (Τὸ αἷμα ὑμῶν ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλήν) is a formula of judgment known from the Old Testament (cf. 2 Sam 1:16; Ezek 33:4).

**The Nazirite vow**, mentioned in connection with Paul's haircutting at Cenchreae, was regulated in Numbers 6. Such a vow involved abstaining from wine, not cutting one's hair, and avoiding contact with the dead. When the vow period ended, the hair was cut and burned as an offering in the temple. That Paul took such a vow shows that he continued to practice Jewish piety customs even while preaching to Gentiles.

## Key Terms

**Ἀνθύπατος** (proconsul) was the title of the governor of a senatorial province – a province governed by the Roman Senate rather than directly by the emperor. Achaia was such a province, and Luke's use of the correct title demonstrates his accuracy in administrative details.

**Σεβόμενος τὸν θεόν** (God-fearing) describes Titius Justus and refers to Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel, followed Jewish morality, and participated in synagogue services, but without becoming full proselytes through circumcision. These "God-fearers" constituted an important target group for the early Christian mission.

**Τὴν ὁδὸν τοῦ κυρίου** (the way of the Lord) and **τὴν ὁδὸν τοῦ θεοῦ** (the way of God) were early designations for the Christian faith and lifestyle. The term "the Way" is used several times in Acts as a name for the Christian movement (cf. 9:2; 19:9, 23; 22:4; 24:14, 22).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Gallio Inscription** from Delphi is the most important archaeological find for dating Paul's ministry. The inscription is a letter from Emperor Claudius mentioning Gallio as proconsul of Achaia. Based on this inscription, Gallio's term of office can be dated to about 51–52 AD, which places Paul's eighteen-month stay in Corinth at approximately 50–52 AD.
