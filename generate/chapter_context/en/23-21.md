# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 21

## Places

**The Desert of the Sea (מִדְבַּר־יָם)**: This enigmatic expression in verse 1 likely refers to Babylon, which lay near the great rivers Euphrates and Tigris in an area of extensive marshes and canals. The southern part of Mesopotamia was known for its wetlands. The term may also hint that Babylon would become like a desert—destroyed and abandoned.

**Elam and Media**: These two nations are mentioned in verse 2 as attackers. Elam lay east of the Tigris with its capital at Susa (in present-day southwestern Iran), while Media was located in northwestern Iran. Historically, it was precisely an alliance of Medes and Persians under Cyrus that conquered Babylon in 539 BC. Isaiah’s prophecy thus points forward to this event.

**Dumah, Seir, and Arabia**: Dumah (v. 11) was an oasis in northern Arabia, while Seir denotes the mountainous region of Edom southeast of the Dead Sea. The Dedanites (v. 13) were an Arabian trading people, and Tema (v. 14) was a major oasis city along trade routes in northwestern Arabia. Kedar (vv. 16–17) was a powerful Bedouin tribe in the Syro-Arabian desert, known as skilled archers.

## Events and Customs

**The Watchman’s Role**: Verses 6–8 describe the prophet as a "metsappeh" (מְצַפֶּה)—a watcher or lookout stationed on high ground. In ancient cities, watchmen were placed on walls or in towers to warn of approaching enemy armies. Here the image is used metaphorically for the prophet’s spiritual calling to keep watch and convey what he "sees" from God.

**The Feast Interrupted**: Verse 5 depicts a banquet with tables set, eating and drinking, followed by a sudden cry to anoint shields for battle. This may allude to the historical account of Babylon’s fall, when King Belshazzar was holding a feast as the Persians entered the city (cf. Daniel 5). Anointing shields with oil was a preparation for combat—leather shields were oiled to keep them supple and effective.

## Key Terms

**Massa (מַשָּׂא)**: This word, which introduces all three prophecies in the chapter (vv. 1, 11, 13), means "burden" or "lifted-up message." It signals a heavy message of judgment—a prophetic load placed upon a nation. The repetition underscores the seriousness of these oracles.

**Bogéd and shodéd (בּוֹגֵד, שׁוֹדֵד)**: Verse 2 uses these words—"the betrayer betrays" and "the destroyer destroys"—in an intensive repetition that emphasizes total treachery and devastation. This may refer to Babylon’s own history of betraying other nations, which now turns back upon itself.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Babylonian chronicles and Persian inscriptions confirm the conquest of Babylon in 539 BC. The Cyrus Cylinder, discovered in Babylon in 1879, describes how Cyrus took the city without major fighting—consistent with the picture of an unprepared people in the midst of a feast. Assyrian reliefs also show the combination of camel and donkey caravans that Isaiah describes in verse 7, typical of trade and military traffic in the region.
