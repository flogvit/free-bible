# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 100

## Events and Customs

The heading "A psalm of thanksgiving" (מִזְמוֹר לְתוֹדָה) links this psalm directly to the *todah* offering, a specific peace offering described in Leviticus 7:12–15. This was a voluntary sacrifice brought when someone wished to express gratitude to God, often after deliverance from illness, danger, or distress. The offering included an animal along with unleavened bread and cakes, and the meat had to be eaten on the same day—requiring that family and friends be invited to a shared festive meal. The psalm likely served as a processional song as grateful worshippers approached the temple with their offerings.

Verse 4 calls for ritual movement through "his gates" and "his courts," reflecting the physical layout of the temple complex. Worshippers first passed through the outer gates, then entered the court where the altar stood. This processional practice was central to Israelite worship and followed specific routes and rituals.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word *todah* (תּוֹדָה) in the heading carries a double meaning essential for understanding the psalm: it refers both to the thanksgiving offering itself and to the verbal confession or praise that accompanied it. Thus, the act of sacrifice and the spoken thanks are inseparably linked in Israelite thought.

The phrase "his flock" (צֹאן מַרְעִיתוֹ) uses the shepherd metaphor deeply rooted in Middle Eastern culture. Kings in Egypt and Mesopotamia were often described as shepherds of their people, but here it is God himself who is the shepherd—a radical theological claim about who truly rules and protects the people.
