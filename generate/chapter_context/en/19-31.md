# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 31

## Key Concepts

Psalm 31 contains several Hebrew expressions with deep theological significance. The expression **"בְּיָדְךָ אַפְקִיד רוּחִי"** (bejadkhá afkíd rukhí – "Into your hand I commit my spirit", v. 6) is especially meaningful. The verb *pakad* is used for entrusting something valuable to another's care, as one would leave valuables with a trustworthy guardian. Jesus quoted these words on the cross (Luke 23:46), which shows the psalm's enduring significance in Jewish and Christian tradition.

The expression **"מָגוֹר מִסָּבִיב"** (magór missavív – "terror from every side", v. 14) is a characteristic phrase that also appears with the prophet Jeremiah (Jer 6:25; 20:3, 10). This expression describes a state of being completely surrounded by enemies and danger, a situation well known to Israelites in the turbulent political reality of the ancient Middle East.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's description of social ostracism (vv. 11–13) reflects important aspects of Old Testament society. Being "forgotten like a dead person" and compared to a "broken vessel" describes complete social marginalization. In a culture where honor and shame were fundamental values, and where community was essential for survival, such exclusion represented an existential crisis. Neighbors and friends turning away (v. 12) could be due to fear of being associated with someone who was persecuted or under God's judgment.

## Places

The reference to **"עִיר מָצוֹר"** (ír matsór – "a besieged city", v. 22) points to a concrete historical experience. Siege warfare was a common military method in the ancient Middle East, and Israelites experienced this repeatedly. The expression can be understood both literally—that the psalmist experienced God's grace during an actual siege—or metaphorically as a situation where he felt surrounded by enemies. Archaeological findings from cities such as Lachish show the dramatic conditions during sieges, with lack of water and hunger as constant threats.
