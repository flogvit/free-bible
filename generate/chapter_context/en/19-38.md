## Key Concepts

The heading "לְהַזְכִּיר" (lehazkir, "to bring to remembrance" or "for a memorial offering") links this psalm to a specific cultic context. The term likely refers to "אַזְכָּרָה" (azkara), the portion of the grain offering that was burned on the altar as a "memorial offering" to the Lord (Lev 2:2, 9, 16). This heading appears only in Psalms 38 and 70, suggesting that the psalm may have been used in connection with specific sacrificial rituals, perhaps as a prayer asking God to "remember" the sufferer.

The expression "חִצֶּיךָ נִחֲתוּ בִי" ("your arrows have pierced me," v. 3) uses a metaphor well known in the ancient Near East. Disease and suffering were often understood as divine arrows striking people. This idea is also found in Mesopotamian literature, where the plague god Nergal sends his arrows against humanity.

## Events and Customs

The psalm’s detailed description of physical suffering—foul, rotting sores (v. 6), a bent and bowed body (v. 7), burning loins (v. 8)—reflects an ancient Near Eastern understanding of the connection between sin and sickness. In Israelite thought, serious illness could be seen as a consequence of sin and God’s discipline, a theme also expressed in the book of Job and in the blessings and curses of the law (Deut 28).

The description that friends and relatives keep their distance (v. 12) may reflect actual purity laws related to certain skin diseases. According to Leviticus 13–14, persons with certain skin conditions were required to be isolated from the community. The social isolation described was therefore not only emotionally painful but also culturally and religiously mandated.
