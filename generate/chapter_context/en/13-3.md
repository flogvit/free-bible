# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 3

## People

The chapter presents the complete royal Davidic lineage from David himself through many generations after the exile. David’s sons born in Hebron (vv. 1–4) include Amnon, Daniel (called Chileab in 2 Samuel 3:3), Absalom, Adonijah, Shephatiah, and Ithream. The variation between the names Daniel and Chileab may be due to the person having two names, a common practice in ancient times.

Zerubbabel (v. 19) is historically confirmed as the Persian-appointed governor of Judah around 520 B.C.E. His name is Akkadian (Zēr-Bābili, "Offspring of Babylon"), indicating that he was born in exile. He led the rebuilding of the temple according to Ezra and Haggai. The list extends to at least six to ten generations after Zerubbabel (vv. 19–24), dating the end of the chapter to around 400 B.C.E. or later.

## Places

**Hebron** (vv. 1, 4) was David’s first capital, where he reigned for seven and a half years over Judah alone. The city is located about 30 kilometers south of Jerusalem in the Judean highlands. Hebron’s significance as a royal seat before Jerusalem underscores the transition from tribal kingship to a united monarchy.

**Geshur** (v. 2) was a small Aramean kingdom northeast of the Sea of Galilee. David’s marriage to Maacah, daughter of King Talmai of Geshur, represents a diplomatic alliance. Absalom later fled there after killing Amnon.

## Key Terms

The expression **אַסִּיר** (assir, v. 17) means "prisoner" and may either be a proper name or describe Jehoiachin’s status as a Babylonian captive. The Masoretic text is ambiguous here, and several translations interpret this differently.

**פִּילַגְשִׁים** (pilagsjiim, v. 9) refers to concubines, women of lower status than full wives. Their children had limited inheritance rights, which explains why they are not named here. The mention of Tamar as a sister underscores her significance despite the tragedy she experienced.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Several kings in this chapter are confirmed by extrabiblical sources. **Hezekiah** (v. 13) is mentioned in the annals of the Assyrian king Sennacherib (the Taylor Prism) from around 701 B.C.E. **Manasseh** (v. 13) appears in Assyrian sources from the time of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. **Jehoiachin** (vv. 16–17) is documented in Babylonian ration lists from Nebuchadnezzar’s palace, where he is called "Yaukin, king of Judah" and received food while in captivity.

Zerubbabel’s name may be attested on seals from the Persian period found in Judah, although this identification remains debated among scholars. These findings confirm the historical grounding of the genealogy through the monarchy and into the exile period.
