# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 11

## Places

**Bethphage and Bethany** were two villages on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, about 3 km from Jerusalem. Bethphage (Greek: Βηθφαγή) was closer to Jerusalem and marked the ritual boundary of the city. Bethany (Greek: Βηθανία) served as Jesus' base during Holy Week (vv. 11–12, 19).

**The Mount of Olives** (Greek: τὸ Ὄρος τῶν Ἐλαιῶν) was a ridge east of Jerusalem with strong messianic significance. According to Zechariah 14:4, the Messiah would come to Jerusalem from this mountain. Entering from this direction would immediately evoke messianic associations among the onlookers.

**The Temple** (Greek: τὸ ἱερόν) refers to the entire temple complex, not just the sanctuary itself. Herod the Great's temple was one of the most impressive structures of antiquity. The commercial activity took place primarily in "the court of the Gentiles" – the outermost courtyard, which was the only place where non-Jews could worship.

## Events and Customs

**The entry on a colt** had deep symbolic meaning. In ancient times, kings rode donkeys during peacetime, while horses were associated with war (cf. Zechariah 9:9). An animal "on which no one has ever yet sat" (Greek: ἐφʼ ὃν οὐδεὶς οὔπω ἀνθρώπων ἐκάθισεν) was ritually pure and suitable for sacred use, corresponding to the requirements in Numbers 19:2 and Deuteronomy 21:3. The custom of laying cloaks on the road is known from royal acclamations in the Old Testament (2 Kings 9:13).

**The cleansing of the temple** must be understood in light of the commercial activity that had developed. Money changers (Greek: κολλυβιστῶν) converted foreign currency into the Tyrian shekel required for the temple tax and charged fees for this service. Dove sellers offered sacrifices that the poor could afford (Leviticus 12:8). This trade was necessary in itself, but Jesus criticized that it took place within the temple grounds, especially in the court of the Gentiles, thereby hindering non-Jews from worshiping. The expression "den of robbers" (Greek: σπήλαιον λῃστῶν) alludes to Jeremiah 7:11 and accused the merchants of exploitation.

**The fig tree** had special symbolic significance in Jewish tradition, where it often represented Israel (Hosea 9:10; Micah 7:1). The fact that the tree had leaves but no fruit was unusual, since fig trees normally bear early figs (Heb. *paggim*) before the leaves are fully developed. This action functions as a prophetic symbolic act, similar to those of Old Testament prophets.

## People

**The chief priests, the scribes, and the elders** (Greek: οἱ ἀρχιερεῖς καὶ οἱ γραμματεῖς καὶ οἱ πρεσβύτεροι) constituted the three groups that formed the Sanhedrin, the Jewish council. The chief priests included the current high priest, former high priests, and members of the leading priestly families. Their question about Jesus' "authority" (Greek: ἐξουσία) was legally grounded – the cleansing of the temple was a direct challenge to their authority over the temple area.

## Key Terms

**Hosanna** (Greek: Ὡσαννά) is a transliteration of the Hebrew *hoshia-na* ("save now"), from Psalm 118:25. By Jesus' time, the word had developed from a prayer into an acclamation. The quote "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" (v. 9) is from Psalm 118:26, one of the Hallel psalms (Psalms 113–118) that were sung during the major festivals, including Passover.

**Κύριος** (*kyrios*, "Lord") appears twice in the chapter with rich meaning. In verse 3 (Ὁ κύριος αὐτοῦ χρείαν ἔχει), it may mean the "owner" of the donkey, but in the context of the Gospel of Mark, it also carries messianic overtones. In verse 9 (ἐν ὀνόματι κυρίου, from Psalm 118:26), it refers to God, but the crowd's usage suggests a messianal confession about Jesus.
