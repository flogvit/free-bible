# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 114

## Events and Customs

Psalm 114 is part of the "Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), a collection of songs of praise sung during major Jewish festivals, especially Passover (pesach). During the Passover meal (seder), Psalms 113–114 were sung before the meal and Psalms 115–118 after. This psalm thus has a central liturgical function connected to the celebration of the exodus from Egypt. The tradition of singing Hallel at Passover dates back to the Second Temple period and continues in Jewish practice to this day.

The psalm refers directly to two great miracles during the wilderness journey: the parting of the Red Sea (Exod 14) and the crossing of the Jordan (Josh 3–4). By mentioning both events together, the psalm frames the entire wilderness journey from beginning to end as one continuous narrative of salvation.

## Places

**The sea (הַיָּם) and the Jordan (הַיַּרְדֵּן)** form the geographical framework of the psalm. "The sea" refers to the Red Sea (Hebrew יַם־סוּף, often translated "Red Sea") at the time of the exodus from Egypt, while the Jordan marks the entrance into the Promised Land—about 40 years later. These two bodies of water represent the boundaries of the Israelites' wilderness journey.

**The mountains and hills** that "skipped like rams" likely allude to events at Sinai, where the mountain shook when God came down (Exod 19:18). The location of Mount Sinai is debated, but it has traditionally been identified with Jebel Musa on the Sinai Peninsula.

## Key Terms

The word **לֹעֵז** (*loʿez*) in verse 1 means "foreign language" or "barbarous speech" and describes Egypt as a people with unintelligible speech. This emphasizes the Israelites' foreignness in Egypt and that they were surrounded by a culture not their own.

The expression **קָדְשׁוֹ** (*qodsho*, "his sanctuary") in verse 2 is unusually used to refer to Judah as a people, rather than to a place. The entire nation is described as God's holy possession and his "dominion" (מַמְשְׁלוֹתָיו).

The word **חַלָּמִישׁ** (*challamish*) in verse 8 denotes flint or especially hard rock, in contrast to **צוּר** (*tsur*, ordinary rock) earlier in the same verse. Both words refer to the event at Meribah where Moses struck water from the rock (Exod 17:6; Num 20:11).

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The concept that nature responds to a divine presence is also found in surrounding cultures. Canaanite texts from Ugarit (ca. 1400–1200 BCE) describe similar scenes where mountains and seas respond to the presence of gods. However, Psalm 114 uses this motif polemically: while Canaanite religion viewed the sea (Yam) as a deity, the psalm portrays the sea as a creature fleeing in fear before Israel's God.
