# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 27

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the construction of the altar of burnt offering and the courtyard of the tabernacle. The altar of burnt offering (מִזְבֵּחַ, *mizbeach*) with horns on its four corners was a widespread form throughout the ancient Near East. The altar horns served several functions: they provided handholds during sacrifices, blood was applied to them in special rituals, and they functioned as places of refuge for those seeking asylum (1 Kings 1:50). The altar was made of acacia wood (עֲצֵי שִׁטִּים, *atsej shittim*) overlaid with bronze—a practical solution that combined lightness for transport with durability against heat.

The instructions for the continual lamp (נֵר תָּמִיד, *ner tamid*) in verses 20–21 form the basis of a practice still observed in Jewish synagogues. The oil was to be pure, pressed olive oil of the highest quality. Aaron and his sons were to tend the lamp from evening until morning (מֵעֶרֶב עַד־בֹּקֶר)—a perpetual statute (חֻקַּת עוֹלָם) for future generations.

## Key Terms

**נְחֹשֶׁת** (*nechoshet*) – traditionally translated as "copper" or "bronze." In this period, it most likely refers to bronze, an alloy of copper and tin that was the dominant metal for tools and weapons in the Bronze Age. Bronze was used for outdoor objects (the altar, the courtyard pillars), while gold was reserved for the inner holy area.

**חָצֵר** (*chatser*) – "courtyard" – describes the enclosed area surrounding the tabernacle. The dimensions (100 by 50 cubits) created a rectangular sacred space that separated the holy from the common. The courtyard was accessible to Israelites bringing offerings, while the tabernacle itself was reserved for the priests.

**שֵׁשׁ מָשְׁזָר** (*shesh moshzar*) – "fine twisted linen" – refers to high-quality linen that was spun and twisted for extra strength and beauty. Such linen was a luxury item in antiquity and is associated with Egyptian textile craftsmanship.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations have uncovered altars with horns from the Iron Age, including at Beersheba, confirming that the four-horned altar form was widespread in Israelite practice. The Beersheba altar, reconstructed from dressed stones found reused in a wall, displays four distinct horns and confirms the biblical description.
