# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 43

## People

In this chapter, **Judah** emerges as the leading spokesman among the brothers, a role that previously belonged to Reuben (the firstborn). Judah’s prominent position here—where he offers himself as guarantor for Benjamin—reflects a shift in power within the family. The phrase "to stand surety" (עָרַב, *'arav*) used by Judah in verse 9 was a legally binding action in the ancient Near East, whereby the guarantor assumed full responsibility if the agreement were broken.

## Events and Customs

**Gift-giving to officials**: Jacob sent his sons with a "minḥah" (מִנְחָה)—a tribute or gift consisting of balm, honey, ladanum, myrrh, pistachio nuts, and almonds (v. 11). These were valuable trade goods from Canaan that were highly prized in Egypt. Archaeological findings confirm that these products were part of the well-organized trade between Canaan and Egypt during the Bronze Age. Gift-giving was a necessary part of diplomatic and business dealings with high-ranking persons in the ancient Near East.

**Separate eating**: Verse 32 describes how the Egyptians ate separately from the Hebrews because it was an "abomination" (תּוֹעֵבָה, *to'evah*) for Egyptians to eat with them. This reflects Egyptian purity laws and cultural exclusivity. Egyptian sources confirm that they regarded foreigners, especially shepherds, as ritually unclean. This practice of ritual separation during meals was well documented in ancient Egypt.

**Seating by age**: The fact that the brothers were seated exactly according to birth order (v. 33) caused great astonishment. In a culture where age and birthright were decisive for status and inheritance, a foreigner could not possibly know this without supernatural insight or intimate knowledge of the family.

## Key Terms

**"El Shaddai"** (אֵל שַׁדַּי) in verse 14 is the divine name Jacob uses when he blesses his sons before their departure. This divine name is used especially in patriarchal blessings (Gen 17:1; 28:3; 35:11) and here expresses Jacob’s trust in God’s almighty power and care in a desperate situation.

**"Double silver"** (כֶּסֶף מִשְׁנֶה, *kesef mishneh*) refers to the brothers taking twice as much silver—both to pay for new grain and to return the mysteriously placed silver. Silver functioned as a medium of payment by weight during this period, before coinage became common.

**"Matsaot"** (מַשְׂאֹת) in verse 34 denotes portions of food sent from the host’s table to guests as an honor. That Benjamin received five times as much as the others (חָמֵשׁ יָדוֹת, "five portions") marked him as especially honored. The number five appears several times in the Joseph story (cf. 45:22; 47:2). This may have tested the brothers’ reaction to favoritism, given their history with Joseph.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian wall paintings from the Middle Kingdom (ca. 2055–1650 BCE) and the New Kingdom (ca. 1550–1070 BCE) depict banquet scenes where guests are seated according to status, and servants bring portions from the host’s table. Particularly relevant are depictions in tombs showing Semitic merchants and visitors at Egyptian courts, wearing characteristic colorful clothing. These images confirm the cultural context for the meeting between Joseph’s brothers and the Egyptian court as described in this chapter.
