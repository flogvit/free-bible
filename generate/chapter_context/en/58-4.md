## People

### Joshua (Ἰησοῦς)
In verse 8, Joshua is mentioned by the Greek name "Ἰησοῦς" (Iēsous), which is identical to the name "Jesus". This was the Greek form of the Hebrew name "Yehoshua" (יְהוֹשֻׁעַ). The author argues that although Joshua led the Israelites into the promised land, he did not give them final rest—otherwise David, several hundred years later, would not still have spoken about a future day of rest. This name similarity between Joshua and Jesus is theologically significant: the Joshua who could not give complete rest points forward to Jesus who can.

### David as Prophetic Author
King David is mentioned in verse 7 as the messenger of God's word "after so long a time"—a reference to Psalm 95, which was written many centuries after the wilderness wandering. The author uses this chronological argument to show that God's promise of rest still stood open in David's day, and thus also for the readers of the letter.

## Events and Customs

### The Wilderness Wandering Account as a Warning Example
The chapter continues the reference from chapter 3 to the Israelites' disobedience at Kadesh-Barnea (Numbers 14). The expression "they did not enter because of disobedience" (διʼ ἀπείθειαν, verse 6) refers to that generation which refused to enter Canaan after the report of the spies. In Jewish tradition, this event became a standard example of catastrophic unbelief. This generation had to wander in the wilderness for forty years until all over twenty years old had died, except Caleb and Joshua.

### Sabbath Observance
The reference to God's rest on the seventh day (verse 4, from Genesis 2:2) and the rare word "Sabbath rest" (verse 9) connect with Jewish Sabbath practice. For Jewish readers, the Sabbath was the highlight of the week—a holy day of rest from work, worship, and communal meals. The author elevates this familiar concept to an eschatological level: the ultimate Sabbath rest awaiting God's people.

## Key Terms

### Κατάπαυσις (katapausis) – "rest"
This word appears nine times in chapters 3–4 and is the central theme of the chapter. The Greek word can mean both physical rest, cessation of work, and a place of rest. In the Septuagint it is used concerning Canaan as Israel's land of rest and concerning God's own rest after creation. The author weaves these meanings together typologically: Canaan was a provisional fulfillment, but God's own Sabbath rest points to a deeper, spiritual rest that still stands open.

### Σαββατισμός (sabbatismos) – "Sabbath rest"
This word in verse 9 appears only here in the entire New Testament and is rare also in other Greek literature. It seems to have been formed specifically to express a Sabbath-like rest—not merely cessation of work (katapausis), but a celebration and completion corresponding to God's own rest on the seventh day. The word emphasizes that the final rest has a quality surpassing both Canaan's rest and the weekly Sabbath.

### Μάχαιραν δίστομον (machairan distomon) – "two-edged sword"
The image of God's living and active word as a two-edged sword (verse 12) uses a familiar image from antiquity. A two-edged sword was sharp on both sides and known for its penetrating power. In Jewish wisdom literature, God's word was often described with similar power (cf. Isaiah 49:2; Wisdom of Solomon 18:15–16). The expression "dividing soul and spirit, joints and marrow" uses anatomical language to describe the word's ability to reach into a person's innermost being.

### Ἀρχιερεὺς μέγας (archiereus megas) – "great high priest"
The title "great high priest" (verse 14) introduces the theme that dominates the rest of the letter. In the Old Testament, the high priest was the only one who could enter the Most Holy Place once a year on the Day of Atonement. That Jesus has "passed through the heavens" corresponds to the high priest's passage through the veil, but surpasses it by reaching all the way into God's presence.
