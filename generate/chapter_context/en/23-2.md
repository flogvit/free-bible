## Places

**The Mountain of the Lord and the House of the Lord** (v. 2–3) refers to the temple hill in Jerusalem where Solomon’s Temple stood. In ancient Near Eastern thought, the dwellings of gods were located on mountain peaks, and the vision that "the mountain of the Lord" shall be "highest among the mountains" expresses Yahweh’s sovereignty over all other deities. The image of nations streaming to Zion contrasts with the reality of that time, when Jerusalem was a relatively small city surrounded by more powerful nations.

**Lebanon and Bashan** (v. 13) were known for their cedar and oak trees. Lebanon cedars were the most valuable building timber in antiquity and symbolized pride and strength. Bashan (today’s Golan Heights) was famous for its mighty oaks and lush grazing lands.

**Ships of Tarshish** (v. 16) denote large merchant vessels designed for long-distance trade. Tarshish is often identified with Tartessos in Spain, and these ships represented the most advanced maritime technology and international wealth.

## Events and Customs

The prophecy criticizes practices widespread in Judah: **customs from the east** and **divination like that of the Philistines** (v. 6). This alludes to foreign religious customs and divination techniques—methods for seeking divine guidance apart from Yahweh. The accumulation of **silver, gold, horses, and chariots** (v. 7) broke with Deuteronomic ideals for kingship (Deut 17:16–17).

## Key Terms

**אַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים** (*acharit hajamim*, "in days to come", v. 2) is an expression in prophetic literature that denotes a future time when God will intervene and establish His order—not necessarily the end of the world, but a decisive turning point in history.

**אֱלִילִים** (*elilim*, "idols", v. 8, 18, 20) is a wordplay: the word resembles *elohim* (God), but means "nullities" or "empty things." Isaiah uses it deliberately in a derogatory sense to emphasize the powerlessness of idols.

**יוֹם יְהוָה** (*jom Yahweh*, "the day of the Lord", v. 12) was originally a term Israelites associated with God’s victory over enemies. Isaiah reverses this expectation: the day of the Lord will strike all human pride, even within Israel.
