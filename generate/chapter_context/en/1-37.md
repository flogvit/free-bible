# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 37

## Characters

**Joseph** (יוֹסֵף) is introduced here as a seventeen-year-old shepherd who becomes the central figure in the concluding narrative cycle of Genesis. The text explains his father's favoritism by noting that Joseph was "the son of his old age" (בֶן־זְקֻנִים). Joseph worked alongside the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah—the servant women's sons—which placed him in an intermediate position within the family hierarchy.

**Reuben** (רְאוּבֵן), as the firstborn, attempts to rescue Joseph and demonstrates leadership responsibility. His plan was to return later to retrieve Joseph and bring him back to his father (v. 22). **Judah** (יְהוּדָה) emerges as a pragmatic voice who suggests selling rather than killing, arguing "for he is our brother, our own flesh" (v. 27).

## Locations

The **Valley of Hebron** (עֵמֶק חֶבְרוֹן) was Jacob's residence, located in the Judean highlands. From here, Joseph was sent northward. **Shechem** (שְׁכֶם) was an important Canaanite city north of Hebron, where the brothers were first shepherding. **Dothan** (דֹּתָן) lay north of Shechem, along a major trade route between Gilead and Egypt. It was there that the brothers were when Joseph found them, and where they saw him approaching from a distance (vv. 17–18). The Ishmaelite caravan also passed through this area.

## Events and Customs

**Robe Symbolism**: The famous כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים (ketonet passim) has an uncertain meaning—possibly "a long robe reaching the feet" or "a variegated robe." Such special garments marked status and favored position. In the ancient Near East, clothing was costly and symbolically charged; giving someone a special robe signaled selection and honor.

**Dreams (חֲלֹמוֹת)** held great significance in the ancient Near East and were regarded as divine messages. Joseph's dreams employ agricultural imagery (sheaves of grain) and cosmic symbolism (sun, moon, stars), which were recognizable images for that time. The act of family members bowing down represented royal submission.

**Sale of Slaves**: The price of twenty pieces of silver corresponds to the market rate for young slaves during this period according to Mesopotamian sources. The Law of Hammurabi and other texts from the second millennium BCE mention similar prices.

## Key Terms

**דִּבָּתָם רָעָה** (dibbatam ra'ah) – "evil report" or "bad reputation" – indicates that Joseph brought negative reports about his brothers to their father, intensifying the conflict.

**בּוֹר** (bor) – "cistern" or "pit" – refers to common water cisterns carved into rock. Empty cisterns could serve as temporary holding places for prisoners; the text specifies that this one was empty and contained no water (רֵק אֵין בּוֹ מָיִם, v. 24).

**שְׁאוֹל** (she'ol) – "the realm of the dead" – where Jacob says he will go down in mourning, represents the Old Testament understanding of the abode of the dead.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The trade route between Gilead and Egypt followed by the Ishmaelites/Midianites is well documented archaeologically. They transported נְכֹאת (tragacanth gum), צְרִי (balm), and לֹט (ladanum)—all known trade goods of the Bronze Age. Egyptian texts confirm the importation of these aromatic substances from the Canaanite region. Wall paintings from Beni Hasan in Egypt (circa 1900 BCE) depict Semitic merchants arriving in Egypt, illustrating the type of caravan trade described in this chapter.
