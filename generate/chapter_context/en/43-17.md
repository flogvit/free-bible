# Historical and Cultural Context for John 17

## Events and Customs

John 17 constitutes what is traditionally called Jesus' "high priestly prayer" – a designation that dates back to the 16th century. The prayer takes place after the farewell discourse (chapters 14–16) and before Jesus crosses the Kidron Valley to the garden where he will be arrested (18:1). The physical gesture of "lifting his eyes to heaven" (ἐπάρας τοὺς ὀφθαλμοὺς αὐτοῦ εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν, v. 1) was a common Jewish prayer posture expressing address to God in heaven, as opposed to the more humble attitude of bowed head and downcast eyes that also occurred.

The structure of the prayer has parallels with the high priest's intercession on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), where the high priest first prayed for himself, then for the priesthood, and finally for all Israel. Jesus follows a similar pattern: first he prays for his own glorification (vv. 1–5), then for his disciples (vv. 6–19), and finally for all future believers (vv. 20–26).

## Key Terms

The expression **"the hour has come"** (ἐλήλυθεν ἡ ὥρα, v. 1) is a central Johannine concept that throughout the Gospel has pointed forward to the crucifixion and exaltation. Here it reaches its climax – the "hour" which previously had "not yet come" (John 2:4; 7:30; 8:20) is now here.

The term **"eternal life"** (ζωὴν αἰώνιον, vv. 2–3) is defined in verse 3 not primarily as infinite duration, but as relational knowledge: "to know you, the only true God." The Greek word γινώσκω (ginōskō) here denotes not merely intellectual knowledge, but intimate, experiential acquaintance – parallel to the Hebrew yada used for the deepest form of personal relationship.

**"Hallow them"** (ἁγίασον αὐτούς, vv. 17, 19) uses the Greek hagiazō, which in the Old Testament (LXX) primarily describes setting something or someone apart for God's service. Jesus does not pray for moral cleansing alone, but that the disciples be consecrated to a holy mission – sent into the world just as he himself was sent.

## Persons

**"The son of destruction"** (ὁ υἱὸς τῆς ἀπωλείας, v. 12) is a Semitic expression meaning "one destined for destruction" or "one who belongs to destruction." The term refers to Judas Iscariot and occurs elsewhere in the New Testament only in 2 Thessalonians 2:3. The phrase "so that the Scripture might be fulfilled" likely points to Psalm 41:10, which is explicitly quoted in John 13:18 regarding the betrayal.
