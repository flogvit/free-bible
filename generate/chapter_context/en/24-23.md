# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 23

## People

The chapter mentions "shepherds" (רֹעִים, *ro'im*) in verses 1–4, which is a metaphor for the kings and leaders of Judah. In the historical period addressed, this refers especially to the last Judean kings: Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah. The use of the shepherd metaphor for kings was widespread throughout the ancient Near East—Egyptian pharaohs, Mesopotamian kings, and Assyrian rulers were all described as "shepherds" over their people. The prophecy about "a righteous Branch for David" (צֶמַח צַדִּיק, *tsemach tsaddiq*) in verses 5–6 describes a future Davidic ruler. The name "The Lord Our Righteousness" (יְהוָה צִדְקֵנוּ, *YHWH tsidqenu*) in verse 6 may be a deliberate contrast to King Zedekiah’s name (צִדְקִיָּהוּ, *Tsidqiyyahu*), which means "The Lord is my righteousness".

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word סוֹד (*sod*) in verses 18 and 22, translated as "council" or "secret counsel", describes the heavenly assembly where God consults with his heavenly servants. The false prophets lacked access to this council and therefore spoke from their own hearts. This concept also appears in 1 Kings 22:19–23 and Job 1–2, reflecting an ancient Near Eastern idea of a divine council.

The word מַשָּׂא (*massa*) in verses 33–40 has a double meaning: "burden" and "oracle/utterance". The false prophets misused this term, and Jeremiah forbids them from using the expression "the burden of the Lord", because they themselves have become a burden that God will cast away. This wordplay is difficult to reproduce in translation, but it was a sharp polemic against prophetic abuse.

## Events and Customs

The chapter reveals a widespread practice among false prophets whereby they legitimized their messages through dreams (verses 25–28). In the ancient Near East, dreams were regarded as a valid channel for divine revelation, documented in both Mesopotamian and Egyptian sources. Jeremiah does not criticize dreams themselves, but rather prophets who fabricate dreams or use them to lead people away from the Lord. The contrast between straw and grain (verse 28) and the image of God’s word as fire and a hammer (verse 29) underscore the difference between human inventions and authentic divine revelation.
