# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 6

## Events and Customs

### Baptismal Practice in the Early Church
The chapter's references to baptism (βαπτίζω/βάπτισμα, verses 3–4) reflect early Christian baptismal practice. In the first century, baptism was normally performed by full immersion in water, making the imagery of "burial" and "resurrection" concrete and physically understandable for readers. Baptism often took place in rivers, natural springs, or public baths. The symbolic act of going down into the water and coming up again provided a powerful visual representation of what Paul describes as dying with Christ and being raised to new life. This practice differed from Jewish ritual washings (mikve) by being a one-time event marking incorporation into the Christian community.

## Key Concepts

### The Old Self and the Body of Sin
In verse 6, Paul introduces the concepts of "the old self" (ὁ παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος) and "the body of sin" (τὸ σῶμα τῆς ἁμαρτίας). "The old self" refers to the former existence under the dominion of sin—the human identity and way of life before union with Christ. "The body of sin" does not describe the physical body as evil in itself, but rather the body as an instrument and arena for sin. Paul's point is that through crucifixion with Christ, this old condition has been rendered powerless (καταργέω), so that believers are no longer to be slaves under sin.

### Slave Metaphor (δοῦλος)
The term "slave" (δοῦλος) and related words such as "to serve" (δουλεύω, verse 6) and "to enslave" (δουλόω, verses 18, 22) appear consistently throughout the chapter (verses 6, 16–22). In Roman society, slaves made up an estimated 30–40% of the population in Italy. Slavery was a well-known institution in which a slave was completely subject to the will and authority of their master. Paul uses this image rhetorically: people are always "slaves" under something—either sin or righteousness. For Roman readers, both free persons and slaves, this was an immediately recognizable picture of total loyalty and belonging.

### Military Language (ὅπλα)
In verse 13, Paul uses the word ὅπλα ("weapons" or "tools"), a military term. Rome was a military power, and Roman soldiers were a daily sight throughout the empire. The image of the members of the body as "weapons" either for unrighteousness or righteousness appealed to a culture that understood military discipline and loyalty. A soldier made their weapons available to their commanding officer—in the same way, believers are to make themselves available to God.

### Wages and Gift (ὀψώνια and χάρισμα)
In verse 23, ὀψώνια ("wages"—originally a soldier's ration or pay) is contrasted with χάρισμα ("gift of grace"). Ὀψώνια was a technical term for the wages a Roman soldier received for their service. By using this word, Paul emphasizes that death is something humans "earn" through their service to sin, while eternal life can never be earned—it is a pure gift from God.
