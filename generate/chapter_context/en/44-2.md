# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 2

## Events and Customs

### Pentecost (Shavuot)
The chapter opens with the disciples gathered on **Pentecost** (Greek: ἡμέρα τῆς πεντηκοστῆς, v. 1). This was the Jewish festival of Shavuot, which fell fifty days after Passover. Originally a harvest festival for wheat, by Jesus' time it had also come to celebrate the giving of the law at Sinai. This made the festival one of the three major pilgrimage feasts where Jewish men were required to travel to Jerusalem. Josephus confirms that the city could swell to several times its normal population during such festivals, explaining the international crowd described in verses 5–11.

### Speaking in Tongues and Prophetic Speech
The expression **ἑτέραις γλώσσαις** (other tongues/languages, v. 4) here describes recognizable languages that the listeners understood (v. 6, 8, 11). The verb **ἀποφθέγγεσθαι** (v. 4, 14) is used in the Septuagint for prophetic and inspired speech (cf. 1 Chron 25:1; Ezek 13:9) and suggests solemn, inspired utterance. Some listeners accused the disciples of being full of **γλεῦκος** (sweet wine/new must, v. 13), which was fresh grape juice that could ferment and become mildly intoxicating.

### Baptismal Practice
Peter's call to baptism (v. 38, 41) builds on an established Jewish tradition of ritual baths (mikveh) for purification. Proselyte baptism was already known as an initiation rite for Gentiles who converted to Judaism. Christian baptism "in the name of Jesus Christ" marked belonging to the new messianic movement.

### The Early Community
Verses 42–47 describe four foundational practices: **the apostles' teaching**, **fellowship** (κοινωνία), **the breaking of bread**, and **prayers**. Holding all things in common (v. 44–45) has parallels with the Essene practice at Qumran, but here it was voluntary rather than mandatory. The shared meals in homes (v. 46) likely combined ordinary meals with remembrance of the Lord's Supper.

## Places

### Jerusalem and the Temple
The events take place in **Jerusalem** during the Roman occupation of Judea. The disciples gathered in a **house** (οἶκος, v. 2), possibly the same upper room mentioned in 1:13. The early church continued to meet **in the temple** (ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ, v. 46), probably in Solomon's Colonnade or one of the outer courts. The temple remained the religious center for Jewish worship, and at this time Christians did not see themselves as separate from Judaism.

### List of Nations and Peoples
The list in verses 9–11 covers a geographical area from **Parthia** in the east to **Rome** in the west, from **Pontus** in the north to **Egypt** and **Libya** in the south. This list represents the spread of the Jewish diaspora and includes both ethnic Jews and **proselytes** (Gentiles who had converted to Judaism). The regions are mentioned roughly from east to west and reflect the old provinces of the Persian Empire combined with Roman territories.

## People

### Peter and the Eleven
Peter appears as the spokesperson for the apostolic group (v. 14). The expression "Peter with the eleven" (σὺν τοῖς ἕνδεκα) refers to the apostolic college after Matthias had replaced Judas (1:26). Peter's speech demonstrates a radical transformation from the man who denied Jesus just a few weeks earlier.

### David's Tomb
Peter refers to **the patriarch David** and notes that "his tomb is with us to this day" (v. 29). David's tomb was a well-known landmark in Jerusalem. According to Nehemiah 3:16, it lay south of the City of David, and Josephus reports that Herod the Great attempted to plunder it. Peter's point is that David himself died and remained in the grave, while the prophecy he quotes (Psalm 16:8–11, rendered in v. 25–28) pointed forward to the Messiah's resurrection.

## Key Terms

### "Pangs of Death" (ὠδῖνας τοῦ θανάτου)
The expression in verse 24, describing God's loosening of the "pangs of death" (ὠδῖνας τοῦ θανάτου), may play on a Hebrew wordplay between "pangs/birth pains" (חֶבְלֵי, hevlei) and "bonds/ropes" (חַבְלֵי, havlei). The Septuagint uses ὠδῖνες (birth pains) where the Hebrew text of Psalm 18:5–6 has "bonds". The image suggests that death could not hold Jesus captive.

### Christ and Lord
Peter's concluding proclamation (v. 36) declares that God has made Jesus both **Lord** (κύριος) and **Christ** (χριστός). "Christ" is the Greek translation of the Hebrew "Messiah" (the anointed one). "Lord" (κύριος) was the title the Septuagint used for God's name YHWH, and applying it to Jesus therefore had enormous theological implications for Jewish listeners.

### The Third Hour
Peter's defense against the accusation of drunkenness refers to the fact that it was "the third hour of the day" (v. 15), that is, about nine in the morning. This was the time of the morning sacrifice in the temple and of morning prayer, when devout Jews fasted. To be drunk at this time would have been especially inappropriate.
