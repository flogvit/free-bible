# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 93

## Key Concepts

Psalm 93 opens with the powerful phrase **יְהוָה מָלָךְ** (*YHWH malak*) – "The Lord is king" or "The Lord has become king." The Hebrew verb *malak* can be translated both as a description of state ("is king") and as an ingressive action ("has taken the throne").

The term **תֵּבֵל** (*tevel*) in verse 1, meaning "the world" or "the inhabited earth," emphasizes that God's kingdom encompasses all creation. The expression **מֵעוֹלָם** (*me'olam*) in verse 2 – "from of old" – marks that God's throne is eternal and stands in contrast to the temporary power of earthly kings.

## Events and Customs

The image of **נְהָרוֹת** (*neharot*, "the rivers/streams") lifting their voice and their waves (verses 3–4) reflects the ancient Near Eastern concept of chaotic waters as a threatening force. In Babylonian and Canaanite mythology, gods had to battle the sea monster to establish order. Psalm 93 employs this motif but with a crucial difference: YHWH does not need to fight – he is **אַדִּיר** (*addir*, "majestic/mighty") over all waters without struggle. This represents a deliberate theological contrast to the cosmic dramas of surrounding cultures.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (circa 1400–1200 BCE) contain hymns to Ba'al as "king" following his victory over the sea (Yamm). These texts use a similar poetic structure with repetition, as seen in Psalm 93:3 with the triple repetition of "the streams lift." The parallel shows that Israelite poetry shared literary conventions with Canaanite culture while formulating a distinct theology where YHWH is the one and only eternal king from the beginning of time.
