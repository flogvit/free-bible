## People

The chapter mentions the "prince" (הַנָּשִׂיא, *hannasi*) in Jerusalem in verses 10 and 12. This is a direct reference to King Zedekiah, Judah's last king who reigned from 597–586 B.C.E. The prophecy describes in detail how he will carry his belongings on his shoulder, break through the city wall in darkness, and cover his face so that he does not see the land. This corresponds remarkably with the account in 2 Kings 25:4–7, where Zedekiah fled through a gap in the wall at night, was captured in the plains of Jericho, brought to Nebuchadnezzar who blinded him, and then taken to Babylon in chains. The prophecy that he "shall not see" the land of Babylon even though he will die there (verse 13) points to this blinding.

## Events and Customs

Ezekiel's symbolic action with "exile gear" (כְּלֵי גוֹלָה, *klei golah*) was immediately recognizable to the observers. This was the minimum a refugee could carry: a mat or blanket, a water jug, a food bag, and perhaps a garment—all packed in a bundle carried on the shoulder. In the ancient Near East, this was a well-known sight when populations were deported. Assyrian reliefs from the 700s B.C.E. show exactly such scenes of deportees carrying their few belongings. That Ezekiel was to break through the house wall (verse 5) symbolized a desperate flight that could not use normal exits—an image of total destruction and panic.

## Key Terms

The expression "house of rebellion" (בֵּית הַמֶּרִי, *beit hammeri*) is used three times in this chapter (verses 2, 9, 25). The Hebrew word *meri* means not only disobedience but implies conscious and persistent defiance against a superior authority. This term emphasizes that Israel's disobedience was willful, not merely ignorance.

The wordplay in verse 10 between "prince" (הַנָּשִׂיא, *hannasi*) and "burden/oracle" (הַמַּשָּׂא, *hammassa*) is significant. Both words come from the root נשׂא meaning "to lift" or "to carry." The prince himself becomes the heavy burden to be carried away, and the prophetic word becomes a judgment against him.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The popular proverb "The days go by, and every vision comes to nothing" (verse 22) reflects widespread skepticism toward prophetic warnings that had circulated for years without apparent fulfillment. Babylonian sources from this period show that prophetic activity was common throughout the region, and people had developed a cynical attitude toward judgment prophecies that did not immediately come true. Ezekiel's response was that God's word would no longer be delayed—judgment was now imminent.
