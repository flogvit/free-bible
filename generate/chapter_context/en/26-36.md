## Places

The chapter opens with a direct prophecy to "the mountains of Israel" (הָרֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל), an expression referring to the central mountain region of Judah and Samaria. This was the core area of the promised land, in contrast to the coastal plain and valley regions. Historically, the mountainous terrain was the most stable Israelite territory and held deep theological significance as the place where the covenant was upheld. Verse 2 mentions "ancient heights" (בָמוֹת עוֹלָם), which may refer to the old cultic high places that enemies now claimed.

Edom is specifically mentioned in verse 5 as a nation that has taken Israel’s land "with joy and with utter contempt". This reflects the historical situation following Jerusalem’s fall in 586 B.C., when the Edomites exploited Judah’s weakness and occupied parts of the Negev and southern Judah. This expansion laid the foundation for what later became Idumea in Hellenistic-Roman times.

## Events and Customs

Verse 17 compares Israel’s uncleanness to "the uncleanness of a menstruous woman" (כְּטֻמְאַת הַנִּדָּה), that is, the ritual impurity associated with menstruation. This refers to the purity system in Leviticus 15:19–30, where a woman was ritually unclean for seven days during her menstrual period. The comparison emphasizes that the land’s uncleanness was as real and serious as ritual impurity and required corresponding cleansing. The expression was strong and provocative, conveying total separation from what is holy.

Verse 25 describes sprinkling with "clean water" (מַיִם טְהוֹרִים). While this may allude to the purification rituals involving ashes from the red heifer (Numbers 19), Ezekiel here uses the phrase "clean water" without mentioning the ashes, possibly suggesting a new and direct divine cleansing.

## Key Terms

The expressions "a new heart" (לֵב חָדָשׁ) and "a new spirit" (רוּחַ חֲדָשָׁה) in verse 26 represent the core message of the chapter. In Hebrew thought, the "heart" (לֵב) was the center of will, thought, and decision-making, not primarily emotions. A "heart of stone" (לֵב הָאֶבֶן) symbolizes hardness, rigidity, and unresponsiveness to God’s word, while a "fleshly heart" (לֵב בָּשָׂר) denotes receptivity and obedience. This transformation is not merely moral improvement but a fundamental renewal of the human inner being.

The phrase "I will sanctify my great name" (וְקִדַּשְׁתִּי אֶת־שְׁמִי הַגָּדוֹל) in verse 23 is central. God’s "name" (שֵׁם) represented his character, reputation, and revealed nature. When Israel was scattered among the nations, God’s name was "profaned" (חִלֵּל) because the Gentiles concluded that Israel’s God was powerless. Therefore, restoration is primarily about restoring God’s honor among the nations.

Verse 38 uses the expression "holy flock" (צֹאן קָדָשִׁים), referring to the sacrificial animals that filled Jerusalem during the three pilgrimage festivals (Passover, Weeks, and Tabernacles). The image conveys that the rebuilt cities will be as full of people as Jerusalem was with sacrificial animals during the feasts.
