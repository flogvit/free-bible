# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 21

## People

**Isaac** (יִצְחָק, *Yitschak*) is born in this chapter, and his name means "he laughs" – a direct link to Sarah's laughter in Genesis 18:12 and her exclamation in verse 6: "God has made laughter for me." Isaac represents the fulfillment of the covenant promise and becomes the patriarch of the people of Israel.

**Hagar and Ishmael** stand at the center of the chapter's dramatic conflict. Ishmael (יִשְׁמָעֵאל, "God hears") is now in his teens, around 14 years old based on the chronology (Abraham was 86 when Ishmael was born in Genesis 16:16, and 100 when Isaac was born in Genesis 21:5). This chapter marks the beginning of Ishmael's separate lineage, with the promise that he will become a great nation (verses 13, 18).

**Abimelech** and his army commander **Pikol** (פִיכֹל) appear as representatives of the kingdom of Gerar. The text calls them Philistines (verses 32, 34), although this likely reflects a later name for the region's population. The name Abimelech ("my father is king") was probably a dynastic title rather than a personal name, similar to "Pharaoh" in Egypt.

## Places

**Beersheba** (בְּאֵר שָׁבַע) is located in the Negev region, about 70-80 kilometers south-southwest of Jerusalem. The name is explained in the text with a double meaning: both "well of the oath" (from נִשְׁבְּעוּ, *nishbe'u*, "they swore," verse 31) and "well of seven" (from שֶׁבַע, *sheva*, "seven," verses 28-30) after the seven lambs. Archaeological excavations at Tel Beersheba have revealed settlement from the Bronze Age, and the site later became an important border town marking the southern boundary of Israel ("from Dan to Beersheba").

**Wilderness of Paran** (מִדְבַּר פָארָן), where Ishmael settled (verse 21), is the dry highland on the Sinai Peninsula between Canaan and Egypt. This area was inhabited by seminomadic tribes and was known as a caravan region along trade routes.

## Events and Customs

**The Weaning Feast** (verse 8) was a significant event in the ancient Near East. Children were typically breastfed for 2-3 years, and weaning marked a critical transition – the child had survived the most dangerous period of infancy. Large celebrations on such occasions are documented in Mesopotamian texts.

**Circumcision on the eighth day** (verse 4) follows the command from Genesis 17. This practice distinguished the Israelites from many neighboring peoples (although Egyptians also practiced circumcision, but at a later age).

**The Expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael** reflects complex inheritance laws in antiquity. Mesopotamian laws (such as the Laws of Hammurabi and the Laws of Lipit-Ishtar) dealt specifically with the status of children born to slave women. According to certain legal traditions, such children could be recognized as heirs, but Sarah's demand for expulsion – though it seems brutal – was intended to secure Isaac's exclusive right to the inheritance. God's approval of Sarah's request shows that the covenant line was to go exclusively through Isaac.

**The Covenant with Abimelech** follows a classic ancient pattern: exchange of gifts (small and large livestock), oath-taking, and establishment of witnesses (the seven lambs). Water rights were vital in the dry climate and often a source of conflict between nomads and settled peoples.

## Key Terms

**פָּקַד** (*pakad*, "to visit/attend to") in verse 1 is a loaded theological word describing God's intervention to fulfill His promises. It involves both "remembering" and "acting on the basis of what one remembers."

**מְצַחֵק** (*metsacheq*, "playing/mocking") in verse 9 is related to the name Isaac and creates a wordplay. The exact meaning of the word is debated – it could mean innocent play, mockery, or something more threatening. Regardless of interpretation, Sarah sees it as a threat to Isaac's position.

**אֵל עוֹלָם** (*El Olam*, "the Everlasting God") in verse 33 is a divine name that emphasizes God's timeless nature. Abraham uses this name in his worship at Beersheba, and it reflects a growing understanding of God's character through the patriarchal narratives.

**אֶשֶׁל** (*eshel*, "tamarisk") which Abraham planted was a tree adapted to the desert climate. Tree planting marked claims on land areas and the establishment of a place of worship – the tamarisk provided shade for worship and the practice of hospitality in the harsh landscape.
