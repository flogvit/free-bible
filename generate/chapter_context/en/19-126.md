## Events and Customs

Psalm 126 is closely linked to the return from the Babylonian exile (from 538 B.C.E.), when the Persian king Cyrus allowed the Jews to return to Jerusalem. The expression "when the Lord brought back those who were captive in Zion" (v. 1) likely refers to this event, which marked the end of nearly 70 years in captivity. The psalm reflects both the immediate joy of the return and the subsequent reality of rebuilding under difficult conditions. The prayer in verse 4 suggests that the psalm was used during a period when not everything had been completed—perhaps during the arduous work of restoring the temple and the city.

## Places

**Zion** is the central place in the psalm and refers to Jerusalem and the temple hill. For those who returned, Zion represented not only a geographical location but the very center of God's presence and the fulfillment of His covenant. **Negev** (v. 4) is the desert area south of Judea, known for its dry riverbeds (wadis) that remain empty most of the year but are dramatically filled with water during the winter rains. This image of sudden abundance in the midst of drought served as a powerful metaphor for hope in comprehensive transformation.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **שִׁיבַת צִיּוֹן** (*shivat tsiyon*, "Zion's return/fate") can be translated both as "captivity" and "fate/fortune", creating a wordplay in the text. **כְּחֹלְמִים** (*kecholmim*, "like dreamers") expresses an almost unreal joy—so overwhelming that it felt like a dream. The agricultural images in verses 5–6, with **זֹרְעִים בְּדִמְעָה** (*zor'im bedim'ah*, "sowing in tears") and **בְּרִנָּה יִקְצֹרוּ** (*berinah yiqtsoru*, "reaping with shouts of joy*), were deeply rooted in the annual cycle of Israel's agricultural society and served as metaphors for suffering and future restoration.
