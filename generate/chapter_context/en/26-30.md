# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 30

## Places

Chapter 30 contains an unusually detailed geographical catalog of Egyptian cities and regions that will be struck by judgment. **Noph** (Hebrew: נֹף) is Memphis, the ancient Egyptian capital south of the Nile Delta, a center of idolatry and political power. **Pathros** (פַּתְרוֹס) refers to Upper Egypt, the southern region. **Zoan** (צֹעַן) is Tanis in the eastern delta area, an important city during this period. **No** (נֹא) is Thebes (also called No-Amon), the powerful religious capital of the south with its famous temples to Amun.

**Sin** (סִין) is usually identified with Pelusium, a strategically vital fortress city at Egypt's eastern border, known as "the fortress of Egypt." **Aven** (אָוֶן, "wickedness") is a wordplay on Heliopolis (On), the main seat of the sun god. **Pi-beseth** (פִי־בֶסֶת) is Bubastis, the center of the cat goddess Bastet cult. **Tahpanhes** (תְּחַפְנְחֵס) was a border fortress where Jewish refugees later settled as well (Jeremiah 43).

## People

The chapter explicitly mentions **Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon** (verse 10), as God's instrument to carry out judgment against Egypt. The historical Nebuchadnezzar II (reign c. 605–562 BC) launched a campaign against Egypt in 568/567 BC. **Pharaoh** is mentioned repeatedly without a name, but the context points to Hophra (Apries), who reigned from 589–570 BC and suffered a catastrophic defeat at the hands of Babylon.

## Events and Customs

The dating in verses 20–21 ("in the eleventh year, in the first month, on the seventh day") places this prophecy in April 587 BC, in the midst of Jerusalem's siege. The reference to "Pharaoh's arm being broken" likely relates to Egypt's failed attempt to help Jerusalem against Babylon (Jeremiah 37:5–7), when the Egyptian army had to retreat without achieving anything.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the day of the Lord"** (יוֹם לַיהוָה) in verse 3 is an important prophetic term denoting a time of divine intervention and judgment. It is described here as "a day of clouds" (יוֹם עָנָן) and "a time for the nations"—a moment when God acts in history through the destinies of nations.

**Cush, Put, Lud, and all the mixed multitude** (verse 5) refer to Egypt's allies and mercenaries: Cush is Nubia/Ethiopia, Put is likely Libya, and Lud may be Lydia in Asia Minor. The expression "mixed multitude" (הָעֶרֶב) describes the many foreign peoples who served in the Egyptian army.

**Gillulim** (גִלּוּלִים) in verse 13, often translated as "idols," is a derogatory Hebrew word that literally means something like "dung heaps"—Ezekiel's characteristic term of contempt for idols.
