# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 5

## Places

**Qeren ben-shemen** (קֶרֶן בֶּן־שָׁמֶן, "a fertile hill", v. 1) literally means "a horn, a son of oil/fat". This refers to the characteristic limestone ridges in the Judean highlands, where terraces were carved out for vine cultivation. The areas around Hebron and Bethlehem were especially known for their vineyards. The rich, reddish-brown "terra rossa" soil in these regions was ideal for wine production.

## Events and Customs

**Vineyard management in ancient Israel** (vv. 1-7) is described with technical precision that reflects actual agricultural practice. To "clear it of stones" (וַיְסַקְּלֵהוּ) meant removing limestone and building terraces. The "watchtower" (מִגְדָּל) was a stone structure where guards lived during the harvest to protect against thieves and animals. The "winepress" (יֶקֶב) was carved directly into the bedrock – an upper treading platform and a lower collection vat. Archaeologists have found hundreds of such winepresses throughout Israel.

**The six woes** (הוֹי, vv. 8, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22) follow a known prophetic form also used in funeral laments. This double association – accusation and mourning – gave the words particular weight. Each "woe" introduces a specific social sin that was widespread in Judean society in the 700s BC.

**Consolidation of estates** (vv. 8-10) reflects a historical reality where wealthy landowners bought up small farmers burdened by debt. This broke with the Israelite ideal that every family should have its own inherited portion (נַחֲלָה). Similar criticism is found in Micah (2:1-2) from the same period.

## Key Terms

**Soreq** (שֹׂרֵק, v. 2) was the noblest variety of grapevine in antiquity, known for its small, dark, seedless grapes. The name survived in the Sorek Valley west of Jerusalem. That this noble vine produced **be'ushim** (בְּאֻשִׁים, "stinking/wild grapes") strengthens the image of total disappointment.

**Mishpat/mispach and tsedaqah/tse'aqah** (v. 7) constitute one of the most striking wordplays in Hebrew poetry. God expected "mishpat" (מִשְׁפָּט, "justice") but found "mispach" (מִשְׂפָּח, "bloodshed"); He sought "tsedaqah" (צְדָקָה, "righteousness") but heard "tse'aqah" (צְעָקָה, "a cry"). The nearly identical sounds underscore the tragic contrast between expectation and reality.

**Shekar** (שֵׁכָר, vv. 11, 22) was an intoxicating drink made from dates, pomegranates, or barley – stronger than wine and often mentioned together with it in warnings against drunkenness.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations have uncovered numerous winepresses from the Iron Age II (ca. 1000-586 BC) in the Judean highlands, exactly as described in v. 2. At Gibeon, an extensive wine cellar complex has been found with 63 carved storage pits that could hold 95,000 liters of wine. Discoveries of jar handles bearing royal seals ("lmlk" seals) from the 700s confirm that wine production was a vital part of Judah's economy, as this chapter assumes.
