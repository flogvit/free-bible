# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 15

## People

**Absalom** appears here as an ambitious prince systematically building political support. His use of a chariot, horses, and fifty runners ahead of him (v. 1) was a royal status symbol in the ancient Near East, something Adonijah later imitated (1 Kings 1:5). This signaled royal ambitions and was known from Egyptian and Mesopotamian court culture.

**Ahithophel from Giloh** was David's counselor and was regarded as exceptionally wise—his advice was compared to "inquiring of God" (2 Sam 16:23). Giloh was located in the hill country of Judah, south of Jerusalem. His betrayal of David was especially serious because of his position as a trusted advisor. Some scholars suggest he may have been Bathsheba's grandfather (based on the fact that Bathsheba's father Eliam in 2 Sam 11:3 might be the same person as Eliam, son of Ahithophel, in 2 Sam 23:34), which could then explain a motive for revenge.

**Ittai from Gath** was a warrior from the Philistine city of Gath who had only recently joined David—verse 20 notes that he came "yesterday." His strong declaration of loyalty (v. 21: "wherever my lord the king goes, whether to death or to life, there your servant will be") shows that David's army included foreign warriors. The six hundred Gittites who followed him (v. 18) constituted a significant military force.

**Hushai the Archite** came from Archi, an area near Bethel. His title "the king's friend" (רֵעֶה דָוִד, v. 37) was an official court position known from Egyptian and other Eastern courts, designating a trusted advisor.

**Zadok and Abiathar** were the priests who carried the ark of the covenant (v. 24). David sent them back to Jerusalem to serve as informants along with their sons Ahimaaz and Jonathan (vv. 27–36).

## Places

**Hebron** was David's first capital, where he reigned over Judah for seven years and six months before capturing Jerusalem. The city had deep symbolic significance as the burial place of the patriarchs and the place where Absalom was born (2 Sam 3:3). By starting his rebellion there, Absalom appealed to older tribal loyalties and possible dissatisfaction with Jerusalem's centralizing power.

**The Kidron Valley** (v. 23) lies between Jerusalem and the Mount of Olives to the east. Crossing this valley marked a definitive departure from the city and was the route toward the Jordan Valley and the wilderness.

**The Mount of Olives** (v. 30, מַעֲלֵה הַזֵּיתִים) east of Jerusalem was a place of worship (v. 32 mentions "the place where one worships God"). David went up the slope barefoot and with his head covered—these were mourning rites expressing both personal grief and public humiliation.

## Events and Customs

**Stealing hearts** (v. 6, וַיְגַנֵּב אַבְשָׁלוֹם אֶת־לֵב אַנְשֵׁי יִשְׂרָאֵל) describes Absalom's manipulative political campaign. He positioned himself at the city gate where legal cases were decided (v. 2), undermining trust in David's judicial system by suggesting that the king did not give people fair treatment (vv. 3–4).

**Vows and offerings** (vv. 7–8) were common religious obligations. Absalom used a claimed vow made during his exile in Geshur as an excuse to travel to Hebron. That David allowed this shows how religious commitments could be exploited politically.

**Mourning rituals** that David performed (v. 30)—going barefoot (יָחֵף), covering the head (רֹאשׁ לוֹ חָפוּי), and weeping (בוֹכֶה)—were established expressions of grief and humility in Israelite culture. That the people did likewise showed solidarity with the king.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the Cherethites and the Pelethites"** (v. 18, הַכְּרֵתִי וְהַפְּלֵתִי) refers to David's personal bodyguard. The names may be connected to Cretan and Philistine peoples, although their exact meaning is debated. These were professional soldiers loyal to David personally, not to Israel as a nation, and were therefore reliable during the civil war.
