# Historical and Cultural Context for Ephesians 6

## People

**Tychicus** (vv. 21-22) is mentioned as the bearer of this letter. He was from the province of Asia (Acts 20:4) and served as Paul's trusted coworker and envoy. He is described as "the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord" (τὸν ἀγαπητὸν ἀδελφὸν καὶ πιστὸν διάκονον) and was tasked with informing the church about Paul's situation. Tychicus is also mentioned in Colossians 4:7-8 with almost identical wording, suggesting that he delivered both letters on the same journey. He is further mentioned in 2 Timothy 4:12 and Titus 3:12.

## Events and Customs

**Slavery in the Roman Empire** (vv. 5-9): Slavery was a fundamental economic and social institution in antiquity. In the Roman Empire, slaves made up an estimated 10-15% of the population, and in urban areas like Ephesus, the proportion could be higher. Slaves ranged from highly educated household managers to mine workers. The Greek word *douloi* (δοῦλοι, v. 5) describes people who were the property of others. Paul does not directly challenge the institution but radically transforms relationships by imposing responsibility on masters (κύριοι, v. 9) toward the same heavenly Lord whom the slaves serve—a concept foreign to Roman household thinking.

**Child Rearing** (vv. 1-4): The Greek word *paideia* (παιδείᾳ, v. 4) encompassed the entire process of cultural formation—education, discipline, and character development. In Greco-Roman culture, the *pater familias* (head of the household) held absolute authority over children, including the right to physical punishment. Paul's exhortation not to "provoke your children to anger" (μὴ παροργίζετε τὰ τέκνα ὑμῶν) represents a moderation of this authority.

**The Fifth Commandment** (vv. 2-3): When Paul writes, "Honor your father and mother" (τίμα τὸν πατέρα σου καὶ τὴν μητέρα), he quotes from Exodus 20:12 and Deuteronomy 5:16. He characterizes this as "the first commandment with a promise" (ἐντολὴ πρώτη ἐν ἐπαγγελίᾳ) and includes the promise of blessing and long life.

## Key Terms

**Panoplia** (πανοπλίαν, vv. 11, 13) – "full armor" – was a technical military term for the complete equipment of a heavily armed soldier. For readers in Ephesus, where Roman soldiers were a daily sight, this image was immediately recognizable. The armor includes: *zōnē* (ὀσφύν, belt, v. 14), *thōrax* (θώρακα, breastplate, v. 14), readiness for the feet (*hetoimasia*, ἑτοιμασίᾳ, v. 15), *thyreos* (θυρεόν, the large shield, v. 16), *perikephalaia* (περικεφαλαίαν, helmet, v. 17) and *machaira* (μάχαιραν, sword, v. 17). Paul also uses *palē* (πάλη, v. 12), which means wrestling—the form of hand-to-hand combat that was most intimate and exhausting.

**Kosmokratores** (κοσμοκράτορας, v. 12) – "rulers of this world" – was a term known from astrological and magical literature, where it denoted the planetary gods believed to control human destiny. In a city like Ephesus, known for its widespread practice of magic and occult arts (cf. Acts 19:19), this term had special resonance.

**Presbeuō en halysei** (πρεσβεύω ἐν ἁλύσει, v. 20) – "ambassador in chains" – is a deliberate paradox. The verb *presbeuō* (to be an ambassador) normally described a high-ranking representative with diplomatic immunity. Paul's situation—being an ambassador while chained to a Roman guard—underscores the paradoxical power of the gospel.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of first-century Roman military equipment, including preserved examples of the *scutum* (large shield), *gladius* (sword) and bronze helmets, confirm the accuracy of Paul's description. Particularly interesting are findings of flaming arrows (*belē pepyrōmena*, τὰ βέλη... πεπυρωμένα, v. 16)—arrows dipped in pitch and set alight—which were known from both Roman and Parthian warfare. Tacitus and other Roman historians describe the use of such arrows in sieges. Shields were often covered with leather that could be wetted to extinguish such arrows—giving Paul's image of the shield of faith added meaning.
