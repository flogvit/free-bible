## People

Romans 5 presents Adam as a central figure in Paul’s theological argument (vv. 12–21). Adam is portrayed as "τύπος τοῦ μέλλοντος" (a type of the one to come) – that is, as a typological figure pointing forward to Christ. In Jewish tradition during Paul’s time, there was considerable interest in Adam’s role in humanity’s destiny. Texts such as 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch (both from the late first or early second century CE) discuss how sin and death entered the world through Adam. Paul’s comparison of Adam and Christ as two representative heads for humanity was thus a line of thought his Jewish readers could recognize, even though the conclusion was radically new.

Moses is mentioned as a chronological reference point (v. 14) for the period between Adam and the giving of the law. The expression "from Adam to Moses" marks the time before the Mosaic Law was given, a period when sin was in the world but not counted in the same way as after the law.

## Key Terms

Several Greek terms in this chapter carry significant cultural and theological weight:

**Προσαγωγή** (prosagōgē, v. 2) – "access" or "approach" – was a technical term from court culture describing being brought before a king or high-ranking person. In imperial Rome, audiences with the emperor required an intermediary who had the right to introduce the individual. Paul uses this image to show how Christ gives believers direct access to God.

**Καταλλαγή** (katallagē, vv. 10–11) – "reconciliation" – was primarily a diplomatic and political term describing the restoration of peaceful relations between hostile parties or nations. Paul applies this to the relationship between God and humanity, which was theologically innovative.

**Δικαίωσις/δικαιωθέντες** (dikaiōsis/dikaiōthentes, vv. 1, 9, 18) – "justification/justified" – drawn from the legal system, where it denoted a verdict of acquittal. In Roman law, such a verdict was final and granted the acquitted person full civic status. This term frames the entire chapter, from its opening (v. 1) to its conclusion (v. 18).

## Events and Customs

Paul’s reference to "dying for a righteous person" or "for a good man" (v. 7) drew on well-known examples from Greco-Roman culture. The idea of a noble death for others – especially for friends, family, or one’s country – was highly valued in antiquity. Philosophers such as Seneca discussed such acts of sacrifice, and Roman heroic tales included examples of those who gave their lives for others. Paul’s point is that even this highest form of human love pales in comparison to Christ’s death for enemies and sinners – a thought that would have been culturally startling.

The term **θλῖψις** (thlipsis, "tribulation", v. 3) had particular resonance for early Christians in Rome. Even before the great persecutions, Christians experienced social marginalization, economic pressure, and occasional local opposition. Paul’s teaching that tribulations produce endurance, character, and hope gave theological meaning to experiences the congregation already knew.
