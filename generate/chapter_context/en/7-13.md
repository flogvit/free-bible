# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 13

## People

**Manoah** (מָנוֹחַ) means "rest" or "calm" in Hebrew. He is presented as a man from the tribe of Dan, living in Zorah. Manoah appears as a devout Israelite who prays to the Lord and seeks guidance on how the child should be raised. His reaction to the theophany—the fear that he would die after seeing God (v. 22)—reflects a widespread Old Testament belief that humans could not survive an encounter with the divine (cf. Exod 33:20).

**Manoah's wife** remains unnamed throughout the entire story, even though she is the central recipient of the angel's message. This is remarkable because she demonstrates greater spiritual insight than her husband, both in recognizing the divine nature of the messenger and in her theological reasoning that God would not have accepted their offering if He intended to kill them (v. 23). Her barrenness and miraculous conception place her among the biblical matriarchs such as Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel.

## Places

**Zorah** (צָרְעָה) was a city in the Shephelah region, the low hill range between the coastal plain and the Judean mountains. The city was strategically located near the border of Philistine territory, about 25 kilometers west of Jerusalem. Archaeological investigations identify the site with Tell Ṣar'a (modern Sar'a). Its location in the border area between Israelite and Philistine control explains why this region became the arena for the conflict that Samson would later initiate.

**Mahaneh-Dan** ("Dan's camp") is mentioned in verse 25 as the place between Zorah and Eshtaol where the Spirit of the Lord began to stir Samson. **Eshtaol** (אֶשְׁתָּאֹל) was a nearby city, also located in the border area between Judah and Dan.

## Events and Customs

**The Nazirite Law** is central to this chapter. According to Num 6:1-21, the Nazirite vow involved three main obligations: abstaining from wine and anything derived from the grapevine, a prohibition against cutting one's hair, and avoiding contact with dead bodies. What makes Samson's Naziriteship unique is that it was imposed from birth and for life—most Nazirite vows were voluntary and temporary. The mother is also given restrictions during her pregnancy, showing that the consecration begins before birth.

**The custom of hospitality** is expressed when Manoah wishes to keep the angel and prepare a young goat for him (v. 15). Offering a meal to a guest was a fundamental duty in Old Testament culture. The angel's refusal of the meal and redirection toward a burnt offering to the Lord (v. 16) gradually reveals his true nature.

## Key Terms

**"The Angel of the Lord"** (מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה) in this chapter appears as more than an ordinary messenger. When he says that his name is **"פֶלִאי"** (wonderful/secret, v. 18), the same Hebrew root used to describe God's wonderful works is employed. The angel's ascent in the flame of the offering and Manoah's exclamation "we have seen God" (v. 22) suggest that this was a theophany—a manifestation of God Himself.

**"To begin to deliver"** (יָחֵל לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, v. 5) is an unusual expression indicating that Samson's task would only be the beginning of liberation from the Philistines. The phrase suggests that his service would initiate a longer process of deliverance from Philistine domination mentioned in verse 1.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The forty years of Philistine domination (v. 1) form the historical framework for Samson's birth and calling. The chapter presents a clear structure: Israel's sin and God's judgment (v. 1), the promise of a deliverer (vv. 2-5), and confirmation of the promise through repeated theophany (vv. 6-23). The conclusion (vv. 24-25) serves as a transition to Samson's active service, where the Spirit of the Lord begins to "stir him" (לְפַעֲמוֹ)—an expression that literally means "to set in motion" or "to push."
