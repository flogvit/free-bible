# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 14

## People

**Samson (שִׁמְשׁוֹן, Shimshon)** appears here as a young man ready for marriage. As a Nazirite from birth (chap. 13), his contact with the dead lion carcass in this chapter is problematic according to the Nazirite laws in Numbers 6.

**The Philistine woman from Timnath** remains unnamed throughout the narrative, which is typical of biblical stories where women are often defined by their relationships to men. She appears caught between two cultures—married to an Israelite yet under pressure from her own people.

## Places

**Timnath (תִּמְנָתָה)** is usually identified with Tel Batash in the Sorek Valley, about 6 km northwest of Beth-Shemesh. Archaeological excavations have uncovered Philistine pottery and Iron Age architecture, confirming the site's Philistine connection during this period. The city lay in the border region between Israelite and Philistine territory, making it a natural meeting point between the cultures.

**Ashkelon (אַשְׁקְלוֹן)** was one of the five major Philistine city-states, located on the Mediterranean coast about 37 km southwest of Timnath. Excavations have revealed a significant port city with impressive city walls and public buildings from the Iron Age. The distance Samson had to travel indicates the extent of his anger.

**The vineyards near Timnath (כַּרְמֵי תִמְנָתָה)** reflect the fertility of the region. The Sorek Valley was known for wine production, and the name "Sorek" may mean "choice vine."

## Events and Customs

**Wedding negotiations** in the ancient Near East were family matters. That Samson asks his parents to "get her for me" (קְחוּ־אוֹתָהּ לִּי) follows normal practice where the father negotiated the marriage agreement. The parents' objection to marrying "the uncircumcised" (הָעֲרֵלִים) reflects how strongly circumcision served as a cultural marker separating Israelites from Philistines.

**The wedding feast (מִשְׁתֶּה)** lasted seven days, which was standard in ancient Israel (cf. Gen 29:27). That the feast was held in the bride's hometown suggests a form of matrilocal marriage arrangement where the bride remained with her family—a practice more common among Philistines than Israelites.

**The thirty groomsmen (מֵרֵעִים)** chosen by the Philistines likely served both as honor guards and overseers. The number and the fact that they were "given" to Samson (וַיִּקְחוּ) suggest the Philistines were suspicious of this Israelite.

## Key Terms

**The riddle (חִידָה, ḥidah)** was an established literary and social genre in the ancient Near East. Riddles were used at festive occasions and often functioned as tests of wisdom. Samson's riddle is however "unfair" because it relies on personal experience no one else knew—a breach of the riddle's normal conventions where the answer should be logically deducible.

**"The Spirit of the Lord came upon him" (וַתִּצְלַח עָלָיו רוּחַ יְהוָה)** – the verb צָלַח (*tsalach*) means to "break forth" or "come with power." This expression appears twice in the chapter (vv. 6 and 19) and marks moments of supernatural strength.

**"Sedinim" (סְדִינִים)** and **"chalifot begadim" (חֲלִיפוֹת בְּגָדִים)** refer to two types of clothing: linen garments (undergarments or linen bedding) and change-of-clothes (finer outer garments). These were valuable trade goods, and the Philistines were known for their textile production.

**"You have plowed with my heifer" (חֲרַשְׁתֶּם בְּעֶגְלָתִי)** is figurative language suggesting they used Samson's wife as a tool to obtain information—they employed his "heifer" for their own work. The heifer was a common image for a young woman in Semitic poetry.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Text-critical note:** Verse 15 in the Hebrew text (MT) says "the seventh day," but this creates tension with verse 14, which states they could not solve the riddle within three days. The Septuagint (LXX) reads "the fourth day" in verse 15, which many scholars consider the original reading.

Archaeological excavations at Tel Batash (Timnath) have uncovered a Philistine cultural layer from the 12th century BCE with characteristic Philistine pottery, including so-called "Philistine Bichrome Ware." The discovery of wine vessel remains and pressing installations confirms the vineyards mentioned in the text. The excavations show signs of cultural blending between Philistine and Canaanite/Israelite material culture, reflecting the borderland character described in this chapter.

Lions were still present in ancient Israel during this period. Assyrian reliefs and texts confirm lion hunting in the Levant, and lion bones have been found in archaeological contexts from the Bronze Age and early Iron Age in the region.
