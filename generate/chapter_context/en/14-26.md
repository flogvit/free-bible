# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 26

## People

**Uzziah (Azariah)** is the main character in this chapter and reigned over Judah from approximately 792–740 B.C. He is known by two names: "Uzziah" (עֻזִּיָּהוּ, "YHWH is my strength") is used in Chronicles, while "Azariah" is used in Kings. His long reign of 52 years was one of the most prosperous periods in Judah’s history, coinciding with Jeroboam II’s equally successful reign in Israel. Assyrian sources from Tiglath-Pileser III mention "Azriau," which some scholars identify with Uzziah, although this identification remains debated.

**Zechariah** (v. 5) is described as one who had "insight into God’s visions" and served as the king’s spiritual guide. He is not identical to the post-exilic prophet Zechariah but represents a type of advisor common at the court—one who could interpret divine revelations.

**Azariah the high priest** (vv. 17–20) led the resistance against the king’s intrusion into priestly duties. The fact that he brought with him eighty courageous priests shows an organized priestly institution willing to confront even royal power to protect the integrity of the sanctuary.

## Places

**Elath** (v. 2) was a strategic port city on the Gulf of Aqaba (modern Eilat/Aqaba). The city provided access to trade routes toward Arabia, East Africa, and India. Uzziah’s rebuilding of this city indicates Judah’s southward expansion and control over lucrative trade routes.

**Gath, Jabne, and Ashdod** (v. 6) were Philistine cities along the coastal plain. Gath lay farthest inland, Jabne (later known as Jamnia) was in the middle of the coast, and Ashdod was one of the most important Philistine cities. The fact that Uzziah tore down their walls and built towns in their territories demonstrates Judahite military dominance over the Philistines.

**Gur-Baal** (v. 7) is otherwise an unknown place, probably located in the Negev desert, inhabited by Arab tribes. The Meunites were a people group from the area around Ma’an in modern Jordan.

## Events and Customs

**The king’s intrusion into priestly service** (vv. 16–19) represents a serious boundary violation in Israelite religion. Incense offerings at the golden altar in the Holy Place were exclusively reserved for Aaronic priests. This division of labor between king and priest was fundamental to Judah’s theocracy. Earlier, King Saul had been rebuked by Samuel for a similar boundary crossing (1 Sam 13), and Jeroboam I was condemned for appointing non-Levitical priests.

**Leprosy as divine judgment** (vv. 19–21) struck Uzziah immediately while he stood at the incense altar. In ancient Israel, leprosy (Hebrew צָרַעַת, *tsara'at*) was regarded as a divine punishment resulting in ritual impurity. A person with leprosy had to live in isolation and could not participate in temple worship (Lev 13–14).

## Key Terms

**"The house of freedom"** (בֵּית הַחָפְשִׁית, *bet hachofshit*) in verse 21 is a rare expression that literally means "house of freedom" or "the secluded house." It likely refers to an isolated residence where lepers of royal rank could live apart from society yet with some dignity.

**"Treachery"** (מַעַל, *ma'al*) in verses 16 and 18 is a technical term for treachery or betrayal against God, especially by violating what is holy. The word is often used for actions that breach the boundary between the sacred and the profane.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations have uncovered a tombstone inscription from the first century B.C. or A.D. with the text: "Here were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah. Do not open!" This secondary burial confirms the tradition that Uzziah was buried separately from the other kings because of his leprosy (v. 23).

In Ashdod, archaeologists have found traces of destruction and rebuilding from the eighth century B.C., which may correlate with Uzziah’s military campaigns described in verse 6. Furthermore, excavations in the Negev desert have revealed fortresses and water systems from this period, matching the description of Uzziah’s towers and wells in the wilderness (v. 10).
