# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 6

## People

**Uzzah and Ahio** were sons of Abinadab, in whose house the ark of the covenant had been kept for many years (possibly about 20 years or more) since it was returned by the Philistines (1 Sam 7:1-2). Their familiar acquaintance with the ark may have led to a casual attitude toward its holiness. Uzzah’s name means "strength," making his death while attempting to steady the ark a tragic irony.

**Obed-Edom** is called a "Gittite," which could mean either that he was from the Philistine city of Gath, or that he belonged to a Levitical clan associated with Gath-Rimmon (Josh 21:24). 1 Chronicles 15:18-21 identifies him as a Levite and gatekeeper, which would explain why the ark could be safely kept in his house. His three months of blessing demonstrated that the ark was not dangerous in itself but required proper reverence.

**Michal**, Saul’s daughter and David’s first wife, had a complicated history with David. She had loved him (1 Sam 18:20), saved his life (1 Sam 19:12-17), but was later given to another man by Saul (1 Sam 25:44). David demanded her return as part of political negotiations (2 Sam 3:13-16). Her contempt for David in this chapter may reflect loyalty to her father’s house.

## Places

**Baale-judah** (v. 2), also called Kiriath-jearim, was located about 13–15 km west of Jerusalem on the border between Judah and Benjamin. The ark had been kept there since it was returned by the Philistines (1 Sam 6:21-7:2). The name "Baale" suggests that the place may previously have been associated with Baal worship before Israelite settlement.

**The threshing floor of Nacon** (v. 6), where Uzzah died, has an uncertain location. Threshing floors were often placed on high ground where wind could separate grain from chaff. The parallel text in 1 Chronicles 13:9 calls the place "the threshing floor of Kidon." The site was named "Perez-uzzah" ("Uzzah’s breach") as a memorial.

**The City of David** was the captured Jebusite fortress on Mount Zion (2 Sam 5:7). By bringing the ark there, David established Jerusalem as both a political and religious center, a strategic move that united the tribes around a new, neutral place with no connection to either Judah or Benjamin.

## Events and Customs

**Transporting the ark on a cart** (v. 3) followed Philistine practice (1 Sam 6:7-8) but broke with the Mosaic law’s instruction that the ark be carried by Levites using poles (Num 7:9; Exod 25:14). This error was corrected during the second attempt, when bearers ("נֹשְׂאֵי", noś'e) transported the ark (v. 13). 1 Chronicles 15:13-15 records that David explicitly acknowledged this mistake.

**David’s dancing** is described with several Hebrew verbs: "מְשַׂחֲקִים" (mesaḥaqim, "playing/dancing") in v. 5, and "מְפַזֵּז" (mefazzez) and "מְכַרְכֵּר" (mekharker) in v. 16, indicating energetic leaping and circular dancing. Such ecstatic dancing was known in ancient Near Eastern worship and expressed total devotion. That the king himself danced before the ark was a radical act in which David set aside his royal dignity to honor God.

**Offering sacrifices every six steps** (v. 13) was extraordinary. Some interpret this as one sacrifice at the beginning, while the literal reading suggests repeated offerings. This caution reflected the lesson from Uzzah’s death—that one must approach the holy with extreme reverence.

**Distributing food** (v. 19)—loaves of bread, portions of meat, and raisin cakes—followed the pattern for public celebrations where the king demonstrated generosity toward the people. Such communal meals strengthened the bond between ruler and people and marked the day as a holy festival.

## Key Terms

**Linen ephod** (אֵפ֥וֹד בָּֽד, 'efod bad) was a simple white linen garment worn by priests during service (1 Sam 2:18; 22:18). That David wore this during the procession shows that he functioned in a priestly role, which was acceptable for kings in certain contexts but which Michal considered unworthy of a king.

**"The Lord, the God of hosts, who sits enthroned above the cherubim"** (v. 2) is one of the most expanded titles for God in the Old Testament. "צְבָאוֹת" (tsevaot, "hosts") refers to heavenly armies, while "יֹשֵׁ֥ב הַכְּרֻבִ֖ים" ("enthroned above the cherubim") describes God’s invisible throne above the mercy seat of the ark of the covenant, where golden cherubim were shaped over the atonement cover (Exod 25:18-22).

**"הַשַּׁל"** (hashshal, v. 7) is a rare word translated variously as "presumption," "carelessness," or "negligence." Its meaning is debated, but the context suggests that Uzzah’s action, though well-intentioned, represented an overstepping of boundaries toward the holy.
