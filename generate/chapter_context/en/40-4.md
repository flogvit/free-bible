# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 4

## Places

**The Wilderness (ἡ ἔρημον):** Traditionally identified as the Judean wilderness, the rugged mountainous region between the highlands of Jerusalem and the Jordan Valley/Dead Sea. This area was associated with spiritual testing in Jewish tradition—here Israel wandered for forty years, and here prophets such as Elijah sought refuge. In Jewish thought, the wilderness was both a place of temptation and a place of closeness to God.

**Capernaum (Καφαρναούμ):** A fishing village on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee, strategically located along the trade route known as Via Maris. Archaeological excavations have uncovered a synagogue from the fourth century built over an earlier structure from the first century, as well as a house complex that early Christian traditions identify as Peter’s home. The city lay in the border region between the territories of Herod Antipas and Philip, which explains the presence of tax collectors.

**Zebulun and Naphtali (Ζαβουλὼν καὶ Νεφθαλίμ):** These ancient tribal territories in northern Israel were among the first areas conquered by the Assyrians in 732 B.C. The prophecy from Isaiah (Isa 9:1–2), quoted in verses 15–16, promised restoration specifically to this region that had experienced humiliation.

**The "pinnacle" of the temple (τὸ πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ):** The Greek word *pterygion* (little wing) likely refers to the southeastern corner of the temple wall, where it rises dramatically above the Kidron Valley—a drop perhaps 45 meters to the bottom. Josephus describes this height as dizzying to look down from.

## People

**The First Four Disciples:** Simon (called Peter, Σίμωνα τὸν λεγόμενον Πέτρον) and his brother Andrew (Ἀνδρέαν), along with the brothers James (Ἰάκωβον) and John (Ἰωάννην), sons of Zebedee (Ζεβεδαίου), were fishermen by the Sea of Galilee. Fishing was a vital industry in the region, organized through cooperatives and fishing rights controlled by authorities. The fact that Zebedee had hired workers (Mark 1:20) indicates a certain level of wealth. Archaeological finds of fishhooks, net sinkers, and anchor remains near the sea confirm active fishing during this period.

## Key Terms

**Temptation/Testing (πειρασθῆναι):** The Greek word *peirazō* can mean both "to tempt" (with evil intent) and "to test/prove" (to reveal character). In the Septuagint, the word is used of God testing Abraham and Israel. The forty days parallel Israel’s forty years in the wilderness and Moses’ forty days on Sinai.

**The Kingdom of Heaven (ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν):** This expression reflects Jewish reverence for speaking God’s name directly. "Heaven" serves as a circumlocution for "God." The term was charged with messianic expectations in the first century—many Jews expected that God would soon intervene to restore Israel’s kingdom.

**Fishers of Men (ἁλιεῖς ἀνθρώπων):** This metaphor likely alludes to Old Testament texts where God "fishes" for people—often in a context of judgment (Jer 16:16; Ezek 29:4). Jesus gives the image a new, saving meaning.

## Events and Customs

**Jesus’ Answers from Scripture:** In response to the devil’s three temptations, Jesus quotes from Deuteronomy—the book that deals with Israel’s wilderness experience. The three quotations are: "Man shall not live by bread alone" (Deut 8:3, v. 4), "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Deut 6:16, v. 7), and "The Lord your God you shall worship, and him only shall you serve" (Deut 6:13, v. 10). This presents Jesus as the true Israelite who succeeds where Israel failed in their testing.

**Synagogues (ταῖς συναγωγαῖς):** In Jesus’ time, synagogues functioned as local gathering places for Scripture reading, prayer, and teaching. Archaeological finds from Galilee, including the recently excavated first-century synagogue at Magdala, show simple buildings with stone benches along the walls. Visiting teachers could be invited to teach, which explains how Jesus was able to preach in the synagogues.
