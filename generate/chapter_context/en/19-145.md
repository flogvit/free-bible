# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 145

## Key Concepts

Psalm 145 is the only psalm in the Book of Psalms that bears the title **תְּהִלָּה** (*tehillah*, "praise"). This is remarkable since the entire Book of Psalms in Hebrew is called *Tehillim* (plural of *tehillah*), yet only this single psalm carries this as a genre designation. The term denotes pure, unconditional praise without elements of lament or requests for help.

Verse 8 reproduces one of the most central confessions in the Old Testament: **חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם** (*channun verachum*, "gracious and merciful"). This formulation is a direct echo of God's self-revelation in Exodus 34:6, and it became a foundational confession in Jewish liturgy. The expression **אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם** (*erekh appayim*, literally "long of nose") means "slow to anger" or "patient"—an idiom describing God's patience.

## Events and Customs

Psalm 145 is constructed as an **acrostic psalm**, where each verse begins with a new letter of the Hebrew alphabet in sequence. This was a well-known literary technique in ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature, used both as a memory aid and as an expression of completeness—from "A to Z." In the Masoretic text, the verse that should begin with the letter **נ** (*nun*) is missing between verses 13 and 14; however, the Dead Sea Scrolls (11QPsa) and the Septuagint include this verse, suggesting it was lost in some textual traditions.

In Jewish tradition, this psalm acquired special liturgical significance. The Talmud (Berakhot 4b) states that whoever recites this psalm three times daily is assured a place in the world to come. This led to the inclusion of the psalm in the daily prayers *Shacharit* (morning prayer) and *Mincha* (afternoon prayer), where it is still recited today.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

In the Dead Sea Scrolls, a version of Psalm 145 (in 11QPsa) was found that includes the "missing" nun verse between verses 13 and 14. This verse reads: "The Lord is faithful in all his words and gracious in all his deeds." The discovery confirms that the acrostic structure was originally complete and provides valuable insight into textual transmission in antiquity. The Dead Sea Scrolls version also includes a refrain after each verse ("Blessed be the Lord, and blessed be his name forever and ever"), which may reflect liturgical use in temple worship.
