# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 10

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the eighth and ninth plagues: the plague of locusts and the plague of darkness. Locust swarms (Hebrew: אַרְבֶּה, *arbeh*) were a well-known and feared disaster in the ancient Middle East. The text notes that an east wind (רוּחַ קָדִים, *ruach qadim*) brought the locusts (verse 13)—this aligns with natural wind patterns where dry desert winds from the east often carried locust swarms from the Arabian Peninsula and Sinai into the Nile Valley. The strong west wind (רוּחַ־יָם, *ruach yam*, literally "the sea wind") removed the locusts and drove them into the Sea of Reeds (verse 19). Egyptian texts from various periods mention locusts as one of the threats to Egypt's crops, and administrative documents contain instructions for reporting the movements of locust swarms.

The dialogue between Moses and Pharaoh about who should participate in the wilderness festival (verses 8–11) reflects Egyptian practice where religious festivals were often limited to specific groups. Pharaoh's counterproposal that only the men (הַגְּבָרִים, *ha-gevarim*) should go (verse 11) may reflect Egyptian cultic practices in which women and children were often excluded from certain rituals. Moses' insistence that the entire people with their livestock must go (verses 9, 26) underscores that Israelite worship was a familial and communal act requiring animals for sacrifices.

The darkness in Egypt has often been compared to *khamsin* winds, which bring intense sandstorms from the desert and can make the day so dark that one can barely see. However, the text's description of a darkness so thick that people could not see each other or move about for three days (verses 22–23) goes beyond natural explanations and points to divine intervention.

## Key Terms

The expression "to cover the face of the earth" (וְכִסָּה אֶת־עֵין הָאָרֶץ, verses 5 and 15) is a Hebrew idiom meaning that something covers the entire land surface so completely that the ground is no longer visible.

The darkness is described as חֹשֶׁךְ אֲפֵלָה (*choshekh afelah*)—"thick darkness" or "gripping darkness" (verse 22). The expression וְיָמֵשׁ חֹשֶׁךְ (verse 21) literally means "and the darkness could be felt/touched," suggesting a supernatural darkness far more intense than natural phenomena such as sandstorms. This darkness lasted for three days (verses 22–23)—the number three often marks a complete or concluded period in biblical literature.
