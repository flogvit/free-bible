# Historical and Cultural Context for Daniel 7

## People

**Belshazzar** is mentioned as king of Babylon in this chapter (v. 1). Historically, Belshazzar was the son of Nabonidus and served as co-regent in Babylon while his father was absent in Tema in Arabia. Belshazzar managed the daily administrative duties of Babylon from approximately 553–539 B.C.E. Babylonian cuneiform texts, including the Nabonidus Chronicle, confirm his role as crown prince with royal authority, even though he technically never held the title "king" in official Babylonian documents. The chapter dates the vision to his first year of rule (around 553 B.C.E.).

**The Ancient of Days** (עַתִּיק יוֹמִין, *Attiq Yomin*) is a unique title for God that appears only in this chapter (vv. 9, 13, 22). The description with white hair and white garments conveys eternity, purity, and wisdom—qualities associated in the ancient Near East with venerable elders and divine authority.

## Key Terms

**"One like a son of man"** (כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kebar enash*) in verse 13 is a central expression. In Aramaic, it literally means "like a son of man" and describes a figure with human appearance, in contrast to the four beasts. In Jewish tradition, this was interpreted messianically, and the expression gained great significance in later apocalyptic literature, including 1 Enoch.

**"The Great Sea"** (יַמָּא רַבָּא) from which the beasts arise (vv. 2–3) represents chaotic forces and evil in ancient Near Eastern cosmology. In Canaanite and Mesopotamian mythology, the sea was often associated with chaos monsters that threatened divine order. The four winds that stir up the sea intensify this image of chaos.

## Events and Customs

**The heavenly courtroom** described in verses 9–10, 26 reflects ancient Near Eastern concepts of divine council assemblies. Thrones being set out, books being opened, and thousands of servants standing before the Judge—all correspond to royal judgment rituals in antiquity, but elevated to a cosmic scale. The river of fire flowing forth symbolizes both purification and judgment, and fire was a common element of theophany in ancient Israel.

**The time expression "a time, times, and half a time"** (v. 25)—in Aramaic עִדָּן וְעִדָּנִין וּפְלַג עִדָּן—is an enigmatic time designation indicating a limited period of distress. This formulation is repeated later in Daniel 12:7.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The animal symbolism in the chapter has interesting parallels in ancient Near Eastern iconography. Winged lions (lamassu) were well-known guardian statues in Assyrian and Babylonian palaces, including at the gates of Nineveh and Babylon. The combination of predatory animals with wings and human features was an established iconographic tradition that the first readers of the Daniel text would immediately recognize as symbols of imperial power.
