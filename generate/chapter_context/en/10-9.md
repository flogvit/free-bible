# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 9

## People

**Mephibosheth (מְפִיבֹשֶׁת)** was the son of Jonathan and grandson of Saul. The name likely means "from Baal's mouth" (originally perhaps Merib-Baal, see 1 Chronicles 8:34), but in tradition it was changed by replacing "Baal" with "boshet" (shame). He became lame in both feet at the age of five when his nurse fled with him after hearing news of Saul and Jonathan's death at Gilboa (2 Samuel 4:4). His disability made him politically harmless, yet also vulnerable in a culture where physical wholeness was important for leadership.

**Ziba (צִיבָא)** was a servant who had belonged to Saul's household. His role as steward with fifteen sons and twenty servants (v. 10) indicates that he was a wealthy man of considerable status.

**Machir, son of Ammiel**, from Lo-debar, was clearly a wealthy man who had given Mephibosheth shelter, suggesting he was an influential supporter.

## Places

**Lo-debar (לוֹ דְבָר)** was a city in Gilead, east of the Jordan. The name may mean "nothing" or "without pasture," implying a desolate or insignificant place. Its location in Gilead, far from David's center of power in Jerusalem, made it a suitable refuge for a descendant of Saul. The city was probably near Mahanaim, where Ish-bosheth had held his kingdom.

## Events and Customs

**Eating at the king's table** was a great honor in the ancient Near East. This involved not only material support but also social status, protection, and inclusion in the king's inner circle. The practice is known from several ancient cultures, including Babylonian texts describing how deposed kings were given places at the victor's table (see 2 Kings 25:29 regarding Jehoiachin in Babylon). For Mephibosheth, this meant complete rehabilitation from refugee to royal privilege.

**Restoration of land holdings** followed the principle that land should remain within the family. David returned Saul's ancestral land to Mephibosheth, securing his economic independence. In ancient Israel, land rights were tied to family and tribe, and losing land meant loss of social position and honor.

## Key Terms

**Ḥesed (חֶסֶד)** – translated as "kindness" or "steadfast love" – is a central concept in this chapter (vv. 1, 3, 7). The expression "ḥesed Elohim" (God's kindness/grace, v. 3) denotes an undeserved, gracious goodness that goes beyond normal obligations. David's ḥesed toward Mephibosheth was motivated by the covenant with Jonathan (1 Samuel 20:14-17), in which they had promised to show kindness to each other's descendants.

**"A dead dog" (הַכֶּלֶב הַמֵּת)** was a strong expression of self-abasement in the ancient Near East. Dogs were regarded as unclean scavengers, and the expression appears in contemporary texts as a sign of extreme humility. Mephibosheth's use of this phrase reflects both cultural politeness and his actual situation as a disabled descendant of a fallen royal line.
