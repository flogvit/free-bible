# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 4

## People

**The Shunammite woman** is described as a "great woman" (אִשָּׁה גְדוֹלָה), an expression denoting social status and wealth, not physical size. She and her husband could afford to build a special room for the prophet, indicating considerable riches. Her independent actions—furnishing the guest room, seeking out the prophet alone, and making decisions without consulting her husband—show that wealthy women in ancient Israel could have significant autonomy.

**Gehazi** (גֵּחֲזִי) serves as Elisha's personal servant and intermediary. His role illustrates the hierarchical relationship between prophets and their disciples. The meaning of his name is uncertain but may be related to Hebrew roots for "valley" or "vision."

## Places

**Shunem** (שׁוּנֵם) was located in the Jezreel Valley, identified with the village of Sulam (modern Shunem/Solem) at the foot of Mount Gilboa. The place held strategic importance along the main road between Samaria and Galilee, which explains why Elisha "often passed through" there. The city is also mentioned in Joshua 19:18 as a town within the territory of the tribe of Issachar.

**Mount Carmel** served as Elisha's base and is the same mountain where Elijah confronted the prophets of Baal. The distance from Shunem to Mount Carmel was approximately 20–25 kilometers, a journey the Shunammite woman made on a donkey in her desperation.

**Gilgal**, mentioned toward the end of the chapter, was a center for prophetic communities, not the same Gilgal near the Jordan associated with Joshua. This Gilgal was likely located in the hill country near Bethel.

**Baal-Shalishah** (בַּעַל שָׁלִשָׁה) was a place in the hills of Ephraim, possibly near modern Kafr Thilth. The name means "Baal of Shalishah" or "Lord of the Third," testifying to the region's Canaanite past.

## Events and Customs

**Debt slavery** was an accepted practice in the ancient Near East. When a man died with unpaid debts, the creditor had the right to take the debtor's children as slaves until the debt was paid off. This practice is regulated in Exodus 21:2–11 and Leviticus 25:39–43, which limited the period of servitude to a maximum of six years for Israelites. The widow's situation in verses 1–7 illustrates the vulnerable position of women without a male provider.

**The guest room** (עֲלִיַּת־קִיר) built by the Shunammite woman was a small upper chamber on the roof, a common architectural solution in the region. The furnishings—a bed, table, chair, and lamp—represented basic comforts for a guest of rank. Such rooms are known from archaeological excavations of Israelite houses.

**Visits on the new moon and Sabbath** (verse 23) show that these days were established times for seeking out prophets and holy men. The husband's question suggests that religious consultations normally took place on such holy days.

## Key Terms

**"Sons of the prophets"** (בְנֵי הַנְּבִיאִים) were not biological sons of prophets but members of prophetic communities or disciples of prophets. These groups lived together, shared meals, and received instruction from a leading prophet such as Elisha. The chapter shows their simple living conditions and community life centered around the prophet.

**Firstfruits bread** (לֶחֶם בִּכּוּרִים) in verse 42 was normally dedicated to God and the priests (Numbers 18:13). The fact that the man from Baal-Shalishah brought this to Elisha shows that the prophet was regarded as a holy man worthy of such gifts, and possibly suggests that Levitical institutions were weakened in the northern kingdom.

**Wild gourds** (פַּקֻּעֹת שָׂדֶה) were likely colocynth (Citrullus colocynthis), also known as bitter apple or wild gourd, a bitter and poisonous plant that grows wild in the Middle East. It resembles edible gourds, which explains the confusion.
