## People

### "The Elder" (ὁ πρεσβύτερος)
The author identifies himself as "the elder" (presbyteros), a title in the early church that could refer to both age and office. The fact that the author uses only this title without giving his name suggests that his identity was well known and his authority unquestioned among the recipients.

### "The Elect Lady" (ἐκλεκτῇ κυρίᾳ)
The recipient is described as "the elect lady and her children." Scholars debate whether this refers to a specific woman named Kyria (a common Greek female name) or if it is a personification of a local congregation (with the "children" being members of that church). The closing greeting from "the children of your elect sister" (v. 13) strengthens the interpretation that this concerns two sister congregations, where "the sister" likely refers to the congregation where the author resides.

## Events and Customs

### House Church and Hospitality
The letter reflects the early church's practice of gathering in private homes (οἰκία, v. 10). Traveling preachers depended on Christian hospitality for food, lodging, and support for further travel. This custom was essential for missionary work but also created vulnerability to false teaching. The instruction to refuse housing and greetings to false teachers (vv. 10–11) was radically strict in a culture where hospitality was a sacred duty. The Greek greeting χαίρειν was more than politeness—it expressed fellowship and recognition.

## Key Terms

### "The Truth" (ἡ ἀλήθεια)
The word "truth" (aletheia) appears five times in this short letter (vv. 1, 2, 3, 4). The truth is not merely correct doctrine but a living reality connected to Christ himself (cf. John 14:6). To "walk in the truth" (v. 4) means to live in faith and obedience to the gospel of Jesus Christ.

### "Comes in the flesh" (ἐρχόμενον ἐν σαρκί)
The participle form ἐρχόμενον (present tense) in verse 7 emphasizes Christ's ongoing incarnation—he came in the flesh and remains human. This was directed against early docetic tendencies that claimed Christ had only an apparent physical body. Confessing Jesus' real humanity was a crucial criterion for true Christian teaching.

### "Antichrist" (ὁ ἀντίχριστος)
The term "antichrist" is used here (v. 7) to refer to anyone who denies the reality of the incarnation. In contrast to later apocalyptic expectations of one end-time figure, the author applies the term to contemporary false teachers who have already "gone out into the world."

## Chapter-Specific Findings

### Letter Format and Writing Materials
Verse 12 mentions "paper and ink" (χάρτου καὶ μέλανος). Archaeological discoveries of papyrus letters from Egypt show that private letters were typically limited to one papyrus sheet, which explains the letter's brevity. Second John corresponds exactly in length to a standard papyrus sheet. The black ink was usually made from soot mixed with gum arabic.
