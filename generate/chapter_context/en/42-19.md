## People

**Zacchaeus** (Ζακχαῖος) was a "chief tax collector" (ἀρχιτελώνης) in Jericho – a title that appears only here in the New Testament. As chief tax collector, he led a network of tax collectors who gathered taxes on behalf of the Romans. Jericho was a strategic hub for trade in balsam, dates, and other goods, making the toll station there extremely lucrative. The name Zacchaeus comes from the Hebrew *Zakkai* ("pure", "innocent" or "righteous"), which ironically stood in contrast to his profession. Tax collectors were despised both because they collaborated with the occupying power and because they often demanded more than was required. His promise to repay fourfold (τετραπλοῦν) exceeded the usual requirement in Mosaic law for full restitution plus one-fifth (Exod 6:5), but aligned with Exod 22:1 regarding theft of small livestock and with Roman restitution law.

## Places

**Jericho** lay about 250 meters below sea level in the Jordan Valley, approximately 25 kilometers northeast of Jerusalem. In Jesus' time there were actually two cities: ancient Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) and new Jericho, which Herod the Great had developed with palaces, a hippodrome, and gardens watered by an advanced aqueduct system. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of Herod's winter palace and its prosperous infrastructure. The city was known for its cultivation of balsam and as a favored winter retreat for the elite. The sycamore-fig tree (συκομορέα) that Zacchaeus climbed was common in the lowlands – a tree with a short trunk and broad branches that made it easy to climb.

**Bethphage and Bethany** were villages on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, along the road from Jericho to Jerusalem. Bethany lay about 2.5–3 kilometers from Jerusalem (cf. John 11:18: "fifteen stadia"), while Bethphage was located closer to the city. The name Bethphage probably means "house of unripe figs". The Mount of Olives (τὸ ὄρος τὸ καλούμενον Ἐλαιῶν) was covered with olive groves and held great religious significance – the prophet Zechariah had foretold that the Lord would stand on this mountain (Zech 14:4).

## Events and Customs

**The entry on a colt** had deep symbolic meaning. The fact that no one had ever sat on the animal before (ἐφʼ ὃν οὐδεὶς πώποτε ἀνθρώπων ἐκάθισεν) made it holy and suitable for royal or sacred use. Spreading cloaks on the road was an ancient gesture of honoring a king, known from when Jehu was anointed (2 Kgs 9:13). The people's cry "Blessed is the King who comes" combined Psalm 118:26 with an explicit royal title, giving the entry an unmistakably messianic character.

**Jesus' weeping over Jerusalem** (vv. 41–44) is one of only two instances in the Gospels where Jesus weeps. When he saw the city, he broke into tears (ἔκλαυσεν) and prophesied its destruction. The prophecy describes a siege with trenches (χάρακα), encirclement, and total devastation – details that were literally fulfilled when the Romans under Titus besieged and destroyed Jerusalem in 70 AD. The expression "your visitation" (τὸν καιρὸν τῆς ἐπισκοπῆς σου) refers to God's gracious presence in Jesus.

**The Parable of the Minas** (μνᾶς) mirrored an actual historical event: When Herod the Great died in 4 BC, his son Archelaus traveled to Rome to obtain the emperor's approval as king. A Jewish delegation of fifty men followed to protest his rule – just as in the parable (v. 14). Augustus gave Archelaus the title "ethnarch" over Judea but later deposed him in 6 AD due to his brutal governance. Jesus' listeners would have immediately recognized this historical parallel.

## Key Terms

**Minah** (μνᾶ) was a Greek monetary unit equivalent to about 100 denarii – roughly three months' wages for a laborer. Unlike the talents in Matthew's parallel account (where one talent equals 6,000 denarii), the minah was a modest sum, emphasizing that everyone started from the same point. The expression "put on the table" (ἐπὶ τράπεζαν) refers to the money changers' tables, where deposits could earn interest (τόκος) – a practice common among Gentiles but controversial among Jews.

**The Cleansing of the Temple** was directed at trade in the Court of the Gentiles – the only area where non-Jews could worship. Jesus' quotation combines Isaiah 56:7 ("a house of prayer") and Jeremiah 7:11 ("den of robbers"). The word for "robbers" (λῃστῶν) did not refer to petty thieves but to violent bandits or rebels, underscoring the seriousness of the accusation.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Herodian Jericho have revealed an extensive palace complex with swimming pools, bathing facilities, and gardens. The discovery of tax stamps and administrative buildings confirms the city's importance as a commercial center. At the Mount of Olives, remains of the ancient road from Jericho to Jerusalem have been found, along with first-century graves that demonstrate the area's use during Jesus' time. Josephus' detailed descriptions of Archelaus' journey to Rome and the Jewish delegation's resistance confirm the historical background for the Parable of the Minas.
