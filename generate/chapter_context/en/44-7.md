# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 7

## People

**Stephen** appears in this chapter as the first Christian martyr. His Greek name (Στέφανος, "crown") suggests he was a Hellenistic Jew—a Jew from the diaspora who spoke Greek as his primary language. His deep knowledge of the Old Testament, demonstrated through his lengthy speech, shows that Hellenistic Jews often had thorough training in the Scriptures, even though they primarily used the Septuagint (the Greek translation).

**Saul** is briefly introduced in verse 58 as a young man (νεανίου) who guarded the clothes of those stoning Stephen. This is the first mention in Acts of him who later becomes the apostle Paul. The term "young man" (νεανίας) could in antiquity describe someone between about 20 and 40 years old. His presence and approval of the execution (8:1) place him as an active participant in the persecution of the early church.

## Places

**Mesopotamia and Haran** (verses 2–4) refer to the region between the Euphrates and Tigris rivers in present-day Iraq and southeastern Turkey. Haran (Akkadian: crossroads) was an important trading post on caravan routes and a well-known center for moon-god worship. Archaeological excavations have confirmed the city's existence from the third millennium B.C.

**Shechem** (verse 16) is mentioned as the burial place of the patriarchs. The city lay in the heart of Samaria at Mount Gerizim and was an important religious and political center in Israelite history. It is here that we find Jacob's well and the tradition of Joseph's tomb, which are still shown in the area today.

**Mount Sinai** (verses 30, 38) was the place of God's revelation to Moses in the burning bush and later for the giving of the law. Its exact location is debated, but tradition has linked it to Jebel Musa on the Sinai Peninsula since Byzantine times.

## Events and Customs

**Stoning as an execution method** (verses 58–59) was the prescribed Jewish penalty for blasphemy according to Leviticus 24:14–16. The procedure required that the condemned be taken outside the city, and that the witnesses who had testified against them cast the first stones (cf. Deut 17:7). The text says the witnesses laid their clothes at Saul's feet (verse 58), indicating his role as overseer or accomplice. Romans normally did not allow Jews to carry out capital punishment (cf. John 18:31), but this event may have occurred during a period of power vacuum or as a lynching that the Romans overlooked.

**The covenant of circumcision** (verse 8) was the central sign of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants. Performance on the eighth day was strictly prescribed in Jewish law and practice. Stephen's point is that the covenant relationship with God existed before the temple and independent of any specific geographic location.

## Key Terms

**"The tent of witness"** (Ἡ σκηνὴ τοῦ μαρτυρίου, verse 44) is the Septuagint's designation for the tabernacle that Moses built in the wilderness. Stephen's argument rests on the fact that this movable sanctuary represented God's presence with His people long before Solomon's temple was built—a central point in his critique of temple-centered theology.

**"Stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears"** (Σκληροτράχηλοι καὶ ἀπερίτμητοι καρδίαις καὶ τοῖς ὠσίν, verse 51) is prophetic language drawn from the Old Testament (cf. Deut 10:16; Jer 4:4). The expression "stiff neck" describes a draft animal that refuses to bow under the yoke. By calling his listeners "uncircumcised in heart," Stephen turns their own identity marker against them.

**"The host of heaven"** (τῇ στρατιᾷ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ, verse 42) refers to astral worship—the veneration of the sun, moon, and stars as deities. This was widespread in Mesopotamia and Egypt and repeatedly tempted the Israelites throughout history.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Stephen's quotation in verse 43 concerning "Molech" and "the star of your god Rephan" follows the Septuagint version of Amos 5:26–27, which differs somewhat from the Hebrew text. "Rephan" (Ῥαιφάν) is likely a Greek rendering of the Egyptian name for the planet Saturn. Assyrian and Babylonian texts confirm widespread star worship in antiquity, and archaeological finds from Canaan have uncovered altars linked to Molech worship, including child sacrifice.
