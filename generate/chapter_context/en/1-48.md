# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 48

## People

**Ephraim and Manasseh** are Joseph’s two sons, born in Egypt to his Egyptian wife Asenath (Gen 41:50–52). Their names are Hebrew and carry theological significance: Manasseh (מְנַשֶּׁה) comes from the verb נשׁה "to forget" – Joseph said that God had made him forget all his hardship. Ephraim (אֶפְרַיִם) comes from the verb פרה "to be fruitful" – Joseph said that God had made him fruitful in the land of his affliction (Gen 41:51–52).

**Jacob/Israel** – both names are used in this chapter. The text uses "Israel" in verses 2, 8, 10, 11, 13, 14, 20, and 21, and "Jacob" in verses 2 and 3. In verse 2 both names appear: first "Jacob" when he receives the message, then "Israel" as he gathers his strength. The alternation of names may have literary or theological significance.

## Places

**Luz/Bethel** (verse 3) was the place where God revealed Himself to Jacob with a ladder reaching to heaven (Gen 28:10–22). Jacob refers back to this revelation as the basis for the blessing he now gives. Luz was the original Canaanite name of the place, while Bethel ("house of God") was the name Jacob gave it.

**Ephrath/Bethlehem** (verse 7) is mentioned in connection with Rachel’s death and burial. When Jacob came from Paddan-aram, Rachel died on the way when only a short distance (כִּבְרַת־אֶרֶץ, a rare expression also found in Gen 35:16 and 2 Kgs 5:19) remained to Ephrath. Jacob’s sudden mention of Rachel’s death in the middle of the adoption ceremony may explain why he takes Joseph’s sons as his own – they are grandchildren of his beloved Rachel, and by adopting them he honors her memory.

## Events and Customs

**Adoption of Grandchildren** (verses 5–6) was a known practice in the ancient Near East. Jacob formally declares that Ephraim and Manasseh shall be counted as his own sons, on equal footing with Reuben and Simeon. This gave them the right to their own tribal portion in the inheritance. Legal texts from Nuzi (second millennium BCE) document similar adoption practices, where grandchildren could be elevated to the status of sons to secure inheritance rights.

**Laying on of Hands and Blessing** (verses 13–20) was a central ritual act in the transfer of blessing and authority. The right hand symbolized the primary position and greater honor. Jacob’s deliberate crossing of his hands (שִׂכֵּל אֶת־יָדָיו – "he crossed his hands with intention/insight") breaks with the rights of the firstborn and establishes Ephraim as the foremost. This pattern of preferring the younger over the older is also seen in Jacob’s own story, adding extra depth to this scene.

## Key Terms

**El Shaddai** (אֵל שַׁדַּי) in verse 3 is the divine name especially associated with the patriarchs and the promises of fruitfulness and multiplication. Jacob uses this name as he anchors the blessing in covenant history.

**"The Angel who redeems me"** (הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל) in verse 16 uses the participle form of the verb גאל (ga’al, "to redeem"). A גֹּאֵל (go’el) was a kinsman with the duty to protect and redeem family members from distress, slavery, or loss of property. Jacob identifies God Himself (or His angel) as his redeemer through life’s trials.

**Shechem** (שְׁכֶם) in verse 22 means both "shoulder/ridge" and is the name of the city of Shechem. Jacob gives Joseph "one shechem more than your brothers" – a wordplay pointing both to an extra inheritance portion and to the specific land area near Shechem which later became Joseph’s burial place (Josh 24:32).
