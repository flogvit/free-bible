# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 77

## People

The psalm is linked to **Asaph** (אָסָף), one of the leading singers appointed by David for temple service (1 Chron 16:5). The heading also mentions **Jeduthun** (יְדוּתוּן), another leading temple singer (1 Chron 16:41-42), indicating that the psalm was to be performed in his musical tradition or by his choir.

At the conclusion of the psalm, **Moses and Aaron** (v. 20) are mentioned as shepherds for the people during the exodus from Egypt. These two represent respectively the prophetic and priestly leadership that God used to deliver His people.

## Events and Customs

The second half of the psalm (vv. 16-20) contains a poetic retelling of the **Red Sea miracle** (Exod 14), where the waters are personified as if they saw God and trembled in fear. This description uses theophanic language—thunder, lightning, earthquake—which was typical for descriptions of divine revelations in the ancient Near East. Similar portrayals of storms as divine weapons appear in Canaanite and Mesopotamian literature, but here these phenomena are entirely subordinate to Israel's God.

The expression "your footprints were not known" (v. 19) reflects the idea that God acts in history in ways that transcend human understanding—the sea closed behind the people, leaving no physical traces.

The expression **"the sons of Jacob and Joseph"** (בְּנֵי־יַעֲקֹב וְיוֹסֵף, v. 15) is an unusual formulation that encompasses all Israel. Joseph is likely mentioned separately because his descendants formed the prominent northern tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **חֶסֶד** (ḥesed, "steadfast love/mercy") in verse 8 is a central covenant term expressing God's enduring loyalty toward His people. The psalmist's question about whether this mercy has "ceased forever" reveals the depth of the crisis.

The expression **שְׁנוֹת יְמִין עֶלְיוֹן** ("the years of the right hand of the Most High," v. 10) is textually debated. The word **חַלּוֹתִי** earlier in the verse can mean either "my weakness/pain" or "change," which affects the understanding of the entire sentence. "Right hand" symbolized power and deliverance in Hebrew thought.

**בַּגַּלְגַּל** (baggalgal, "in the whirlwind/wheel," v. 18) may refer to the rolling thunder across the sky, a powerful image of God's voice in the storm.
