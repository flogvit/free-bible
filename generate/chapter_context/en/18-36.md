## Key Concepts

Chapter 36 contains several Hebrew words and expressions that require further explanation. The word **"כַּבִּיר" (kabbir)** in verse 5, meaning "mighty" or "great," is a rare term in the Old Testament and occurs mainly in the book of Job. It emphasizes God's overwhelming greatness and power. Elihu also uses the expression **"תְּמִים דֵּעוֹת" (temim de'ot)** in verse 4, which can be translated as "perfect in knowledge." This phrase may be understood to mean that Elihu claims to speak on behalf of one who possesses perfect knowledge (God), or it could serve as a rhetorical demand for sincerity in his speech.

In verse 14 we find the expression **"בַּקְּדֵשִׁים" (baqqedeshim)**, often translated as "temple prostitutes" or "cultic prostitutes." The term קָדֵשׁ (qadesh) refers to persons associated with pagan cults, possibly involving sexual rituals, although the exact practice remains debated among scholars. Elihu uses this as an example of a shameful death—dying among such people represented moral decay.

## Events and Customs

Elihu's description of the water cycle in verses 27–28 is remarkable. He portrays how God "draws up drops of water" which then "fall down as rain." This presents a poetic depiction of the water cycle attributed to God's direct activity, consistent with ancient Near Eastern cosmology where natural processes were understood as expressions of divine action. In the ancient Middle East, control over weather was a central characteristic of divinity—both the Canaanite god Baal and the Babylonian Marduk were worshipped as storm gods.

Verse 18 warns against letting "ransom" (כֹּפֶר, kofer) lead one astray. In ancient Israel, ransom was generally forbidden in cases of murder (Numbers 35:31), but the concept of compensation was known within the broader context of restitution law. Elihu uses this image to warn Job against believing that wealth can buy freedom from God's judgment.
