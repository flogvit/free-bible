# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 47

## Places

The chapter mentions a number of geographical locations connected to the flow of water and the borders of the land. The **Dead Sea** (Heb. הַיָּם הַקַּדְמוֹנִי, "the eastern sea") is the final destination of the water, where it will be "healed" – a remarkable vision since the Dead Sea, with its extreme salt content, is biologically dead. **En-Gedi** and **En-Eglaim** (v. 10) are oases on the western shore of the Dead Sea where fishermen will stand. En-Gedi is well documented archaeologically as a fertile oasis with fresh water springs, while the exact location of En-Eglaim is debated, possibly near today's Ain Feshkha north of the Dead Sea.

The description of the borders (vv. 15–20) outlines an idealized land area reminiscent of the promises to Abraham. The boundaries extend from **Hamath** (identified with ancient Hamath, near modern Hama in Syria) in the north to **Kadesh-barnea** ("the waters of Meribah at Kadesh") in the south, and from the **Jordan River** in the east to the **Mediterranean Sea** ("the great sea") in the west. **Hauran**, **Gilead**, and **Damascus** mark the eastern border and represent areas that historically belonged to Israel under David and Solomon.

## Events and Customs

The distribution of the land as an inheritance (Heb. נַחֲלָה, *naḥalah*) was to take place by casting lots – an established Israelite practice with roots going back to Joshua's allocation of the land (Josh 14–19). The revolutionary aspect of Ezekiel's vision is that **foreigners** (גֵּרִים, *gerim*) were to receive an inheritance on equal terms with native-born Israelites (vv. 22–23). This broke radically with earlier practice in which *gerim* had limited rights and could not own land permanently. In the ancient Near East, land rights were closely tied to kinship and tribal bonds, so this inclusion of foreigners represented a significant expansion of the covenant community.

## Key Terms

The expression **מֵי אָפְסָיִם** (*me afsayim*, v. 3) literally means "water to the ankles" and introduces a progressive measurement of depth that symbolizes the growth of God's blessing. The verb **נִרְפְּאוּ** (*nirpe'u*, "were healed", v. 8) is used concerning the transformation of the Dead Sea – the same root is used for healing from sickness and restoration. This points to a total overturning of the natural order.

The term **לִתְרוּפָה** (*litrufah*, "for healing", v. 12) describes the healing leaves of the trees, a vision of paradisiacal restoration. The image of water flowing out from the sanctuary and giving life to trees on both banks (vv. 7, 12) has clear parallels with the motif of Eden's garden (Gen 2:10), presenting the temple as a new paradise where life-giving water flows outward.
