# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 103

## People

The psalm is attributed to David (verse 1) and contains a direct reference to Moses (verse 7). The statement that the Lord "made known his ways to Moses" (יוֹדִיעַ דְּרָKÁיו לְמֹשֶׁה) points back to the intimate revelation Moses received on Mount Sinai, especially the episode described in Exodus 33–34 where Moses asked to see God's glory. The wording in verse 8 – "The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" – is nearly a direct quotation from Exodus 34:6, where God proclaimed his name and character to Moses. This shows that the psalmist consciously connects his song of praise with Israel's foundational revelation.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's call "Bless the Lord, O my soul" (Hebrew: *barakhi nafshi*) represents a distinctive form of self-exhortation in Hebrew poetry. This kind of inner dialogue, where the psalmist addresses his own soul, is also found in Psalms 42 and 104. The expression "all my inward parts" (verse 1) reflects the Hebrew view that the whole person – not just the intellect – should participate in worship. In ancient Israelite thought, the inner organs were regarded as the seat of emotions and the deepest part of a human being.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word *chesed* (חֶסֶד) appears four times in this psalm (verses 4, 8, 11, 17) and is a central concept that is difficult to translate fully. It denotes God's faithful, covenant loyalty – a combination of love, loyalty, and grace flowing from his covenant relationship with Israel. The word *rachamim* (רַחֲמִים), "mercy" (verse 4), and the verb form *richam* (רִחַם) in verse 13 are related to the Hebrew word for womb (*rechem*) and carry connotations of the instinctive, deep love a mother has for her child.

The expression in verse 5, often translated "your crown" or "your lifetime," is in Hebrew *edyek* (עֶדְיֵךְ), a rare word that interpreters have understood differently. The image of the eagle renewing its youth (תִּתְחַדֵּשׁ כַּנֶּשֶׁר נְעוּרָיְכִי) was widespread in antiquity and played on the idea of the eagle's long life and its ability to molt its feathers.

Verse 14 uses the word *yetser* (יִצֶר), "our formation" or "how we are shaped," which points to humanity's creation from dust (Genesis 2:7). The same root is later used in Jewish tradition regarding human inclinations (*yetser hara* and *yetser hatov*).
