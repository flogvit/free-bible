# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 38

## People

King Hezekiah is the main character in this chapter. His illness and healing can be dated to around 701 B.C., the same period as Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem. Hezekiah's additional fifteen years of life (v. 5) align with biblical chronologies for his reign.

## Places

"The steps of Ahaz" (מַעֲלוֹת אָחָז, v. 8) refer to a sundial or stair-like device constructed by King Ahaz. This was likely a form of sundial based on the movement of shadows across steps, a technology known in the ancient Near East. Such sundials are documented in both Egyptian and Mesopotamian cultures. The location was probably within or near the palace area in Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

Hezekiah's prayer facing the wall (v. 2) reflects a common prayer practice of turning away from surroundings to create an intimate space with God. The use of fig poultices as medical treatment (v. 21) was known in antiquity. Figs were applied to hasten the maturation of boils and as an anti-inflammatory remedy. This illustrates the interplay between divine healing and natural remedies in Israelite thought.

## Key Terms

The word "מִכְתָּב" (miktav, v. 9) means "writing" or "document" and introduces Hezekiah's song of thanksgiving—a genre similar to lament psalms. The expression "שְׁאוֹל" (sheol, vv. 10, 18) denotes the realm of the dead, understood as a shadowy existence separated from God and without the ability to praise Him. The phrase "בִּדְמִי יָמַי" (v. 10) means "in the midst of my days" and expresses grief over an untimely death, which was especially tragic in a culture where long life was seen as God's blessing.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The historical framework for this chapter is confirmed by several archaeological discoveries. A bulla (seal impression) with the inscription "Belonging to Hezekiah, son of Ahaz, king of Judah" was found in Jerusalem in 2015. Sennacherib's prism mentions the siege of Jerusalem and describes how he shut Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage." These findings confirm the historical setting for the events in Isaiah 36–39, including the Assyrian threat mentioned in verse 6.
