# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 24

## Places

The psalm mentions "the mountain of the Lord" (הַר־יְהוָה) and "his holy place" (מְקוֹם קָדְשׁוֹ), referring to Mount Zion in Jerusalem where the temple stood. The expression "to go up" (יַעֲלֶה) reflects actual geographical reality—pilgrims had to physically ascend to reach the temple area. Jerusalem is situated on a high ridge in the Judean hills, and the temple was located on Mount Moriah on the city's eastern side. This topography made "ascent" a central concept in Israelite worship life.

## Events and Customs

The structure of the psalm suggests a liturgical procession. Traditionally, the psalm has been linked to when David brought the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6), but it may also have been used during later festival processions. The dramatic dialogue in verses 7–10, where the gates are urged to lift up and the questions "Who is this King of glory?" are answered, points toward an antiphonal performance at the temple gate. This type of entrance liturgy (called "torah-liturgy" by scholars) also appears in Psalm 15, where similar questions about who is worthy to enter the sanctuary are asked and answered.

Verses 3–4 describe requirements for those who will "go up"—clean hands and a pure heart. This reflects purity requirements that were central to Israelite temple worship, where both ritual and moral purity were prerequisites for appearing before God.

## Key Terms

The expression "פִּתְחֵי עוֹלָם" (everlasting doors/ancient gates) in verses 7 and 9 is poetically charged. The Hebrew word "עוֹלָם" can mean both "eternal" and "of old/ancient times." The expression may refer to the venerability of the temple gates or to a cosmic dimension where heaven's gates open for the divine King. The title "יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת" (the Lord of hosts) in verse 10 emphasizes God's role as cosmic warrior and ruler over both heavenly and earthly armies—a title especially connected with the ark of the covenant and its presence in the sanctuary.

The expression "נְקִי כַפַּיִם" (clean hands) symbolizes innocent actions, while "בַר־לֵבָב" (pure heart) denotes inner purity and sincerity. Together they express that both outward behavior and inward attitude are required of those who wish to appear before God.
