# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 3

## People

**Jehoram (Joram) of Israel** was the son of Ahab and Jezebel, and reigned in Samaria approximately 852–841 B.C.E. The text presents him as a complex figure: he removed the Baal idol that his father had erected, yet still held fast to Jeroboam’s calf cult. This shows an attempt at religious reform without complete conversion to the worship of Yahweh alone.

**Mesha, king of Moab**, is one of the best-documented figures from the Old Testament through archaeology. He is described as a *noqed* (נֹקֵד), a term also used for the prophet Amos. This means "sheep breeder" or "large-scale sheep owner," indicating that Moab’s wealth was based on livestock. The enormous tribute of 100,000 lambs and wool from 100,000 rams reveals both Moab’s resources and the heavy burden Israel had imposed upon them.

**Elisha** is introduced here as Elijah’s successor with the description "who poured water on the hands of Elijah" – an expression of personal service and a disciple relationship. His use of music to receive prophecy (v. 15) reflects a practice known from prophetic communities in Israel.

## Places

**The Desert of Edom** was the route chosen by the three kings to avoid direct confrontation with Moab’s fortifications in the north. This seven-day detour led them through the dry landscape east of the Dead Sea and south of Moab. The area is known for its dry wadis (river valleys) that fill with water only during heavy rains in the mountains.

**Kir-Hareseth** (קִיר חֲרָשֶׂת, v. 25), identified with modern Kerak in Jordan, was Moab’s main fortress. The city sits on a plateau about 1,000 meters above sea level and was nearly impregnable due to its steep slopes on three sides. Archaeological excavations have confirmed the city’s strategic importance in the Iron Age.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Mesha Stele** (also called the Moabite Stone), discovered in 1868 in Dhiban, Jordan, is one of the most important archaeological finds illuminating this chapter. The inscription, dated to approximately 840 B.C.E., is written from King Mesha’s perspective and describes his rebellion against Israel. The stele mentions Omri, Ahab’s father, and confirms that Israel had oppressed Moab for many years. Mesha boasts of having liberated Moab and rebuilt cities using Israelite captives as slave laborers. Interestingly, the stele also mentions Yahweh (YHWH) and describes how Mesha took cult objects from Israelite sanctuaries.

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** (v. 27) was a known but abhorrent practice in Canaanite and Moabite culture. That Mesha sacrificed his firstborn son on the city wall was a desperate attempt to invoke divine intervention from Chemosh, Moab’s national god. The expression "great wrath came upon Israel" (קֶצֶף־גָּדוֹל) is theologically ambiguous – it may refer to the renewed fighting spirit of the Moabites, divine anger, or the shock and demoralization of the Israelites witnessing such an act. This event caused the Israelites to withdraw, demonstrating the psychological and perhaps theological impact of such extreme religious actions.

**Prophetic music** (v. 15): Elisha’s request for a harp player (*menaggēn*) before he prophesied reflects an ancient practice in which music was used to induce a prophetic trance or open the mind to divine revelation. Similar practices are documented in 1 Samuel 10:5–6, where groups of prophets used musical instruments.
