# Historical and Cultural Context for Esther 5

## Places

The chapter provides a detailed picture of the palace architecture in Susa. The "inner court" (חֲצַר בֵּית־הַמֶּלֶךְ הַפְּנִימִית) was an area with restricted access, and entering it without being summoned carried the risk of death. The throne room ("the king's house") was positioned so that the king had a direct view of the entrance, which aligns with the description of him seeing Esther as she stood in the court.

## Events and Customs

The king's golden scepter (שַׁרְבִיט הַזָּהָב) played a central role in Persian court ceremony. Extending the scepter was a sign that the king accepted the visitor's presence and granted permission to approach. Persian kings were surrounded by strict protocols, and unannounced access could be punished by death. Esther touching the tip of the scepter was an act of submission acknowledging the king's power and mercy.

The expression "half the kingdom" (עַד־חֲצִי הַמַּלְכוּת) was a formal courtesy formula in Eastern courts, not a literal offer.

## Key Terms

The word מִשְׁתֶּה ("mishteh" – feast, banquet) refers to a festive meal where wine was central. Esther's strategy of inviting the king and Haman to two successive banquets shows sophisticated use of court culture to achieve her goals.

The Hebrew verb וַיִּתְאַפַּק ("vayit'appaq" – he restrained himself) in verse 10 describes Haman's self-control and is the same word used about Joseph in Genesis 43:31. It indicates intense emotional suppression.

## The Stake

The stake or pole that Haman had erected (עֵץ גָּבֹהַּ חֲמִשִּׁים אַמָּה) was, according to the text, about 25 meters high. The Hebrew עֵץ can refer to both a stake for impalement or a gallows, and the verb תלה can mean both "hang" and "impale". Such structures were intended to be visible so as to serve as deterrent examples.
