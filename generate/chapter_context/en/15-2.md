## People

Ezra 2 introduces eleven leaders who led the return from Babylon (verse 2). Zerubbabel (זְרֻבָּבֶל) was the political leader, a descendant of King Jeconiah and thus of Davidic lineage. Joshua (יֵשׁוּעַ) was the high priest and religious leader, son of Jozadak. These two formed the dual leadership—political and religious—that characterized the early post-exilic period. The other nine leaders likely represented various clans or groups within the people.

The title "governor" (הַתִּרְשָׁ֙תָא֙, *hatirshata*, verse 63) is a Persian loanword denoting a governor or high official. This shows that the Jews operated within the Persian administrative system, even though they enjoyed considerable internal autonomy.

## Places

The chapter mentions several towns and villages in Judah and Benjamin where the returnees settled. Many of these have been identified archaeologically: Bethlehem (verse 21), Anathoth (verse 23)—Jeremiah's hometown about 5 km northeast of Jerusalem, Kiriath-jearim, Kephirah, and Beeroth (verse 25) in the Benjamin area, and Jericho (verse 34). Also mentioned are Lod, Hadid, and Ono (verse 33), located in the Shephelah region west of Jerusalem.

Tel-melah and Tel-harsha (verse 59) were Babylonian localities from which Jews of uncertain lineage came. The prefix "Tel" (תֵּל) indicates ruin mounds, suggesting that the Jews lived at ancient settlements in Babylonia.

## Events and Customs

Genealogical purity was essential for temple service. Those who could not document their ancestry (verses 59–62) were temporarily excluded from priestly duties until a priest could consult the "Urim and Thummim" (אוּרִ֥ים וּלְתֻמִּֽים, verse 63)—holy objects used to receive divine guidance. This reflects the strict attitude toward cultic purity in the post-exilic period.

The Nethinim (הַנְּתִינִים, verses 43–54) and "the descendants of Solomon's servants" (verses 55–57) were groups of lower status who performed manual tasks in the temple. Tradition links the Nethinim to the Gibeonites, who were assigned to serve the congregation and the altar (Joshua 9:27), while Solomon's servants likely descended from prisoners of war who were put to work during the construction of the temple.

## Key Terms

The expression "the province" (הַמְּדִינָ֗ה, verse 1) refers to Judah as an administrative unit within the Persian Empire. This shows that the Jews were now subjects in a world empire, not an independent kingdom.

"Drams" (דַּרְכְּמוֹנִים, verse 69) is a Persian/Greek coin name indicating that a monetary economy was established. This is one of the earliest biblical references to coins as a medium of exchange, in contrast to earlier weight-based systems.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The parallel list in Nehemiah 7 contains many of the same names with some variations in numbers, confirming that such administrative lists were preserved and copied. Persian administrative documents from the same period show that the empire kept detailed registers of population groups and their obligations. The total number of 42,360 persons (verse 64), plus servants and singers, suggests a returning population of around 50,000—a significant group requiring extensive organization and resources.
