# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 11

## People

The chapter mentions Dathan and Abiram (verse 6), sons of Eliab from the tribe of Reuben. These two, together with Korah, led a rebellion against Moses' leadership in the wilderness, as described in Numbers 16. The punishment came when the earth opened up and swallowed them along with their households. It is noteworthy that Korah himself is not mentioned here—possibly because the focus is on the civil rebellion (Dathan and Abiram) rather than the priestly one (Korah), or because Korah's descendants survived and continued to serve in the temple (Numbers 26:11).

## Places

The chapter describes the borders of the promised land (verse 24): from the wilderness in the south to Lebanon in the north, from the Euphrates River in the northeast to "the latter sea" (הַיָּם הָאַחֲרוֹן, the Mediterranean Sea) in the west. The term "latter" arises because the Israelites oriented themselves toward the east, so that west became "behind." This represents the ideal extent of Israel's territory, which historically was only approximated during the reigns of David and Solomon.

Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal (verses 29–30) are located near Shechem in the central highlands, today's Nablus. These two mountains form a natural amphitheater-like formation suitable for public assemblies. Gerizim (about 881 meters) lies south of the valley, while Ebal (about 940 meters) is north of it. "The oaks of Moreh" (אֵלוֹנֵי מֹרֶה) was a well-known landmark near Shechem, also mentioned in connection with Abraham (Genesis 12:6).

## Events and Customs

The chapter contrasts agricultural practices in Egypt with those in Canaan (verses 10–12). In Egypt, farming depended on artificial irrigation from the Nile through canal systems, where farmers "watered with their feet"—likely a reference to opening and closing irrigation channels with their feet or treading water wheels. Canaan, however, relied on rain, which theologically emphasized dependence on God.

Verses 18–20 repeat the command from Deuteronomy 6:6–9 to lay these words upon one's heart, bind them as signs on the hand and forehead, and write them on the doorposts. This was later literally interpreted as the basis for tefillin (prayer straps with scripture passages) and mezuzot (containers with scripture scrolls placed on doorframes).

## Key Terms

The terms יוֹרֶה (yoreh) and מַלְקוֹשׁ (malqosh) in verse 14 refer respectively to the early rain (October–November) and the latter rain (March–April). These two rainy seasons were crucial for Canaan's agricultural cycle. The early rain softened the soil for plowing and sowing, while the latter rain ensured the ripening of the grain harvest before the summer drought. When the rain failed, it meant famine, and this vulnerability is emphasized theologically: obedience brings rain; disobedience brings drought (verses 13–17).

The expression אֶרֶץ זָבַת חָלָב וּדְבָשׁ ("a land flowing with milk and honey," verse 9) describes the land's fertility. "Honey" (דְבָשׁ) likely refers primarily to fruit syrup from dates and figs, not bee honey, although both were present in the region.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at Tell Balata (ancient Shechem) confirm the site's significance as a religious and political center during the Bronze Age. The Samaritans, who still regard Mount Gerizim as holy, have preserved traditions connected to this place and continue to celebrate Passover there. Inscriptions and temples on Gerizim from the Persian and Hellenistic periods testify to the site's enduring religious importance.
