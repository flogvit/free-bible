# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 21

## People

**Philip the evangelist** (v. 8) was one of the seven men chosen to serve at the tables in Jerusalem (Acts 6:5), who later preached in Samaria and to the Ethiopian official. He had now settled in Caesarea with his four daughters, who prophesied. The fact that these daughters possessed prophetic gifts reflects the early church's practice where spiritual gifts were not limited by gender (cf. Joel 2:28-29).

**Agabus** (vv. 10–11) was a prophet from Judea who had previously predicted a famine during the reign of Emperor Claudius (Acts 11:28). His symbolic action with Paul's belt follows the pattern of Old Testament prophets like Isaiah and Ezekiel, who used dramatic acts to convey messages.

**Mnason of Cyprus** (v. 16) is described as an "early disciple" (ἀρχαίῳ μαθητῇ), suggesting he was among the first Christians, possibly from Pentecost or shortly thereafter. He served as host for Paul and his companions on their journey to Jerusalem.

**James** (v. 18) was Jesus' brother and leader of the church in Jerusalem. Here he appears as the primary authority among the elders, a role confirmed by other sources from that period. The "many thousands" (μυριάδες) of Jewish believers he mentions indicate a significant Jewish-Christian movement that still adhered to the Mosaic Law.

**Trophimus of Ephesus** (v. 29) was a Gentile Christian traveling companion of Paul. It was seeing him with Paul in the city that led to the false accusation that Paul had brought a Gentile into the temple.

## Places

**The travel route** (vv. 1–7) went from Miletus via Cos, Rhodes, and Patara, past Cyprus to Tyre, then on to Ptolemais and Caesarea. These were established sea routes in the Mediterranean. Tyre was an ancient Phoenician port city known for its trade. Ptolemais (modern-day Akko) was an important harbor north of Mount Carmel.

**Caesarea Maritima** (vv. 8–16) was the Roman capital of the province of Judea, built by Herod the Great with a magnificent harbor, theater, and palaces. The city had a mixed population of Jews and Gentiles. Archaeological excavations have revealed the city's impressive infrastructure, including a large amphitheater and aqueducts.

**The Antonia Fortress** (παρεμβολή, vv. 34–37) was the fortress located at the northwest corner of the temple complex, connected to the temple by staircases. The Roman garrison was stationed there to monitor the temple, especially during festivals. The staircases (ἀναβαθμοί) mentioned in verses 35 and 40 are archaeologically confirmed.

## Events and Customs

**The Nazirite ritual** (vv. 23–26), which Paul participated in, involved a seven-day purification period. The four men had a vow (εὐχή) upon them, likely a Nazirite vow as described in Numbers 6. Shaving the head marked the completion of the vow. That Paul paid for the sacrifices was an accepted pious act that wealthy Jews could perform on behalf of poorer fellow believers.

**The temple boundaries** (vv. 28–29) were strictly regulated. Non-Jews could only enter the "Court of the Gentiles." Inscriptions in Greek and Latin warned Gentiles not to proceed further under threat of death. One such inscription was discovered in 1871 and reads: "No foreigner may enter within the railing surrounding the sanctuary. Whoever is caught will be responsible for his own death that follows."

## Key Terms

**Ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου** (zelotai tou nomou, "zealots for the law", v. 20) describes Jewish Christians who were passionately committed to preserving the Mosaic Law. This should not be confused with the political Zealot party, although the term suggests similar intensity in religious devotion.

**Τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ** ("in the Hebrew language", v. 40) refers to the language Paul spoke to the crowd, most likely Aramaic, which was the everyday language of Judea, rather than classical Hebrew.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Egyptian rebel** (v. 38), whom the tribune (χιλίαρχος) mistook for Paul, is also mentioned by the historian Josephus. According to Josephus, this Egyptian gathered around 30,000 followers (Jewish War 2.261–263) or 4,000 (Antiquities 20.169–172) on the Mount of Olives around 54–58 AD and promised that the walls of Jerusalem would fall. The Roman procurator Felix crushed the rebellion, but the Egyptian himself escaped. That Luke and Josephus give different numbers may be due to different sources or different methods of counting.

**The Sicarii** (σικάριοι, v. 38) were a radical Jewish resistance group who carried daggers (sica) hidden under their cloaks and killed pro-Roman Jews in crowds. Their activity increased significantly in the decade before the Jewish War (66–70 AD), illustrating the tense political situation in Jerusalem when Paul arrived.
