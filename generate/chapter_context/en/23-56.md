# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 56

## People

**Eunuchs (סָרִיס, saris)**: The chapter gives special attention to eunuchs, men who were castrated. In the ancient world, eunuchs often served at royal courts, including those of Babylon, Persia, and Egypt. Castration was practiced to ensure loyalty and to protect royal harems. For Israelites, this was particularly painful because Deuteronomy 23:2 excluded eunuchs from the assembly of the Lord. The eunuch’s complaint, "I am a dry tree" (v. 3), reflects the sorrow of being unable to continue one’s lineage—a fundamental value in Israelite culture.

**Foreigners (בֶּן־הַנֵּכָר, ben-hannekar)**: The chapter also addresses non-Israelites who wished to join YHWH. During the exile and after the return, the question of foreigners’ status within the covenant community was highly relevant. Some texts, such as Ezra-Nehemiah, show a restrictive attitude, while Isaiah 56 presents an inclusive vision.

## Places

**My holy mountain and my house of prayer (v. 7)**: This refers to the temple in Jerusalem on Mount Zion. The phrase "a house of prayer for all peoples" points toward a universal vision where the temple is not only for Israel but for all nations. Jesus later quoted this text when he cleansed the temple (Mark 11:17).

## Events and Customs

**Sabbath observance**: The Sabbath is repeatedly emphasized in the chapter (vv. 2, 4, 6) as the central identity marker for belonging to God’s covenant people. During the exile, when the temple lay in ruins, the Sabbath and circumcision became the most visible signs of Jewish identity. That Sabbath observance here becomes an entry requirement also for eunuchs and foreigners shows a radical rethinking of who belongs to God’s people.

**Burnt offerings and sacrifices (v. 7)**: The text assumes a functioning temple system with sacrifices. This places the fulfillment of the vision either in the period after the temple was rebuilt (516 BCE) or as a future eschatological reality.

## Key Terms

**יָד וָשֵׁם (yad vashem) – "a memorial and a name"** (v. 5): This expression literally means "hand and name" and refers to a lasting memory or monument. For eunuchs who could not have children, God promised something better than physical descendants—an eternal name that will not be cut off. This expression is today known as the name of Israel’s Holocaust memorial museum, Yad Vashem.

**צֹפָיו עִוְרִים (tsofav ivrim) – "his watchmen are blind"** (v. 10): Watchmen (prophets and leaders) who were supposed to guard the people are strongly criticized. They are compared to mute dogs that cannot bark—a highly derogatory image in a culture where dogs were seen as unclean scavengers. This marks a sharp shift from the oracle of inclusion to harsh criticism of Israel’s leaders.
