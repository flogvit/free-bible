# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 42

## People

The psalm is attributed to **the Sons of Korah** (לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח), a Levitical family of singers who served in the temple.

## Places

The psalm mentions several geographical locations that reveal the situation of the psalmist. **The land of Jordan** (אֶרֶץ יַרְדֵּן) refers to the area around the Jordan River, far from Jerusalem. **Mount Hermon** (חֶרְמוֹנִים) is Israel's highest mountain range in the north, with peaks over 2,800 meters, known for its many summits (the plural form may indicate this). **Mount Mitzar** (הַר מִצְעָר, "the little mountain") is an unknown location, possibly a local height at the foot of Hermon. These places place the psalmist in the northern border region, far from the temple in Jerusalem—a geographical distance that intensifies the spiritual longing for God's presence.

## Events and Customs

Verse 5 describes a **pilgrimage procession** to the temple: the psalmist remembers how he "walked with the crowd" (אֶעֱבֹר בַּסָּךְ) and "went with them to God's house" (אֶדַּדֵּם עַד־בֵּית אֱלֹהִים) with joy, thanksgiving, and a festive throng. The expression הָמוֹן חוֹגֵג ("festive crowd") points to the collective, solemn celebration that characterized Israelite pilgrimage practice. For a Levite accustomed to serving in the temple, separation from these rituals was especially painful.

## Key Terms

The word **מַשְׂכִּיל** (maskil) in the heading is a technical term for a specific type of psalm, possibly an "instructional psalm" or a psalm requiring special musical skill.

**אַיָּל** (ajjal)—the deer or hind longing for flowing streams—is a powerful image in the dry Israeli landscape. During the summer months, many streams dry up, and the animals' desperate search for water became a recognizable symbol of spiritual thirst. Interestingly, the Hebrew text uses a feminine verb form (תַּעֲרֹג), which may indicate a female deer.

**תְּהוֹם אֶל תְּהוֹם** ("deep calls to deep") uses the word *tehom*, which in Genesis 1:2 refers to the chaotic primeval sea. Here it describes overwhelming masses of water—either literally (the waterfalls and meltwater of Hermon) or figuratively (the psalmist's feeling of drowning in suffering). **צִנּוֹרֶיךָ** (water channels/waterfalls) may refer to the natural waterfalls in the Hermon region where the Jordan River has its source.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The psalm's description of the waters at the Jordan and Hermon corresponds with the geographical reality of this area. At Banias (Caesarea Philippi) and Dan, the sources of the Jordan emerge from Mount Hermon with powerful water flows. Archaeological excavations at Tel Dan have uncovered a sacred area from Israelite times, and the region was known for its water springs. The poetic description of "deep calling to deep" and "waves and breakers" reflects the actual sounds of the waterfalls in this landscape, giving the spiritual metaphor a concrete, sensory grounding.
