# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Solomon 7

## Characters

The term "Shulamite" (הַשּׁוּלַמִּית, *ha-Shulammit*) in verse 1 is the only place in the Bible where this designation appears. The name has been interpreted in several ways: as a feminine form of "Solomon" (שְׁלֹמֹה), as a reference to a woman from the town of Shunem in the Jezreel Valley, or as a symbolic name meaning "the peaceful one" (from the root שׁלם, *shalom*). The location of Shunem in the fertile Jezreel Valley aligns with the rural imagery of the chapter.

## Places

The chapter mentions several specific geographical locations. **Heshbon** (חֶשְׁבּוֹן) in verse 5 was an ancient city east of the Jordan, known from the books of Moses as a Moabite capital. Archaeological excavations at Tell Hesban have uncovered water installations, although the most famous finds are from later periods. The city's association with water illuminates the comparison to "the pools of Heshbon." **Lebanon** and **Damascus** in the same verse represent majestic landmarks north of Israel—Lebanon known for its cedar trees and mountains, Damascus as one of the ancient world's most important cities. **Carmel** (כַּרְמֶל) in verse 6 was a fertile mountainous region by the Mediterranean Sea, famous for its lush vegetation. The comparison likely refers to either the majestic shape or the rich, dark color of the vegetation.

## Events and Customs

The expression "the dance of Mahanaim" (כִּמְחֹלַת הַמַּחֲנָיִם) in verse 1 has been the subject of much discussion. Mahanaim was a place in Transjordan associated with Jacob's encounter with angels (Gen 32:2). The dance may refer to a traditional wedding or festival dance performed by or before two groups (the name means "two camps"), or a cultic festive dance. The description of the woman's body from her feet upward (verses 2–7) follows a pattern called *wasf*—a detailed physical praise known from Egyptian and Mesopotamian love poetry, often performed during wedding celebrations.

## Key Terms

**Dudaim** (דּוּדָאִים) in verse 14 are mandrakes, a plant (*Mandragora officinarum*) that grows wild in Israel and ripens in the spring. In ancient times, the mandrake was believed to have aphrodisiac and fertility-enhancing properties due to its human-like root shape. The plant has a strong, sweet fragrance. This term also appears in the story of Rachel and Leah (Gen 30:14–16), where it is explicitly connected with fertility and sexuality.

**Argaman** (אַרְגָּמָן)—purple—in verse 6 was the most expensive dye in ancient times, extracted from snails along the Phoenician coast. The color purple was reserved for royalty and the very wealthy, and comparing the woman's hair to purple underscores her exalted status and beauty.
