# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 22

## People

**Balak son of Zippor** was king of Moab at this time. His name (בָּלָק) likely means "destroyer" or "empty," while his father's name, Zippor (צִפּוֹר), means "bird." The Moabites were descendants of Lot through his eldest daughter (Gen 19:37) and thus related to the Israelites. Balak’s fear of Israel was understandable given the Israelites’ recent victories over the Amorite kings Sihon and Og of Bashan.

**Balaam son of Beor** was a famous seer or diviner from Pethor by the Euphrates, in upper Mesopotamia. His reputation had reached all the way to Moab, testifying to his considerable standing in the ancient world. The Deir Alla inscription (see Chapter-Specific Findings) confirms that Balaam was a well-known figure in the region. The Hebrew name בִּלְעָם may mean "devourer of peoples" or "without people." His role as a seer places him among professional diviners common in Mesopotamia.

## Places

**Pethor** (פְּתוֹרָה), Balaam’s hometown, is usually identified with Pitru by the Euphrates in northern Mesopotamia, about 600 km north of Moab. Assyrian texts mention the city as Pitru. The phrase "by the river, in his own land" (v. 5) points to the Euphrates region, known as a center for divination.

**Arabah of Moab** (עַרְבוֹת מוֹאָב), "the plains of Moab," were the steppes on the east side of the Jordan, directly opposite Jericho. This was the last stopping place before entering Canaan. The **Arnon River** (v. 36) formed the northern boundary of Moab’s core territory.

**Bamoth-Baal** (בָּמוֹת בָּעַל, v. 41), "the high places of Baal," was a high place sanctuary dedicated to the Canaanite god Baal. High places were typical cult sites throughout the Semitic world, located on hills to be closer to the gods.

## Events and Customs

**Divination and cursing** were widespread in the ancient Near East. The term "qesamim" (קְסָמִים, v. 7) refers to divination tools or payment for divination services. In Mesopotamia, it was common to hire professional seers to pronounce curses over enemies before war. It was believed that effective curses could decide battles before they began. Balak’s confidence in Balaam’s abilities ("the one you bless is blessed, and the one you curse is cursed," v. 6) reflects this widespread belief.

**The alliance between Moab and Midian** (vv. 4, 7) is remarkable since these peoples were normally rivals. The Midianites were nomads whose main territory lay south and east of Moab. The common threat from Israel led to an unusual diplomatic cooperation.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew verb **אָרַר** (arar, "to curse," v. 6) refers to powerful, binding curses believed to have real effect in the spiritual realm. A related verb **קָבַב** (qavav, vv. 11, 17) is also used for cursing. The difference between these terms suggests nuances in the nature and intensity of the curse.

The word **שָׂטָן** (satan, vv. 22, 32) here means "adversary" and describes the angel’s role as one who blocks the way. This is not a reference to the devil, but to the function of obstruction or opposition.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The **Deir Alla inscription**, discovered in 1967 in the Jordan Valley, is among the most significant archaeological finds related to this chapter. Dated to approximately 840–760 BCE (several hundred years after the events described in the chapter), it mentions "Balaam son of Beor, the seer of the gods" and describes a night vision he received from the gods. The text confirms that Balaam was a historical figure with an established reputation as a seer in the Transjordan region, and that traditions about him remained known long after the time of the Pentateuch. The inscription also shows that Balaam traditions existed independently of the biblical account.
