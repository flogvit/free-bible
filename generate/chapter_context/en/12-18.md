# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 18

## People

**Hezekiah** (Hebrew: חִזְקִיָּהוּ, "Yahweh is my strength") reigned in Judah from approximately 715–686 B.C. and is portrayed as the most god-fearing king since David. His mother Abi (אֲבִי) was the daughter of Zechariah. Hezekiah's religious reforms included the removal of high places, sacred pillars, and Asherah poles—measures that were radical even for a pious king, since the high places had been tolerated by previous reforming kings.

**Hoshea son of Elah** (הוֹשֵׁעַ בֶּן־אֵלָה) was the last king of Israel and serves in this chapter as a chronological reference point. Hezekiah's third year of reign corresponded to Hoshea's seventh year (verse 9), and Samaria fell in Hezekiah's sixth year, which was Hoshea's ninth year (verse 10).

**Shalmaneser V** (שַׁלְמַנְאֶסֶר), king of Assyria from 726–722 B.C., began the siege of Samaria (verse 9). The city fell after a three-year siege, likely under his successor Sargon II.

**Sennacherib** (Akkadian: Sîn-ahhē-erība, "Sin has replaced the brothers") was king of Assyria from 705–681 B.C. He succeeded Sargon II and had to deal with revolts throughout his empire, including Hezekiah's revolt in Judah. His campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. is well documented in Assyrian sources.

**The Assyrian officers** in verse 17:
- **Tartan** (תַּרְתָּן) – the supreme commander of the Assyrian army
- **Rab-saris** (רַב־סָרִיס) – "chief eunuch," a high-ranking court official
- **Rabshakeh** (רַב־שָׁקֵה) – "chief cupbearer" or chief officer, who served as the military spokesperson and diplomat. His fluent Hebrew (יְהוּדִית) suggests he was either a deported Israelite serving in the Assyrian army or specially trained for western campaigns.

**Hezekiah's representatives** (verses 18, 26, 37):
- **Eliakim son of Hilkiah** (אֶלְיָקִים בֶּן־חִלְקִיָּהוּ) – "over the house" (chief steward), the highest official at the court
- **Shebna** (שֶׁבְנָה) – scribe (סֹּפֵר), responsible for royal correspondence
- **Joah son of Asaph** (יוֹאָח בֶּן־אָסָף) – recorder/herald (מַזְכִּיר), responsible for archives and public announcements

## Places

**Lachish** was the second most important fortified city in Judah after Jerusalem, strategically located in the Shephelah lowlands controlling the roads to Egypt. Sennacherib used Lachish as his headquarters during the campaign, which is underscored by Hezekiah sending his surrender there (verse 14). The city was besieged and captured before the Assyrians turned their attention to Jerusalem.

**Samaria** (שֹׁמְרוֹן), the capital of the northern kingdom, was besieged for three years and fell in 722 B.C. (verses 9–10). This marked the end of the northern kingdom of Israel.

**The places of exile** (verse 11): The Israelites were deported to **Halah** (חַלַח), **Habor by the river Gozan** (חָבוֹר נְהַר גּוֹזָן), and **the cities of the Medes** (עָרֵי מָדָי)—regions in the northeastern part of the Assyrian Empire.

**The conduit of the upper pool on the road to the Fuller's Field** (verse 17) was an important water supply point outside Jerusalem's walls. This is likely the same place where Isaiah had previously met King Ahaz (Isaiah 7:3), creating a deliberate literary connection between the different responses of father and son to Assyrian threats.

**The cities mentioned in Rabshakeh's speech** (verse 34): **Hamath**, **Arpad**, **Sepharvaim**, **Hena**, and **Ivvah**—cities/nations whose gods had been unable to save them from Assyria. These serve as a rhetorical argument that neither Yahweh could save Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

**The bronze serpent Nehushtan** (נְחֻשְׁתָּן) refers to the snake Moses made in the wilderness (Numbers 21:8–9). The name is a wordplay on the Hebrew words for bronze (נְחֹשֶׁת) and serpent (נָחָשׁ). The fact that Israelites had burned incense before it for generations shows how a legitimate religious object could develop into idolatry. Hezekiah's destruction of it was a bold break with established practice.

**The tribute of 300 talents of silver and 30 talents of gold** (verse 14) represented an enormous sum. Hezekiah had to empty both the temple and palace treasuries, and even remove the gold plating he himself had placed on the temple doors and doorposts—a humiliating reversal of his own religious investments.

## Key Terms

The expression **"make a treaty with me"** (עֲשׂוּ־אִתִּי בְרָכָה, verse 31) literally means "make blessing with me" and is a euphemism for submission. Rabshakeh's promise that each man shall eat from his own vine and fig tree and drink from his own well uses traditional Israelite images of peace and prosperity (cf. 1 Kings 4:25; Micah 4:4), demonstrating his rhetorical skill in appealing to the Judeans on their own cultural terms.

**Aramaic** (אֲרָמִית) was the international diplomatic language of the Assyrian Empire, while **Judean/Hebrew** (יְהוּדִית) was the common tongue. The request by Judah's representatives to speak Aramaic (verse 26) reveals their fear that Rabshakeh's propaganda would demoralize the people on the walls.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Sennacherib's prism inscriptions** (especially the Taylor Prism and Chicago Prism from around 689 B.C.) describe the campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. Sennacherib claims to have besieged 46 fortified cities in Judah and trapped Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem. Interestingly, he never claims to have captured Jerusalem itself, which aligns with the biblical account. The tribute he reports (800 talents of silver, 30 talents of gold) differs somewhat from the biblical figures, but the amount of gold matches.

**The Lachish Reliefs**, discovered in Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh and now in the British Museum, show in detail the Assyrian siege and capture of Lachish. The reliefs depict siege ramps, archers, deported captives, and Assyrian soldiers mirroring Judean prisoners. This confirms Lachish's importance in the campaign and provides visual documentation of the Assyrian warfare that the Judeans faced.
