# Historical and Cultural Context for Mark 2

## People

**Levi, son of Alphaeus** (v. 14) is the tax collector whom Jesus calls to follow him. He is traditionally identified with Matthew (cf. Matt 9:9). As a tax collector (*τελώνης*), he worked at the tax booth (*τελώνιον*) in Capernaum, likely collecting tolls on goods passing along the trade route Via Maris or fishing taxes by the Sea of Galilee. Tax collectors were despised by Jews because they collaborated with the Roman occupying power and often collected more than was required.

**Scribes** (*γραμματεῖς*) are mentioned several times in the chapter (vv. 6, 16). These were professional interpreters of the Mosaic Law and oral tradition. The expression "scribes of the Pharisees" (v. 16) indicates that there were scribes associated with various groups. They functioned as religious authorities and guardians of traditional law interpretation.

## Places

**Capernaum** (*Καφαρναούμ*, v. 1) was Jesus' base in Galilee. The houses in the town were typically built from local basalt stone with flat roofs made of branches, straw, and clay laid over wooden beams—explaining how men could "dig through" (*ἐξορύξαντες*) the roof to lower the paralyzed man (v. 4).

## Events and Customs

**Roofs and Architecture** (v. 4): Mark uses the verbs *ἀποστεγάζω* (to uncover a roof) and *ἐξορύσσω* (to dig out). This fits Galilean houses where roofs consisted of wooden beams covered with branches, reed mats, and a layer of hard clay. Such a roof could be broken through relatively easily, unlike the tiled roofs more common in Greco-Roman architecture.

**Meal Fellowship** (vv. 15–16): In antiquity, eating together (*συνανάκειμαι* – to recline at table with) was an act of deep social and religious significance. One entered into fellowship with those with whom one ate. That Jesus ate with "tax collectors and sinners" was scandalous because it signaled acceptance and equality. The Pharisees practiced strict table fellowship (*חברותא*) and avoided meals with those who did not observe purity laws.

**Fasting** (vv. 18–20): The Mosaic Law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29), but the Pharisees fasted voluntarily twice a week (Monday and Thursday, cf. Luke 18:12). John's disciples likely followed strict ascetic practices. Jesus' use of the wedding imagery (*νυμφίος/νυμφών*) alludes to messianic expectations where God's salvation was portrayed as a wedding feast.

**Sabbath Law** (vv. 23–28): Picking grain on the Sabbath was regarded by the Pharisees as "reaping"—one of the thirty-nine categories of forbidden work defined in later rabbinic tradition (Mishnah, Shabbat 7:2). The Pharisees developed detailed rules (*halakha*) to "build a fence around the law." Jesus refers to 1 Sam 21:1–6 where David ate the showbread—an event showing that human needs can take precedence over ritual regulations.

## Key Terms

**Son of Man** (*ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου*, vv. 10, 28) is a title with background in Dan 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" receives eternal dominion from God. The title carries overtones of both divine authority and messianic identity. Jesus uses the title to claim divine authority—both to forgive sins and over the Sabbath.

**Blasphemy** (*βλασφημέω*, v. 7) in Jewish understanding meant to insult God's honor or usurp God's rights. Forgiveness of sins belonged to God alone (Isa 43:25; Ps 103:3). The scribes' reaction shows that they understood Jesus' words as a divine claim.

**Showbread** (*ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως*, v. 26) were twelve loaves placed in the sanctuary every Sabbath (Lev 24:5–9). Only priests were allowed to eat them. Jesus' reference to Abiathar is disputed, since 1 Sam 21 mentions Ahimelech as priest. Several solutions have been proposed: that the expression *ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως* means "in Abiathar's [later] time as high priest," that Abiathar was present as son of Ahimelech, or that it reflects a textual tradition.
