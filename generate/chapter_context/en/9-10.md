# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 10

## People

**Saul son of Kish** appears in this chapter as a young man from the tribe of Benjamin who becomes Israel's first king. His physical appearance is remarkable – he is "head and shoulders taller" than all others (v. 23). His family, the Matrite clan (v. 21), is otherwise unknown in biblical literature, which underscores that Saul did not come from a prominent lineage. His uncle (דּוֹד, *dod*) who asks about the journey (vv. 14–16) highlights the importance of family structure in Israelite society. Notably, Saul hides the anointing and the matter of kingship from his uncle (v. 16).

**The band of prophets** (חֶבֶל נְבִיאִים, *ḥevel nevi'im*) represents a distinctive phenomenon in early Israelite religion. These prophetic groups came down from the high place with musical instruments (harp, tambourine, flute, and lyre) and prophesied together. They were clearly well-known figures in society, since people reacted with surprise that Saul had joined them.

## Places

**Rachel's tomb** (v. 2) is located "on the border of Benjamin at Zelah." The traditional location near Bethlehem (Gen 35:19) stands in tension with this text, which places it farther north in the territory of Benjamin. Archaeologists have proposed various sites near Ramah as possible candidates.

**Elon-Tabor** (v. 3) was likely a well-known oak tree that served as a landmark. Such sacred trees were common meeting places in ancient Israel. **Gibeah** or **Gibeah of God** (גִּבְעַת הָאֱלֹהִים, v. 5) is often identified with Tell el-Ful, about 5 km north of Jerusalem. The site had a Philistine garrison (נְצִבֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים), demonstrating Philistine control over Benjaminite territory.

**Mizpah** (v. 17) was an important religious and political gathering place for the tribes. The site is likely identical with Tell en-Nasbeh, where archaeologists have found extensive Israelite settlement remains from the Iron Age.

## Events and Customs

**The anointing** (v. 1) with oil from a small flask (פַּךְ, *pak*) was a ritual act marking divine selection. Anointing kings with oil was widespread throughout the ancient Near East. The Hebrew word for "anointed" (*mashiaḥ*) is the origin of "Messiah." The kiss (וַיִּשָּׁקֵהוּ) that followed the anointing was a sign of homage and recognition of the new leader.

**The casting of lots** (vv. 20–21) was an accepted method for determining God's will. The process involved bringing the tribes "forward" (וַיַּקְרֵב) one by one until the lot "caught" (וַיִּלָּכֵד) the right one. This practice is also known from Achan's sin (Josh 7) and the Jonathan episode (1 Sam 14).

**Sacrificial meals at the "high place"** (בָּמָה, *bamah*, vv. 5, 13) were legitimate worship sites before the temple was built in Jerusalem. These high places functioned as local sanctuaries where prophets gathered and religious activities took place.

## Key Terms and Theological Themes

The title **נָגִיד** (*nagid*, v. 1) is often translated "prince" or "leader" rather than "king" (*melek*). This term emphasizes that the king is appointed by God as leader over his "inheritance" (נַחֲלָה, *naḥalah*), not an independent ruler. The difference between these titles may reflect a theological tension surrounding kingship.

The expression **"the Spirit of God came upon him"** (וַתִּצְלַח עָלָיו רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים, v. 10) uses the verb צָלַח (*tsalaḥ*), meaning to "rush upon," "come powerfully over," or "fall upon." The same expression is used concerning Samson (Judg 14:6) and describes a sudden, powerful divine empowerment.

**"Is Saul also among the prophets?"** (vv. 11–12) became a proverb (מָשָׁל, *mashal*) in Israel. The question "Who is their father?" (v. 12) suggests that prophetic gift was not hereditary – anyone could receive God's Spirit regardless of background.

**"Sons of Belial"** (בְנֵי בְלִיַּעַל, v. 27) is a Hebrew expression for wicked or worthless people. Their contempt for Saul and their failure to bring an offering (מִנְחָה) foreshadow future opposition to the kingship. Saul's reaction – he remained silent (וַיְהִי כְּמַחֲרִישׁ) – shows his restraint in this early phase.
