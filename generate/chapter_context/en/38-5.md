# Historical and Cultural Context for Zechariah 5

## Places

### Shinar (שִׁנְעָר)

Shinar is the Hebrew name for Babylonia, southern Mesopotamia (v. 11). This name carries deep symbolic meaning in biblical tradition. The first time Shinar is mentioned is in connection with the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:2) and as part of Nimrod’s kingdom (Genesis 10:10). For Zechariah’s audience, who had recently returned from the Babylonian exile, this place name would immediately evoke memories of captivity. That wickedness ("rishah") is being transported *to* Babylon represents a dramatic reversal: what once came from Babylon to Judah (idolatry and ungodliness) is now sent back to its place of origin.

## Key Terms

### The Flying Scroll (מְגִלָּה עָפָה)

The scroll’s dimensions—20 cubits long and 10 cubits wide (about 9×4.5 meters)—exactly match the measurements of the porch of Solomon’s temple (1 Kings 6:3). This parallel suggests that judgment proceeds from the sanctuary. The scroll contains a curse (אָלָה) against thieves and those who swear falsely—sins against fellow humans (the second tablet) and against God (the first tablet) in the Ten Commandments.

### The Ephah Measure (אֵיפָה)

The ephah was a standard dry measure in Israel, used to measure grain and flour—approximately 22 liters. As a commercial measure, the ephah was often associated with justice or injustice in business transactions. The prophets repeatedly condemned manipulated measures (Amos 8:5; Micah 6:10). In the vision, the ephah likely represents the people’s "injustice"—their sin is now "filled up" and ready to be removed.

### The Lead Cover (כִּכַּר עֹפֶרֶת)

Lead was the heaviest metal known in antiquity and symbolized something that should be permanently held down. A "talent of lead" weighed about 30–35 kg. By casting the lead cover over the opening of the ephah (v. 8), wickedness is sealed so it cannot escape during transport to Shinar.

## Events and Customs

### Theft and False Oaths

The two sins condemned by the flying scroll—theft and swearing falsely in God’s name—represent the core of covenant breaking. In the restored community after the exile, economic honesty and religious integrity were crucial to avoiding further judgment. Swearing falsely in the Lord’s name was especially serious because it profaned God’s character and undermined the entire legal system, which was built upon oaths.

### The Curse Entering the House

The image of the curse "lodging" (וְלָנֶה) in the sinner’s house and consuming both wood and stone (v. 4) may reflect familiar Old Testament concepts regarding leprosy in houses (Leviticus 14:33–45), where affected houses had to be completely demolished.
