## People

The chapter mentions three figures as examples of righteousness: Noah, Daniel (or Dan'el), and Job (vv. 14, 20). These three represent different eras and backgrounds—Noah from primeval history, Job as a non-Israelite from the land of Uz, and Daniel. The Daniel mentioned here may be the legendary wise figure Dan'el known from Ugaritic texts from the 13th century BCE, rather than the biblical Daniel (who was a contemporary of Ezekiel). In the Ugaritic Aqhat epic, Dan'el is described as a righteous judge who defends widows and orphans. The spelling in the Hebrew text shows both the shorter form דנאל (ketiv) and the longer form דָּנִיֵּאל (qere), which has contributed to discussion about his identity. By choosing three non-Israelite or pre-Israelite righteous figures, Ezekiel emphasizes the universal character of the message.

## Events and Customs

The opening of the chapter describes a typical prophetic consultation in which Israel's elders come to the prophet to "seek the Lord" (vv. 1, 7). This was an established practice where leaders sought out prophets to receive divine guidance. However, Ezekiel reveals that these elders simultaneously "have taken their idols into their hearts" (v. 3). The expression describes an inner devotion to other gods while outwardly maintaining worship of Yahweh—a form of religious duplicity that was widespread among the exiles. The practice of consulting multiple deities at once was common in the ancient Near East, where people sought answers from various sources to ensure their safety.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word "gillulim" (גִּלּוּלֵיהֶם, "idols") appears several times in this chapter (vv. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). The term is a derogatory expression likely derived from "gelal" (dung/excrement) and is used almost exclusively by Ezekiel to emphasize the worthlessness and uncleanness of idols. The phrase "mikshol avon" (מִכְשׁוֹל עֲוֺנָם, "stumbling block of iniquity") is distinctive to Ezekiel and describes how idolatry becomes an obstacle that causes the people to fall.

The four judgments—sword, famine, wild beasts, and pestilence (v. 21)—constitute a traditional listing of covenant curses, known from Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28. In the Babylonian context, these four plagues were also well-known as instruments of divine punishment, making the message understandable to the exiles in their new surroundings.
