# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Corinthians 7

## Events and Customs

Chapter 7 opens with the phrase "Περὶ δὲ ὧν ἐγράψατε" ("now concerning what you wrote"), indicating that Paul here begins to answer specific questions from the church's letter to him. This marks a shift in the structure of the letter. The congregation had clearly asked about marriage, celibacy, and sexuality—topics that were controversial at the intersection of Jewish, Greek, and Christian thought.

In the Greco-Roman world, tensions existed between different views on marriage. Some philosophical schools, particularly certain Stoics and Cynics, emphasized celibacy as ideal for those wishing to devote themselves to philosophy and wisdom. At the same time, marriage was a social duty in Roman culture, and Emperor Augustus had enacted laws that punished unmarried people and rewarded families with children. In Corinth's religious diversity, there were also cults where ritual abstinence or temple prostitution was practiced. This complex background explains why the church needed guidance.

## Key Terms

The expression **"γυναικὸς μὴ ἅπτεσθαι"** (v. 1, "not to touch a woman") was likely a quotation from the Corinthians' own letter, which Paul then nuances. The verb "ἅπτεσθαι" was a common euphemism for sexual intercourse in Greek. Some in the church may have adopted an ascetic position where all sexuality, even within marriage, was viewed as spiritually inferior.

The term **"ὀφειλή"** (v. 3, "due" or "what is owed") refers to marital relations. This was unusual in antiquity: Paul presents mutual obligation between husband and wife, breaking with the patriarchal norm where a woman was considered her husband's property. Verse 4 elaborates on this with "ἐξουσιάζει" ("has authority over"), emphasizing that both spouses have equal rights over each other's bodies.

The word **"ἄγαμος"** (vv. 8, 11, 32, 34) refers generally to unmarried people, but in verse 11 it is used specifically for a separated woman. This differs from **"παρθένος"** (virgin), which denotes an unmarried woman who has never been married.

The expression **"ἐνεστῶσαν ἀνάγκην"** (v. 26, "the present crisis") and **"ὁ καιρὸς συνεσταλμένος"** (v. 29, "the time is short/limited") reflect an eschatological awareness. Paul seems to expect Christ's imminent return, which influences his practical advice.

## Specific Cultural Conditions

Verses 17–24 address circumcision and slavery as examples of a person's "calling" (κλῆσις). The procedure **"ἐπισπάσθω"** (v. 18) refers to epispasm—a surgical or mechanical method to restore the foreskin. This practice is documented in ancient medical texts (including by Celsus) and was used by Jews who wished to conceal their circumcision in order to participate in Greek gymnasia or avoid discrimination.

The discussion about **slaves** (δοῦλος, vv. 21–23) reflects Corinth's reality where a significant portion of the population were slaves or freedmen. The expression "ἀπελεύθερος κυρίου" (freedman of the Lord) uses terminology from Roman manumission practices, where a freed slave remained in a client-patron relationship with their former owner.

## Marriage and Divorce

Paul's guidance on divorce (vv. 10–16) must be understood against the background of different legal systems. In Roman law, both husband and wife could initiate divorce relatively easily. In Jewish law, divorce (גט, "get") was primarily a man's right. Paul quotes Jesus' teaching (vv. 10–11) but also gives his own guidance for "mixed" marriages between believers and non-believers (vv. 12–16)—a situation Jesus had not directly addressed, which arose when one spouse became Christian after marriage.

The term **"ἡγίασται"** (v. 14, "has been sanctified") reflects Jewish thinking about purity and impurity, but Paul applies it in a new way: the believer's presence sanctifies the household rather than the unbeliever's impurity defiling the family.
