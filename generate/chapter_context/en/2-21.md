# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 21

## Events and Customs

Exodus 21 opens with the words וְאֵלֶּה הַמִּשְׁפָּטִים ("and these are the ordinances"), which directly link these laws to the preceding Ten Commandments. The chapter contains what is called *mishpatim* (מִשְׁפָּטִים) – "ordinances" or "judgments" – forming the first part of the Book of the Covenant. These laws regulate legal matters in daily life and represent one of the oldest law codes in the Bible. The chapter covers three main areas: slave legislation (vv. 2–11), laws concerning violence, murder, and bodily injury (vv. 12–27), and liability for damage caused by livestock – especially the ox (שׁוֹר) – or negligence (vv. 28–37).

The slavery described here was primarily debt slavery – a way to handle financial ruin in a time without bankruptcy laws or social safety nets. A person unable to pay debts could sell their labor for a limited period. The six-year service period (שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים) with release in the seventh year (וּבַשְּׁבִעִת, v. 2) reflects the Sabbath principle that permeates Israelite law. The ritual of piercing the ear at the door or doorpost (v. 6) marked voluntary lifelong service and was a public, legally binding act performed "before God" – likely at a sanctuary or in the presence of judges.

Female slaves (אָמָה) have special provisions in vv. 7–11. If a woman is sold as a female slave, she shall not be released in the same way as male slaves. If she is designated (יְעָדָהּ) for her master himself, or if he gives her to his son, she retains rights to food, clothing, and marital obligations (שְׁאֵרָהּ כְּסוּתָהּ וְעֹנָתָהּ, v. 10). These provisions gave women legal protection in a vulnerable situation.

The laws concerning murder (vv. 12–14) make an important distinction between intentional murder with premeditation (בְעָרְמָה, "with cunning", v. 14) and unintentional killing. For the latter, a place of refuge (מָקוֹם אֲשֶׁר יָנוּס שָׁמָּה, v. 13) is established where the guilty party may seek protection.

## Key Terms

The expression **עֶבֶד עִבְרִי** (*eved ivri*, "Hebrew slave/servant", v. 2) has been the subject of extensive discussion. While traditionally understood as an Israelite, some scholars have linked it to *habiru/apiru* – a social class of marginalized people known from texts throughout the ancient Near East. Regardless of origin, the law protects this group with a time limit on service.

The term **לַֽחָפְשִׁי** (*lachofshi*, "as free", v. 2) describes the status of a released person. This is a legal technical term indicating full restoration of civil rights. The expression **אֶל־הָאֱלֹהִים** (v. 6, "to God/gods") can mean either "to the sanctuary" or "to the judges", since Hebrew *elohim* can refer to both God and human authorities acting on His behalf.

The famous principle **עַיִן תַּחַת עַיִן** (*ayin tachat ayin*, "eye for eye", v. 24) is called *lex talionis* – the law of retaliation. In practice, this was probably never interpreted literally in Israelite law but functioned as a principle of proportional compensation. The context of this chapter, surrounded by provisions for monetary restitution in silver, supports that it concerns fair economic compensation rather than physical retribution.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Laws of Hammurabi from Babylon (ca. 1792–1750 BC) contain striking parallels to the laws in Exodus 21. For example, Hammurabi §196–197 literally sets "eye for eye", while §250–252 deals with liability for goring oxen in almost identical fashion to Exodus 21:28–32. The Babylonian law sets the compensation for a slave killed by an ox at 20 shekels of silver, while the biblical law sets it at 30 shekels (שְׁלֹשִׁים שְׁקָלִים, v. 32) – a remarkable similarity suggesting a common legal tradition in the ancient Near East.

At the same time, there are significant differences: The Laws of Hammurabi operate with a class-based system where punishment varies according to the victim's social status (free man, *mushkenum*, or slave). The biblical laws are more egalitarian – for example, Exodus 21:20–21 and 26–27 give slaves legal protection against abuse without parallel in other ancient Near Eastern legislation. That a slave is automatically freed if the master injures their eye or knocks out a tooth represents a radical limitation of the owner's power over their slave.
