# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 46

## Events and Customs

The chapter provides detailed regulations for Sabbath and New Moon worship services in the future temple. The Sabbath (שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*) and the day of the new moon (יוֹם הַחֹדֶשׁ, *yom hachodesh*) were the only days when the east gate to the inner court was to be opened (v. 1), emphasizing their holy character in contrast to the six regular workdays. The day of the new moon marked the beginning of each month in the lunar calendar and was an important festival with special offerings (Num 28:11-15).

The movement rules for the people during the festivals (v. 9)—that those entering from the north were to go out toward the south, and vice versa—had practical significance for ensuring orderly crowds, but also communicated theologically that one could not leave the Lord's presence in the same way one entered. This unidirectional movement symbolized that meeting with God transforms a person.

## Key Terms

The term "prince" (הַנָּשִׂיא, *hannasi*) in this chapter refers to a future ruler distinct from the kings of Judah and Israel. While earlier kings often abused their power, Ezekiel's vision gives the prince a limited, primarily liturgical role—he offers sacrifices on behalf of the people but does not perform the priestly service himself. The title *nasi* was deliberately chosen over *melek* (king), possibly to avoid associations with the fallen monarchy.

"The year of release" (עַד־שְׁנַת הַדְּרוֹר, *ad shenat haddror* - "until the year of release", v. 17) refers to the Jubilee year described in Leviticus 25, when property was to be returned to the original families. The word *dror* means "freedom" or "release" and was used concerning the freeing of slaves and the return of land. The provision that the prince's gifts to servants lasted only until this year protected the tribes' inheritance from permanent alienation.

## Places

The chapter describes several architectural elements in the temple complex. The four cooking rooms (בֵּית הַמְבַשְּׁלִים, *bet hambashelim*) in the corners of the court (vv. 21-24) were intended for preparing the people's peace offerings. The dimensions are given as 40 by 30 (v. 22), indicating substantial structures. That the priests' kitchen was separated from that of the people (vv. 19-20) reflected the degrees of holiness in the temple—the most holy offerings (אָשָׁם, *asham* - guilt offering and חַטָּאת, *chattat* - sin offering, v. 20) had to be prepared in a holy area so as not to "consecrate" the people through accidental contact, while the peace offering that the people could eat was prepared in the outer court.
