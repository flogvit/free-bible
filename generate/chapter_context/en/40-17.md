# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 17

## People

**Moses and Elijah** appear together with Jesus at the Transfiguration on the mountain. In Jewish tradition, these two figures represented respectively the Law (Torah) and the Prophets—the two main divisions of the Hebrew Bible. Moses was the supreme lawgiver, while Elijah was regarded as the foremost representative of the prophets. Jewish tradition expected Elijah to return before the Messiah (based on Malachi 4:5), which explains the disciples' question in verses 10–13.

**Peter, James, and John** formed the inner circle among the twelve disciples. These three were also witnesses to other central events, such as the raising of Jairus's daughter and Jesus's prayer struggle in Gethsemane. Their presence at the Transfiguration underscores the significance of this event.

## Places

**The high mountain** (v. 1) has traditionally been identified with Mount Tabor in Galilee, although Mount Hermon north of Caesarea Philippi has also been suggested based on the geographical context from chapter 16. The tradition linking it to Tabor dates back at least to the third or fourth century, but during Jesus's time the mountain was crowned by a fortress, making it less likely as an isolated place for prayer. Hermon (2,814 meters) is closer to Caesarea Philippi and fits better with the timing "six days later" after Peter's confession.

**Capernaum** (v. 24) was Jesus's main base in Galilee and Peter's hometown. Archaeological excavations have revealed a prosperous fishing village with a synagogue and private homes. Archaeological finds, including what is traditionally called "Peter's house," testify to early Christian activity in the city. The town also had a tax station, which explains why the temple tax was collected there.

## Events and Customs

**The Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot)** may lie behind Peter's suggestion to build three shelters or tents (σκηνάς, v. 4). During this feast, Jews lived in temporary leafy huts in remembrance of the wilderness wanderings. Peter's impulsive proposal may reflect an expectation that the messianic age had broken in, since the Feast of Tabernacles was associated with God's final salvation.

**The temple tax** (τὰ δίδραχμα, v. 24) was an annual levy of two drachmas (equivalent to half a shekel) that every adult Jewish man was required to pay to the temple in Jerusalem. This tax was based on Exodus 30:11–16 and was used to fund the daily sacrifices and temple maintenance. The levy was typically collected in the month of Adar (February–March), before Passover. The collectors mentioned in the text were local representatives, not temple authorities.

## Key Terms

**Μετεμορφώθη** (metemorfōthē, "was transformed," v. 2) describes an outward transformation that reveals an inner reality. The word is used in the Septuagint regarding Moses's shining face after meeting with God (Exodus 34:29–35). The parallel to Moses is clear, and Matthew presents Jesus's transfiguration as a revelation of his divine glory.

**Σεληνιάζεται** (selēniazetai, "is moonstruck," v. 15) comes from the Greek word for moon (σελήνη). In antiquity, certain seizure-like conditions were popularly associated with the phases of the moon, hence the term "moonstruck." Matthew shows in this case that the condition had a demonic cause (v. 18), although elsewhere the gospel mentions "moonstruck" people as a separate category (4:24).

**Στατήρ** (statēr, v. 27) was a Greek silver coin worth four drachmas—exactly the amount needed to pay the temple tax for two persons (Jesus and Peter). The coin corresponded to the Jewish shekel and was a common trade currency in the region.
