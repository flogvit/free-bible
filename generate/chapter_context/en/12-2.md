# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 2

## People

**Elijah** (אֵלִיָּהוּ, "My God is YHWH") concludes his earthly ministry in this chapter. His being taken up without dying places him in a unique category alongside Enoch (Gen 5:24). In later Jewish tradition, Elijah was expected to return before the Messiah (Mal 4:5), which explains the enduring interest in his fate.

**Elisha** (אֱלִישָׁע, "My God is salvation") was the son of Shaphat from Abel-meholah and was called to prophetic service in 1 Kings 19:19–21. His request for a "double portion" of Elijah's spirit (פִּי־שְׁנַיִם, verse 9) refers to the birthright described in Deut 21:17, where the firstborn inherited twice as much as other sons. Elisha is thus asking to be recognized as Elijah's primary spiritual heir.

**The Sons of the Prophets** (בְנֵי־הַנְּבִיאִים) were organized groups of prophets who lived in community at holy sites such as Bethel and Jericho. These prophetic communities appear to have existed since the time of Samuel (1 Sam 10:5; 19:20) and served as training centers for prophetic tradition.

## Places

**Gilgal** (הַגִּלְגָּל) is mentioned as the starting point of the journey. This was likely Gilgal in the hill country near Bethel, not Gilgal by the Jordan. The place was an important cultic center in Israel.

**Bethel** (בֵּית־אֵל, "House of God") was an ancient holy site connected with the patriarchs (Gen 28:19), but it had also become a center for calf worship under Jeroboam I (1 Kings 12:28–29). The fact that there was a school of prophets there shows that YHWH worship continued alongside idolatry. Archaeological excavations at Beitin (traditionally identified with Bethel) have revealed continuous settlement throughout the Iron Age.

**Jericho** (יְרִיחוֹ) was strategically located on the western side of the Jordan Valley. The city was known for its water spring (Ain es-Sultan), which, according to verses 19–22, had problems with water quality before Elisha's miracle. This spring still exists today and supplies modern Jericho with water.

**The Jordan** (הַיַּרְדֵּן) was the boundary river between the western and eastern lands. The parting of the Jordan by Elijah's cloak (verse 8) and later Elisha's repetition of it (verse 14) creates a deliberate parallel to Joshua's crossing (Josh 3) and Moses' parting of the Red Sea.

**Mount Carmel** (הַר הַכַּרְמֶל), where Elisha goes at the end (verse 25), was the site of Elijah's confrontation with the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18). Elisha's visit there marks the continuity of his ministry.

## Events and Customs

**The Cloak as a Symbol of Power**: Elijah's cloak (אַדֶּרֶת) was more than just a garment—it symbolized prophetic authority. The fact that Elisha picks up the cloak that fell from Elijah (verse 13) publicly confirms that he has inherited the prophetic office. This cloak was likely made of animal hair or wool and was characteristic of prophets (cf. Zech 13:4).

**Tearing Clothes** (verse 12): Elisha's action of tearing his own clothes in two pieces was a conventional expression of mourning in ancient Israel (Gen 37:34; Job 1:20). By tearing them "in two" (לִשְׁנַיִם קְרָעִים), he shows complete grief, while at the same time symbolically putting aside his old life before taking up Elijah's cloak.

**The Healing of the Water** (verses 19–22): The use of salt in a new bowl to purify the water combines practical and symbolic elements. Salt was associated with purity and covenants (Lev 2:13; Num 18:19). The new bowl ensured ritual purity, while the action demonstrated that it was YHWH, not magical techniques, who performed the healing.

## Key Terms

**"Taken up in a whirlwind"** (בַּסְעָרָה, verses 1, 11): The word סְעָרָה describes a powerful whirlwind or storm and is often associated with theophanies—divine manifestations (cf. Job 38:1; Ezek 1:4). The storm represents God's mighty presence.

**"Chariots of fire and horses of fire"** (רֶכֶב־אֵשׁ וְסוּסֵי אֵשׁ): This heavenly-military imagery reflects the concept of God's heavenly army. Elisha's exclamation "The chariots and horsemen of Israel!" (verse 12) acknowledges that Elijah was Israel's true defense—more important than military power.

**"Baldhead"** (קֵרֵחַ, verse 23): The mocking term used by the young men could be more than just ridicule of physical appearance. A bald head could be associated with leprosy or shame, and the taunt "Go up!" may have been a derisive reference to Elijah's ascension—an rejection of the prophet's authority and message.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The water spring at Jericho (Ain es-Sultan/Elisha's Spring) has been in continuous use for thousands of years and currently produces around 1,000 liters of water per minute. The spring still supplies the oasis at Jericho, and its productivity aligns with the biblical account that the water was "healed" permanently (verse 22: "to this day"). Although there is no direct archaeological evidence for the miracle itself, the existence and significance of the spring confirm the geographical reality of the narrative.
