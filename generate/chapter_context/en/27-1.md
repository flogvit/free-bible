## People

**Nebuchadnezzar** (נְבוּכַדְנֶאצַּר) was king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire from approximately 605–562 BC. His name means "Nebo, protect my boundary" or "Nebo, protect the heir."

**Jehoiakim** (יְהוֹיָקִים) was king of Judah from about 609–598 BC, installed by Pharaoh Neco II. He was originally an Egyptian vassal but was forced to submit to Babylon after Nebuchadnezzar’s victory at Carchemish in 605 BC.

**Ashpenaz** (אַשְׁפְּנַז), "chief of the eunuchs," held a title (רַב סָרִיסִים) known from Assyrian and Babylonian texts. The title *rab saris* denotes a high-ranking court official, not necessarily a physical eunuch.

**The four Jewish youths**—Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah—all bear Hebrew names containing references to God: Daniel (דָּנִיֵּאל, "God is my judge"), Hananiah (חֲנַנְיָה, "Yahweh is gracious"), Mishael (מִישָׁאֵל, "Who is like God?"), and Azariah (עֲזַרְיָה, "Yahweh helps").

## Places

**Shinar** (שִׁנְעָר) is an archaic name for Babylonia, used as early as Genesis 11 to refer to the region of Babel. The use of this ancient name underscores the connection to the Tower of Babel and Babylon’s role as a symbol of resistance against God.

**The king’s palace in Babylon**—Archaeological excavations have uncovered administrative rooms where young men from conquered nations were trained.

## Events and Customs

The practice of taking young men of royal lineage from conquered peoples for court training is well documented in the ancient Near East. This served several purposes: to secure loyalty from conquered elites, to obtain competent administrators, and to hold potential rebel leaders as hostages. Assyrian and Babylonian texts describe similar programs.

**The name change** from Hebrew to Babylonian names was a deliberate assimilation strategy. The new names contained references to Babylonian deities: Belteshazzar (בֵּלְטְשַׁאצַּר, "Bel, protect his life"), Shadrach (שַׁדְרַךְ, possibly "command of Aku"), Meshach (מֵישַׁךְ, uncertain meaning), and Abednego (עֲבֵד נְגוֹ, possibly "servant of Nebo"). This represented an attempt to replace the religious identity of the Jewish youths.

## Key Terms

**פַּתְבַּג** (*patbag*)—this Persian loanword for "delicacy" or "king’s portion" suggests that the text was written or edited under Persian influence. The food from the king’s table was problematic because it was likely sacrificed to Babylonian gods and not prepared according to Jewish dietary laws.

**זֵרֹעִים / זֵרְעֹנִים** (*zero'im / zer'onim*)—"vegetables" or "what grows from seed" (vv. 12, 16)—represented food that was ritually clean and not associated with idol worship.

**יִתְגָּאַל** (*jitga'al*)—"to make oneself unclean"—shows that Daniel’s resistance concerned ritual purity and faithfulness to the laws of the Torah, not personal preference.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Babylonian administrative texts** from Nebuchadnezzar’s time, found in the palace archives, mention rations distributed to captives and hostages from various nations, including Judah. This confirms the biblical portrayal of Judean royalty and nobility in Babylonian captivity.
