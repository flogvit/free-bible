# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 3

## People

The superscription of the psalm (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד בְּבָרְחוֹ מִפְּנֵי אַבְשָׁלוֹם בְּנוֹ) links it directly to David and his flight from his son Absalom. This event is described in 2 Samuel 15–18, where Absalom carried out a coup against his father. For several years, Absalom had systematically undermined the people's trust in David by stationing himself at the city gate and suggesting that the king did not treat people fairly (2 Sam 15:1–6). The rebellion gained such widespread support that David was forced to flee Jerusalem with only a small group of loyal followers. The fact that his enemy was his own son makes this psalm an expression of deep personal pain combined with political crisis.

## Places

The phrase "his holy mountain" (הַר קָדְשׁוֹ, *har qodsho*) in verse 5 refers to Zion, the temple hill in Jerusalem. This is especially poignant given the situation: David had just left Jerusalem and Zion, yet he still expresses confidence that God hears him from there. In Israelite thought, Zion was the place where God chose to let His name dwell, and the idea that God "answers from his holy mountain" emphasizes that distance from the sanctuary does not break the connection between the one praying and God.

## Key Terms and Expressions

The word **סֶלָה** (*sela*), which appears three times in this short psalm (verses 3, 5, and 9), is one of the most mysterious words in the Psalms. Its meaning is uncertain, but it may indicate a musical pause, an interlude, an invitation to reflect, or a signal to raise the voice. The frequent use of this word in this psalm suggests that it was intended for liturgical performance.

The expression **מָגֵן בַּעֲדִי** (*magen ba'adi*, "shield around me") in verse 4 uses imagery from warfare. The shield was the soldier's primary defensive weapon, and in this context, where David is surrounded by enemies (verse 7), the metaphor takes on concrete meaning: God surrounds him with protection from all sides.

The phrase **וּמֵרִים רֹאשִׁי** (*umerim roshi*, "the one who lifts my head") in verse 4 carries cultural resonance. A lowered head signaled shame, defeat, or sorrow in ancient culture, while a lifted head expressed honor and dignity. For David, who had left Jerusalem in shame with his head covered (2 Sam 15:30), this image represents hope for the restoration of honor.
