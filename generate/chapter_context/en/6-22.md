## People

**Phinehas, son of Eleazar the priest**, plays a central role as the leader of the delegation sent to the Transjordan tribes. Phinehas was known for his zeal for the Lord, especially demonstrated during the Peor incident (Numbers 25:7-13), where he stopped a plague by killing an Israelite man and a Midianite woman. For this, he received the promise of "a covenant of an everlasting priesthood" (Numbers 25:12-13). It is significant that he specifically was sent in this situation—his presence underscores the seriousness of the accusation and his authority to judge religious matters. As the son of the high priest Eleazar, he represented the highest religious authority in Israel after his father.

**The ten leaders** who accompany Phinehas represent the western tribes, one from each tribe. This reflects Israel's tribe-based leadership structure where "heads of their fathers' houses" (רֹאשׁ בֵּית־אֲבוֹתָם) had authority to speak on behalf of their tribes in important matters.

**Achan** (עָכָן בֶּן־זֶרַח) is mentioned in verse 20 as a warning precedent. His treachery (ma'al) regarding the devoted things (Joshua 7) brought God's wrath upon all Israel, not just upon himself. This example is used to emphasize the danger that one person's sin can strike the entire people.

## Places

**Gilead** (הַגִּלְעָד) designates the mountainous region east of the Jordan that was allotted to Reuben, Gad, and half the tribe of Manasseh. The area extends from the Arnon River in the south to Bashan in the north and was known for its pastures and balsam trees. The name's similarity to "Gal-Ed" (witness heap) from Genesis 31:47 provides an interesting resonance with the chapter's theme of the altar as a "witness."

**Gelilot by the Jordan** (גְּלִילוֹת הַיַּרְדֵּן)—the place where the altar was built—is geographically disputed. The word gelilot can mean "districts" or "circles/stone circles." Verse 10 places it "in the land of Canaan," while verse 11 describes it as "opposite the land of Canaan... on the Israelite side" (אֶל־עֵ֖בֶר בְּנֵ֥י יִשְׂרָאֵֽל), making its exact location a matter of debate. Regardless of precise localization, the altar was visible and monumental—"a great altar" (מִזְבֵּ֥חַ גָּד֖וֹל).

**Shiloh** was at this time Israel's religious center where the tabernacle stood. From here the assembly gathered for war (v. 12), and this underscores the contrast between the one legitimate place of sacrifice and the seemingly rival altar.

## Events and Customs

**The Altar as a Witness** (עֵד, ed) represents a known custom in the ancient Near East where monuments, stone heaps, or structures were erected as "witnesses" to agreements or boundaries. Similar practices are seen in Genesis 31:44-52, where Jacob and Laban raise a stone heap called Gal-Ed (גַּלְעֵד, "witness heap") as a witness to their covenant—an interesting parallel given that the Transjordan tribes' land is named Gilead. The Transjordan tribes' altar was explicitly not intended for sacrifices, but as a visual memorial for future generations—a "tavnit" (תַּבְנִית, "likeness/copy") of the Lord's altar.

**Fear of Religious Centralization** shown in the chapter reflects the tension between tribal autonomy and national religious unity. It was strictly forbidden to build alternative altars for sacrifice (Deuteronomy 12:5-14), and the assembly reacted by preparing for war—the prescribed response to idolatry in an Israelite city (Deuteronomy 13:13-16).

## Key Terms

**Ma'al** (מַעַל)—"treachery" or "breach of faith"—is a key word that appears several times in the chapter (vv. 16, 20, 22, 31). The term denotes a serious offense against the Lord, often linked to sanctuary violations or covenant breaches. An accusation of ma'al was extremely serious and could result in capital punishment for the entire community.

**The Sin of Peor** (עֲוֺן פְּעוֹר, v. 17) refers to the incident in Numbers 25 where Israelites participated in idolatry and sexual immorality with Moabite women at Baal-Peor. This resulted in a plague that killed 24,000 (Numbers 25:9). The reference serves as a warning about collective punishment—one man's sin can strike the entire people.

**"El, Elohim, YHWH"** (אֵל אֱלֹהִים יְהוָה)—the triple divine designation in verse 22 is unusual and expresses a solemn oath. The repetition emphasizes that the Transjordan tribes are invoking God as witness with maximum seriousness, a practice known from other oath formulas in ancient Israel.
