## People

**Ittai the Gittite** (אִתַּי הַגִּתִּי) is mentioned here as one of three commanders to whom David entrusted his forces. The fact that this Philistine mercenary from Gath—Goliath’s hometown—now leads a third of David’s army demonstrates the remarkable trust David placed in him. Ittai had arrived shortly before as a refugee (2 Sam 15:19–22), but his loyalty during the crisis made him one of David’s most trusted leaders.

**Ahimaaz, son of Zadok**, was the high priest’s son and served as a messenger. His eagerness to bring news to David, along with his cautious manner in avoiding mention of Absalom’s death, reveals both his loyalty to the king and his understanding of David’s feelings.

**The Cushite** (הַכּוּשִׁי) was a soldier of Cushite (Ethiopian/Nubian) origin serving under Joab. The fact that Joab sent him with the heavy news instead of Ahimaaz may reflect his view that the message was dangerous to deliver—messengers bearing bad news could be punished.

## Places

**The Forest of Ephraim** (יַעַר אֶפְרָיִם) lay, according to the context, east of the Jordan in the Gilead region, even though its name suggests a connection to the tribe of Ephraim. The text emphasizes the forest’s dense vegetation—it "devoured" more people than the sword (v. 8). This rugged, wooded terrain favored David’s experienced guerrilla fighters over Absalom’s larger but less experienced force.

**The King’s Valley** (עֵמֶק הַמֶּלֶךְ), where Absalom set up his memorial pillar, is traditionally identified with the Kidron Valley near Jerusalem. Josephus places it two stadia (about 370 meters) from Jerusalem. The present-day "Tomb of Absalom" in the Kidron Valley dates from the Hellenistic period and is not the original monument.

## Events and Customs

**The heap of stones over Absalom** (v. 17) represents a dishonorable burial. Unlike honorable graves, a pile of stones thrown over a corpse often marked a criminal or enemy (cf. Josh 7:26; 8:29). This stands in sharp contrast to the memorial pillar Absalom had erected himself—ironically, he receives the opposite of the honored legacy he sought.

**Organization into three divisions** was an established military tactic in ancient Israel (cf. Judg 7:16; 1 Sam 11:11). This structure allowed flexible maneuvering and encirclement of the enemy in difficult forest terrain.

**The mule as a riding animal** (v. 9): The mule was a royal mount in Israel. That Absalom rode on a mule underscores his royal pretensions—but the animal’s flight beneath him symbolizes how his hold on the kingdom slipped from him.

## Key Terms

The expression **"לְאַט־לִי לַנַּעַר"** (le'at-li lanna'ar – "be gentle with the young man for my sake") in verse 5 shows David’s enduring fatherly love despite the rebellion. The word נַעַר (na'ar) is repeatedly used of Absalom and emphasizes that David still sees him as his "boy".

**The reward Joab offers**—ten silver pieces and a belt (v. 11)—represents substantial pay. The belt (חֲגֹרָה) was not merely clothing but a sign of warrior rank and status. That the soldier refuses this generous reward shows how seriously the king’s command was taken.
