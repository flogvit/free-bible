## People

**Ahab** (אַחְאָב) was king of the northern kingdom of Israel. In this chapter, he invites Jehoshaphat to a military alliance against Aram and gathers 400 prophets who give him the message he wants. His disguise in battle (verse 29) shows his attempt to avoid Micaiah’s judgment, yet he is struck by a random arrow and dies at sunset (verses 33–34).

**Jehoshaphat** (יְהוֹשָׁפָ֛ט, "the Lord judges") was king of Judah. The marriage alliance mentioned in verse 1 (וַיִּתְחַתֵּ֖ן) established family ties between the two royal houses. Jehoshaphat shows spiritual awareness by asking for a prophet of the Lord (verse 6), yet he still joins the war. God rescued him when the Arameans mistakenly attacked him (verse 31).

**Micaiah son of Imlah** (מִיכָ֣יְהוּ בֶן־יִמְלָ֑א) was a true prophet of Yahweh who stood in sharp contrast to the court prophets. Ahab hated him because he "never prophesies anything good about me" (verse 7). Micaiah delivered a vision of Israel’s defeat (verse 16) and of the heavenly council where a spirit of deception was sent (verses 18–22).

**Zedekiah son of Kenaanah** (צִדְקִיָּ֥הוּ בֶֽן־כְּנַעֲנָ֖ה) led the group of 400 prophets and used symbolic prophetic action with iron horns (verse 10). He struck Micaiah on the cheek and challenged his authority (verse 23).

**The 400 prophets** (אַרְבַּ֣ע מֵא֣וֹת אִישׁ, verse 5) were court prophets who unanimously gave Ahab the message he wanted to hear. Their unanimous positive message (verse 11) stands in contrast to Micaiah’s true prophecy.

**Amon** (אָמ֣וֹן) was the city governor (שַׂר־הָעִ֑יר), and **Jehoshaphat** (יוֹאָ֖שׁ) was the king’s son (verse 25). They were given responsibility to keep Micaiah imprisoned on reduced rations until Ahab’s planned return.

## Places

**Ramoth-Gilead** (רָמֹ֥ת גִּלְעָֽד) was a strategic city in Transjordan that at this time was under Aramean control. Ahab wanted to recapture the city, and this became the goal of the joint military campaign (verses 2–3, 28). It was here that Ahab was mortally wounded (verses 33–34).

**Samaria** (שֹׁמְר֑וֹן) was the capital of the northern kingdom, where Jehoshaphat visited Ahab (verse 2) and where the kings held a public council at the city gate (verse 9).

## Events and Customs

**The threshing floor at the city gate** (verse 9) was a typical place for public assemblies and legal decisions in ancient Israel. The fact that the kings sat there "on their thrones, dressed in royal robes" (מְלֻבָּשִׁ֤ים בְּגָדִים֙) emphasizes the formal and public nature of the council.

**Prophetic symbolic action** is seen when Zedekiah makes iron horns (verse 10). This was a common prophetic practice in which physical objects illustrated the message. Horns symbolized strength and military power, and the iron emphasized invincibility.

**Disguise in battle** (verse 29) was a known military strategy. Ahab’s attempt to avoid the prophecy by disguising himself reflects the belief that prophecies could be manipulated, while the text shows that God’s will was fulfilled anyway through a "random" arrow (verse 33).

## Key Terms

The expression **רוּחַ שֶׁקֶר** ("a spirit of deception," verses 21–22) raises theological questions about God’s relationship to deceit. The text presents a heavenly vision in which God allows a spirit to mislead the prophets—this shows that even false prophecy stands under God’s sovereign control, not that God Himself lies.

The verb **הִתְחַפֵּשׂ** ("to disguise oneself," verse 29) is also used of Saul before his meeting with the medium (1 Sam 28:8), and in both cases suggests an attempt to escape God’s judgment.

The expression **בֵּ֥ין הַדְּבָקִ֖ים וּבֵ֣ין הַשִּׁרְיָ֑ן** ("between the joints of the armor," verse 33) describes the vulnerable point where Ahab was struck, and emphasizes that even "random" events fulfill God’s plan.
