# Historical and Cultural Context for Micah 3

## People

The chapter addresses three groups of leaders in Judah: **רָאשֵׁי** (råshey, "heads/leaders") and **קְצִינֵי** (qetsiney, "chiefs/officers") who formed the political elite, **כֹּהֲנֶיהָ** (kohaneyha, "priests") responsible for religious guidance, and **נְבִיאִים** (nevi'im, "prophets") who claimed to speak on God's behalf. These three groups represented the complete leadership structure in pre-exilic Judah. Micah accuses them all of corruption: the leaders take bribes, the priests sell their teaching, and the prophets prophesy for payment.

## Places

**Zion** and **Jerusalem** are explicitly mentioned in verses 10 and 12. Jerusalem was the capital of the Southern Kingdom during this period and underwent significant expansion, particularly with preparations against an Assyrian siege. Archaeological excavations have revealed extensive building activity from the 7th century BC, including the city wall in the Jewish Quarter. Micah's accusation that Zion is built "with blood" (verse 10) may refer to forced labor, unjust wages, or oppression of the poor in connection with such construction projects.

## Events and Customs

The chapter reveals widespread practice of **bribery in the judicial system** (שֹׁחַד, shokhad). The Mosaic Law explicitly forbade this (Exodus 23:8; Deuteronomy 16:19), but archaeological finds such as ostraca with administrative notes show that corruption was a persistent problem in ancient Israel. The prophets who "declare peace" when they are fed (verse 5) but "proclaim war" against those who do not pay illustrate a form of religious extortion known also in surrounding cultures, where cult personnel often depended on gifts and payment.

## Key Terms

The expression **וְעָטוּ עַל־שָׂפָם** (ve'atu al-safam) in verse 7, "they shall cover their upper lip/beard," refers to a custom associated with mourning, shame, or uncleanness (cf. Leviticus 13:45; Ezekiel 24:17). When the false prophets must do this, it symbolizes that they have been put to shame because God no longer answers them. The term **קֶסֶם** (qesem, "divination") is used negatively and was forbidden in Israelite law (Deuteronomy 18:10), but appears to have been practiced by certain prophets who combined it with Yahweh worship.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The prophecy in verse 12 that "Zion shall be plowed like a field" is remarkable because it was quoted about one hundred years later in Jeremiah 26:18, where it was used as precedent to spare Jeremiah's life. This is a rare case where a prophecy is explicitly cited in a later biblical text. The expression "the temple mount" (הַר הַבַּיִת, har habbayit) appears here for the first time in the Bible.
