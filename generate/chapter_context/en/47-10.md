# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 10

## People

In this chapter, Paul indirectly refers to opponents in Corinth who criticized him. Verse 10 quotes their accusations directly: "His letters are weighty and strong, but when he is present in person, his speech is contemptible." These critics were likely traveling preachers who arrived in Corinth after Paul and presented themselves as more authoritative and charismatic leaders. They evaluated Paul according to Greco-Roman standards of rhetoric and physical presence, where a powerful personality and impressive speaking abilities were essential for a leader's credibility.

## Events and Customs

Paul uses extensive military terminology in verses 3–6. The words "ὅπλα" (weapons), "στρατεία" (campaign/military service), "ὀχυρώματα" (fortifications) and "αἰχμαλωτίζοντες" (taking captive) allude to Roman siege techniques. The Romans were famous for their systematic sieges of fortified cities, where they tore down walls and took defenders captive. The Corinthians, living in a Roman colony with significant military presence, would immediately recognize this imagery. However, Paul redefines the nature of the battle—his weapons are not "fleshly" (σαρκικά), but spiritual.

## Key Terms

The word "κανών" (canon) in verses 13, 15 and 16 is a central concept in the chapter. Originally, it denoted a straight rod or measuring stick used by craftsmen. In a figurative sense, it was used to refer to an assigned standard, area, or sphere of activity. Paul uses it here to describe the geographical and spiritual field God has assigned him—an area that includes Corinth. This was a response to opponents who were working within "others'" mission fields.

The expression "κατὰ πρόσωπον" (verses 1 and 7)—literally "according to face"—was an idiomatic Greek phrase meaning "face to face" or "outward appearance." Paul's opponents believed that his physical presence (παρουσία τοῦ σώματος) did not match the weight of his letters. In Greco-Roman culture, physiognomy—the belief that outward appearance reveals character—was widespread. A weak body was perceived as a sign of weak character.

The concepts "πραΰτης" (meekness/gentleness) and "ἐπιείκεια" (forbearance/mildness) in verse 1 were virtues exemplified by Christ, but which in Greco-Roman culture were often looked down upon as signs of weakness. Paul turns this around by making these qualities the foundation of his appeal.
