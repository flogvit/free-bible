# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 29

## People

**Pharaoh** is mentioned as the addressee of the prophecy, and based on the dating in verse 1 (587 B.C.), it likely refers to **Hophra** (Apries, reign 589–570 B.C.). Hophra was the pharaoh who sent an Egyptian army to aid Jerusalem during Nebuchadnezzar's siege, providing temporary relief before the siege resumed (Jer 37:5–8). Hophra is known from Egyptian sources and the Greek historian Herodotus, who describes his failed military campaigns and ultimately his downfall through an internal revolt.

**Nebuchadnezzar** (verses 18–20) was king of Babylon from 605–562 B.C. and the most powerful ruler of his time. His siege of Tyre lasted thirteen years (c. 585–572 B.C.) and ended without the wealth he had expected, as the city managed to negotiate a settlement. Ezekiel presents Egypt as God's compensation to Nebuchadnezzar for this effort.

## Places

**The Nile** (Hebrew: יְאֹר, *ye'or*) is central to the imagery of the chapter. The Nile was not only Egypt's lifeline for agriculture and trade but also a religious symbol closely tied to pharaoh's power and Egyptian deities such as Hapi (the god of the Nile). Pharaoh's claim "The Nile is mine; I have made myself" (verse 3, וַאֲנִי עֲשִׂיתִנִי) and "The Nile is mine, and I have created [it]" (verse 9, וַאֲנִי עָשִׂיתִי) reflect Egyptian royal ideology in which the pharaoh was regarded as divine and responsible for the Nile's annual floods.

**From Migdol to Syene** (verse 10, מִמִּגְדֹּל סְוֵנֵה) marks Egypt's geographical extent from north to south. Migdol ("tower") was a fortress city in the northeast, near the border with Sinai. Syene (Hebrew: סְוֵנֵה, modern Aswan) lay at Egypt's southern border with Cush (Nubia). The expression corresponds to the English "from one end of the land to the other" and indicates that the entire country will be affected.

**Pathros** (verse 14) refers to Upper Egypt, the region in the south of the Nile Valley. This is described as the Egyptians' "native land" (*eretz mekuratam*), reflecting historical awareness that Egyptian civilization originated in this region.

## Events and Customs

**Egypt as a "staff of reed"** (verses 6–7) alludes to Judah's policy of seeking Egyptian military support against Babylon. The image of a reed staff that breaks and injures the one leaning on it was a well-known metaphor in the ancient Near East (cf. 2 Kgs 18:21; Isa 36:6). Egypt had repeatedly disappointed its allies by promising help they could not or would not give.

**The siege of Tyre** (verse 18) is described with vivid details: soldiers with shaved heads and scraped shoulders from carrying siege equipment and protecting themselves from projectiles. This prolonged siege is confirmed in Babylonian sources and represented an enormous expenditure of resources without corresponding gain, since Tyre's wealth could be evacuated by sea.

## Key Terms

**Tannin** (הַתַּנִּם הַגָּדוֹל, verse 3) is often translated "sea monster" or "crocodile." In the Egyptian context, the crocodile was a royal symbol, and the god Sobek was depicted as a crocodile. By calling pharaoh a *tannin* that will be dragged up with hooks, Ezekiel reverses Egyptian royal iconography and reduces the pharaoh to an animal that is caught.

**The two dates** in the chapter represent the chronological framework for the oracles: Verse 1 is dated to January 587 B.C., in the middle of Jerusalem's siege. Verse 17 is dated to April 571 B.C., sixteen years later and the latest date in the book of Ezekiel. This shows that the prophet received updates and additions to his prophecies over a long period.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Babylonian chronicles and administrative texts confirm Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns against both Tyre and Egypt. A fragmentary Babylonian text from Nebuchadnezzar's 37th regnal year (568/567 B.C.) mentions a campaign against Egypt under pharaoh Amasis (Hophra's successor), confirming that Babylon indeed attacked Egypt as Ezekiel prophesied. Hophra's fall and Amasis' rise to power in 570 B.C. also align with the prophecy's picture of a weakened Egypt.
