# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 24

## People

The chapter names several women who were witnesses to the empty tomb: Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Mary the mother of James (v. 10). Joanna is mentioned only in the Gospel of Luke and was previously introduced as the wife of Chuza, an administrator under Herod Antipas (Luke 8:3). Her presence shows that the Jesus movement had followers even within court circles. Cleopas (v. 18) is mentioned only here in the New Testament. The name is a shortened form of the Greek Kleopatros. The church father Hegesippus (ca. 110–180 AD) identifies a Clopas as the brother of Joseph, Jesus’ stepfather (Eusebius, Church History 3.11), which may indicate family connection. Note that Clopas (Κλωπᾶς in John 19:25) and Cleopas (Κλεοπᾶς) are different Greek names, so the identification is uncertain.

## Places

**Emmaus** (v. 13) is described as a village sixty stadia (about 11 km) from Jerusalem. Its location is disputed. Several sites have been proposed, including modern Emmaus-Nicopolis (about 30 km west of Jerusalem), el-Qubeibeh, and Abu Ghosh. The Byzantine tradition favored Nicopolis, but the distance does not match Luke’s indication. Archaeological excavations at el-Qubeibeh have uncovered ruins from the first century, but a secure identification is lacking.

**Bethany** (v. 50) was located on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, about 3 km from Jerusalem. The site is identified with modern al-Eizariya. Archaeological findings show continuous settlement from the first century, including burial caves and remains of the village.

## Events and Customs

**The early Sunday morning visit to the tomb** (v. 1) reflects Jewish burial customs. The expression "the first day of the week" (τῇ δὲ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων) means the day after the Sabbath, that is, Sunday according to Jewish timekeeping. The women came with fragrant spices (ἀρώματα) to complete the embalming process that had been hastily begun before the Sabbath. Jewish tradition allowed visits to the tomb during the first three days to ensure that death had indeed occurred.

**The breaking of bread at Emmaus** (v. 30) follows Jewish meal customs: the host takes the bread, pronounces the blessing (berakah), breaks it, and distributes it. That Jesus assumes the role of host while being a guest is remarkable and may explain their recognition of him. This action became central in early Christian worship and is linked to the Lord’s Supper tradition.

**Women’s testimony** (v. 11) was generally not considered reliable in ancient Jewish legal culture. The fact that the disciples dismissed their report as "idle talk" (λῆρος – an expression used in medical literature for feverish fantasies or nonsense) reflects the attitudes of the time and paradoxically underscores the credibility of the narrative: no one would invent women as primary witnesses.

## Key Terms

**"On the third day"** (τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ, vv. 7, 46) was an important theological concept. In Jewish thought, the soul was believed to leave the body definitively after three days. The resurrection on the third day thus demonstrated a complete victory over death. The expression also has its background in Hosea 6:2 and possibly Jonah 2:1 (cf. Matt 12:40).

**"Opening the Scriptures"** (διήνοιγεν ἡμῖν τὰς γραφάς, v. 32) and "opening their minds" (διήνοιξεν αὐτῶν τὸν νοῦν, v. 45) use the same Greek verb. This reflects Jewish hermeneutical tradition in which the meaning of Scripture requires divine revelation to be understood correctly—a concept known from the Qumran texts as pesher interpretation.

**"The Father’s promise"** (τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν τοῦ πατρός, v. 49) refers to the Holy Spirit, foretold in the prophecies (Joel 3:1–5; Isa 32:15; Ezek 36:26–27). The expression "clothed with power from on high" (ἐνδύσησθε ἐξ ὕψους δύναμιν) uses the clothing metaphor common in Jewish apocalyptic literature for divine empowerment.
