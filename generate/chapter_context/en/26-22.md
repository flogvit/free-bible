# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 22

## Places

The chapter focuses on Jerusalem as the "city of blood" (עִיר הַדָּמִים, *'ir haddamim*), a strongly charged expression also used for Nineveh in Nahum 3:1. The term points to the systematic violence and injustice that characterized the city in its final years before Babylon's conquest in 586 B.C. At this time, Jerusalem was not only the political capital but also the religious center with the temple—making the corruption there especially serious. The reference to "the hills" in verse 9 alludes to the high places where idolatry and cultic meals took place—a practice widespread in Judah.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains a detailed catalog of violations that reflect both the ethical commands of the Mosaic Law and the Holiness Code in Leviticus 17–26. The sins mentioned include: dishonoring parents (cf. Exodus 20:12), oppression of foreigners, fatherless children, and widows (cf. Exodus 22:21–24), Sabbath-breaking, cultic meals at idol shrines, sexual offenses such as incest and intercourse during menstruation (cf. Leviticus 18 and 20), as well as economic exploitation through usury and bribery. The prohibition against interest (*neshek* and *tarbit*) was fundamental in Israelite economy to protect poor relatives (Leviticus 25:36–37). That these sins occurred in Jerusalem showed a total collapse of the covenant relationship.

The chapter also offers a rare glimpse into the various groups within society and their specific betrayals: **Prophets** (verses 25, 28)—where verse 25 compares them to roaring lions—are accused of covering up the truth with "loose plaster" and giving false oracles for personal gain. **Priests** (verse 26) failed in their duty to teach the people the difference between holy and common, clean and unclean. **Princes** (*sarim*, verse 27) are compared to wolves hunting for their own profit. **"The people of the land"** (*'am ha'arets*, verse 29), which in this period denotes free citizens who owned land, are accused of oppressing the weak. This systematic review shows that corruption permeated every level of society in pre-exilic Jerusalem.

## Key Terms

The expression **"dross"** (*sig*, סִיג) in verses 18–22 is drawn from metallurgy. In smelting furnaces, precious metals were purified by heating, where impurities rose to the surface as dross and were removed. The image was well known in ancient Judah. Normally fire represents purification with hope that pure metal will remain, but here the point is that all of Israel has become dross—there is no precious metal left to refine. **"Princes"** (*nesi'im*) in verse 6 refers to Judah's leading families and officials, not necessarily the king alone. The expression **"build a wall and stand in the breach"** (verse 30) is an image from city defense: when a wall developed a crack, someone had to physically stand there to hold off the enemy until repairs could be made. The metaphor describes the need for a spiritual intercessor.
