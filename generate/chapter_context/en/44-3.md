# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 3

## Places

**The Beautiful Gate (Ὡραία Πύλη):** This gate is mentioned only in the book of Acts and has been the subject of scholarly discussion. The most likely identification is the Nicanor Gate, an ornate bronze door that led from the Court of the Gentiles into the Court of Women. According to Josephus, this gate was made of Corinthian bronze and surpassed the other gates in splendor. It was so heavy that it required twenty men to open and close it. Its location was strategic for beggars since all Jewish worshippers had to pass through this gate to participate in temple service.

**Solomon’s Colonnade (στοὰ Σολομῶντος):** This was a covered colonnade running along the eastern side of the temple complex, overlooking the Kidron Valley. The name linked the colonnade to King Solomon, although the structure at the time of Jesus had been built by Herod the Great. The colonnade served as a natural gathering place for teaching and discussion and early on became a regular meeting spot for the Christian community in Jerusalem (Acts 5:12).

## Events and Customs

**The Hour of Prayer at the Ninth Hour:** The ninth hour (about 3 p.m.) was the time of the afternoon sacrifice (tamid) in the temple. This was one of the three fixed prayer times in Jewish practice, and many Jews gathered in the temple for prayer while incense was offered. Peter and John thus continued to follow Jewish devotional practices even after Pentecost. This pattern shows that the early Christian movement originally functioned within Jewish religious life.

**Almsgiving at the Temple Gate:** Giving alms (ἐλεημοσύνη) was a central act of piety in Judaism, often mentioned together with prayer and fasting. The temple gates were ideal places for beggars because believers were in a state of religious devotion and thus more inclined to give. A man who had been lame from birth could not work and was entirely dependent on the mercy of others. His placement at the gate was carefully chosen to maximize his chances of receiving alms.

## Key Terms

**Παῖς (pais) – "servant":** Peter uses this word for Jesus (vv. 13, 26), which in Greek can mean both "child," "son," and "servant." In this context it alludes to the "Servant of the Lord" in Isaiah (especially Isa 52:13), a messianic text central to the early church’s understanding of Jesus’ suffering and exaltation. The Septuagint uses the same word in the Isaiah passages.

**Ἀρχηγός (archēgos) – "author of life":** This rare word (v. 15) combines meanings of "leader," "founder," and "pioneer." Peter uses it paradoxically: they killed the very source of life. The word also appears in Hebrews (2:10; 12:2) and underscores Jesus’ unique role as the one who opens the way to salvation.

**Ἀποκατάστασις (apokatastasis) – "restoration":** In verse 21 this term refers to the future restoration of all things. The word was known from Greek philosophy and Jewish apocalyptic literature, referring to the belief that God will ultimately bring creation back to its original state. For Jewish listeners this would evoke messianic expectations concerning Israel’s restoration.

## Chapter-Specific Scripture References

Peter’s speech is interwoven with Old Testament references that would have resonated strongly with his Jewish audience. The quotation in verses 22–23 combines Deuteronomy 18:15–19 about the prophet like Moses with language from Leviticus 23:29 concerning being "cut off from the people." This combination was a common Jewish interpretive method (gezerah shavah) and presents Jesus as the ultimate prophet foretold by Moses—one who surpasses Moses himself. The reference to the covenant with Abraham (Gen 22:18) in verse 25 anchors the message in Israel’s foundational identity as the people of blessing.
