# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 6

## Places

The chapter describes the construction of Solomon’s temple in Jerusalem. The temple was built on Mount Moriah, the place where tradition says Abraham was to sacrifice Isaac (Genesis 22:2; 2 Chronicles 3:1). The temple site was purchased by David from Araunah the Jebusite (2 Samuel 24:18–25). The temple building itself measured approximately 27 meters in length, 9 meters in width, and 13.5 meters in height (based on a cubit of about 45 cm). The structure was oriented east-west, with the entrance facing east. The temple stood on this site until it was destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 B.C.

## Events and Customs

The text specifies that the stones were finished at the quarry, so that "neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron" was heard during construction (v. 7). This reflects an ancient tradition that iron tools were inappropriate for sacred buildings, possibly because iron was associated with weapons and warfare. The parallel to the prohibition against using cut stones on altars (Exodus 20:25) shows the same reasoning. In surrounding cultures, especially in Egypt, similar traditions exist stating that holy places should be built without profaning sounds or materials.

## Key Terms

The chapter uses several specialized architectural terms. **Debir** (דְּבִיר) refers to the Most Holy Place, the innermost room where the ark of the covenant was to stand. The word may mean "inner chamber" or possibly "place of speaking" (from דבר, "to speak"). **Hekal** (הֵיכָל) refers to the main hall, the Holy Place, and is borrowed from Sumerian/Akkadian *ekallu* ("great house" or "palace"). **Ulam** (אוּלָם) is the porch. The months **Ziv** (v. 1, 37) and **Bul** (v. 38) are pre-exilic Canaanite month names later replaced by Babylonian names. Ziv was the second month (April/May), and Bul the eighth (October/November). **Cherubim** (כְּרוּבִים) were winged creatures that served as guardians of the holy place, known from Mesopotamian and Levantine iconography.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Although Solomon’s temple has not survived, archaeological finds from the region provide important insight. The temples at *Ain Dara* in Syria (ca. 1300–740 B.C.) and *Tell Tayinat* in Turkey (ca. 900 B.C.) show the same three-part layout (porch–main hall–Most Holy Place) described in this chapter. Phoenician ivory carvings from Samaria and Nimrud depict cherubim, palm motifs, and floral ornaments that resemble the decorations described in verses 29 and 32. This confirms that the temple followed a well-established Syrian-Phoenician building tradition, which aligns with Solomon bringing craftsmen from Tyre (1 Kings 5:18).
