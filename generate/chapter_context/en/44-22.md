## People

**Gamaliel** (Γαμαλιήλ, v. 3) was one of the most respected rabbis in the first century. He led the Pharisaic school founded by Hillel (his grandfather) and held the title "Rabban" – a higher honorific than "Rabbi." In rabbinic tradition it is said that "when Rabban Gamaliel the Elder died, the honor of the Torah ceased." The fact that Paul was educated "at the feet of Gamaliel" indicates that he sat on the floor before his master, a standard position for students in rabbinic instruction.

**Ananias in Damascus** (Ἁνανίας, v. 12) is described by Paul with emphasis on his Jewish piety. Paul highlights that he was "devout according to the law" (εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον) and had a good reputation among the Jews in Damascus. This description is strategic: Paul shows his Jewish audience that his conversion was mediated by a respected, law-abiding Jew – not by an ungodly Gentile.

**The Tribune** (χιλίαρχος, v. 24–29) was the commander of the Roman garrison stationed in the Antonia Fortress. His Greek title means "leader of a thousand," corresponding to the Roman title tribunus militum. The fact that he had purchased his citizenship for "a large sum" (v. 28) was common during the reign of Emperor Claudius, when citizenship was often sold to raise revenue for the treasury.

## Places

**Tarsus in Cilicia** (Ταρσῷ τῆς Κιλικίας, v. 3) was a significant city in southeastern Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey). The city was known as an intellectual center with a philosophical school that rivaled Athens and Alexandria. Strabo described the inhabitants of Tarsus as "so eager for philosophy and all other learning that they surpassed both Athens and Alexandria." The city had the status of civitas libera (free city) with special privileges, but this did not automatically grant Roman citizenship to its residents.

**The Antonia Fortress** (παρεμβολή, v. 24) was located at the northwest corner of the Temple Mount and was named after Mark Antony. It housed a cohort (about 480–600 soldiers) and had staircases leading directly down to the Temple Mount, enabling rapid intervention in case of unrest. This is where Paul was taken and nearly flogged.

## Events and Customs

**Flogging for interrogation** (μάστιξιν ἀνετάζεσθαι, v. 24) was a brutal Roman method used to force confessions from non-citizens and slaves. The victim was bound in a forward-leaning position with leather straps (ἱμᾶσιν, v. 25) and whipped with a flagellum – a whip embedded with metal or bone fragments in its thongs. This torture was legally forbidden against Roman citizens under the Lex Porcia (198 BC) and Lex Sempronia (123 BC). Flogging an uncondemned citizen was a serious crime that could result in punishment for the responsible officer.

**Throwing dust into the air** (κονιορτὸν βαλλόντων εἰς τὸν ἀέρα, v. 23) was a traditional Jewish expression of extreme disgust and grief. Along with tearing clothes and shouting, this signaled that the crowd regarded Paul's words as blasphemy. The specific trigger was his claim to have been sent to the Gentiles (ἔθνη, v. 21) – a statement implying that Gentiles could share in God's salvation without becoming Jews.

## Key Terms

**Hebrew dialect** (τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ, v. 2) likely refers to Aramaic, the common language of Palestine at that time, rather than Biblical Hebrew. The fact that Paul spoke this language brought silence to the crowd (μᾶλλον παρέσχον ἡσυχίαν) – they may have expected Greek from a diaspora Jew. His choice of language marked Paul's Jewish identity and solidarity with his listeners.

**"The Way"** (ταύτην τὴν ὁδόν, v. 4) was an early designation for the Christian movement. In this chapter, Paul uses the term to describe the faith he once persecuted. The concept may have its background in Isaiah 40:3 or Jesus' own words about being "the way" (John 14:6).

**Citizenship** (πολιτείαν, v. 28) – the Greek word here denotes Roman citizenship (civitas Romana). Paul's claim to have been "born" a citizen (γεγέννημαι) indicates that his family already held this privilege, perhaps as a reward for services rendered to Rome or as freed slaves of Roman citizens. Birth records were kept in colonies and municipalities, so false claims of citizenship could potentially be uncovered and were punishable by death.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

A Latin inscription from the time of Claudius confirms the practice of selling citizenship, which the tribune refers to in verse 28. Under Claudius (41–54 AD), this became so widespread that prices dropped significantly toward the end of his reign. The fact that the tribune paid "a large sum" suggests he purchased his citizenship early in this period, whereas Paul's inherited citizenship gave him higher social status – an ironic twist that the text subtly emphasizes.
