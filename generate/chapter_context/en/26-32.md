## People

The chapter is directed at **Pharaoh, the king of Egypt**. Although no specific pharaoh is named by name, the dating in verse 1 (twelfth year, twelfth month, first day – about March 585 B.C.) points to **Hophra** (Hebrew: Khofra; Egyptian: Wahibre; Greek: Apries), who reigned from 589–570 B.C.

## Places

The chapter mentions several nations and peoples already in the realm of the dead: **Assyria** (v. 22–23), **Elam** (v. 24–25), **Meshech and Tubal** (v. 26–27), **Edom** (v. 29), and **princes from the north and Sidonians** (v. 30). Assyria represents the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which fell between 612–609 B.C. Elam was an ancient kingdom east of the Tigris with its capital at Susa. Meshech and Tubal were Anatolian peoples in what is now Turkey, known from Assyrian sources as Muški and Tabal. Sidon was a Phoenician coastal city north of Tyre. All these represent powerful nations that once spread fear but now lie in the realm of the dead.

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains two laments (*qinah*) over Egypt (v. 2–16 and v. 17–32). The lament genre was an established literary form in the ancient Near East, used at deaths and national disasters. The second lament describes **descent to Sheol** (*sheol*) and burial among "the uncircumcised" (*arelim*). In Israelite and Egyptian thought, this was a shameful fate. Egyptians placed great emphasis on proper burial and mummification to ensure a dignified afterlife. To be laid among the uncircumcised war dead was therefore a special humiliation for the Egyptian pharaoh, who was regarded as divine. Verse 27 mentions the custom of placing swords under the heads of fallen warriors, a burial practice known from archaeological finds in the region.

## Key Terms

**Tannim** (תַּנִּים, v. 2) is often translated "sea monster" or "crocodile" and represents chaotic forces. The Nile was home to crocodiles, and the image links Pharaoh with the dangerous animal that stirs up the water – a metaphor for Egypt’s destabilizing influence. **Sheol** (שְׁאוֹל) is the realm of the dead or the dwelling place of the deceased, described as a place deep under the earth ("the depths of the earth", *erets tachtit*). **Bor** (בּוֹר, "pit/grave") is used parallel to Sheol and denotes the grave as a descent into the realm of the dead. **Arelim** (עֲרֵלִים, "the uncircumcised") was a derogatory term for pagan peoples; lying among them was a shame that stripped the deceased of honor even in death.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Babylonian chronicles document Nebuchadnezzar’s campaign against Egypt in 568/567 B.C., confirming the historical framework for this prophecy. Assyrian inscriptions record Ashurbanipal’s plundering of Susa in 646 B.C., explaining why Elam is mentioned among the fallen nations in the realm of the dead. Archaeological excavations in Anatolia have uncovered Bronze Age burial sites where warriors were buried with weapons, including swords placed beside the body – a parallel to the description in verse 27 of heroes who "went down to Sheol with their weapons of war and laid their swords under their heads".
