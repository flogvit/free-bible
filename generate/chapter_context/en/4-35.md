# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 35

## Places

The chapter concerns 48 Levitical cities scattered throughout the promised land, including six cities of refuge. Three of these were to be located east of the Jordan (later identified as Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan) and three west of the Jordan (Kedesh, Shechem, and Hebron, according to Joshua 20). The placement was strategically planned so that no one would have an unreasonably long journey to a place of refuge. The detailed measurements for pastureland around the cities (1,000 cubits from the city wall, with a total area extending 2,000 cubits in each direction) reflect a carefully planned land allocation designed to secure the Levites' livelihood without their owning agricultural land.

## Events and Customs

The city-of-refuge system represents a groundbreaking legal ordinance in the ancient world. While blood vengeance (*gə'ullat haddam*) was a widespread custom throughout the ancient Near East, where the nearest male relative had the duty to avenge a killing, this chapter introduced a distinction between intentional murder and unintentional manslaughter. This was unusual in contemporary legal systems. The law required a trial before the congregation (*'edah*) before a verdict could be rendered, and it explicitly forbade accepting ransom (*kofer*) for murder—a practice common in other cultures where wealthy murderers could buy their freedom.

## Key Terms

**Migrash** (מִגְרָשׁ) – The word is often translated as "pastureland" or "suburb" and denotes the open area surrounding the Levitical cities. This was not agricultural land, but rather an area for livestock and other property. The term occurs almost exclusively in connection with the Levitical cities.

**Go'el haddam** (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם) – "The avenger of blood" or literally "the redeemer of blood." The role of *go'el* had several aspects in Israelite culture, including redeeming property and marrying widows (see Ruth). As the avenger of blood, he had both the right and the duty to pursue the killer, but the city-of-refuge system limited this right by requiring a trial.

**Kofer** (כֹּפֶר) – "Ransom" or "atonement payment." The prohibition against accepting *kofer* for murder (verses 31–32) emphasizes that human life has a value that cannot be measured in money. This stands in contrast to many other ancient law codes where financial compensation was the standard remedy for killing.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological investigations of cities identified as Levitical cities reveal interesting patterns. Several of the named cities of refuge, such as Shechem and Hebron, have long settlement histories and were located at important crossroads, which aligns with the text's intention regarding accessibility. The Laws of Hammurabi from Babylon (c. 1750 B.C.) and the Middle Assyrian laws contain provisions concerning killing and compensation but lack a corresponding city-of-refuge concept. The closest parallel is found in Hittite laws, which also distinguish between intentional and unintentional killing, but resolve this through financial compensation rather than a geographical refuge system.
