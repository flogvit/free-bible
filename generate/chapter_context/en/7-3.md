# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 3

## People

**Othniel son of Kenaz** (vv. 9–11) is introduced as the first judge. He was Caleb's younger brother (or nephew) and had already proven himself a skilled warrior by capturing Kiriath-sefer (Judg 1:13).

**Ehud son of Gera** (vv. 15–30) is described as "left-handed" (אִטֵּר), meaning either left-handed or possibly having an injured right hand. He was from the tribe of Benjamin, whose name ironically means "son of the right hand." The Benjamites were known for their left-handed sling throwers (Judg 20:16), and Ehud's condition gave him a tactical advantage since guards would normally check the left thigh for weapons.

**Eglon, king of Moab** (vv. 12–25), is described as "very fat" (בָּרִיא מְאֹד). The name Eglon is related to the Hebrew word for "calf" (עֵגֶל), suggesting a polemical wordplay by the author.

**Shamgar son of Anath** (v. 31) is mentioned briefly. His name and patronymic "son of Anath" may link him to the Canaanite war goddess Anat, possibly indicating Canaanite origins or that he came from an area with strong Canaanite influence.

## Places

**Aram-naharaim** (v. 8), "Aram between the two rivers," refers to the Mesopotamian region between the Euphrates and Tigris rivers. Cushan-rishathaim ruled from there and oppressed Israel for eight years.

**Ir-hattemarim** ("City of Palms," v. 13) is Jericho, known for its date palms and strategic location at the Jordan River crossings. Moabite control over this area gave them influence over important trade routes and agricultural land.

**Gilgal** (v. 19) was a significant cult site near Jericho where Israel had camped after crossing the Jordan. The reference to "pesillim" (carved images/stone pillars) at Gilgal suggests that pagan cult objects had been established there, illustrating the religious syncretism of the period.

**Seirah** (v. 26) was likely a wooded area in the hill country of Ephraim where Ehud fled and gathered Israelite forces.

## Events and Customs

**The Tribute System** (vv. 15–18) is described through "minha" (מִנְחָה), which could refer to both religious offerings and political tribute. The fact that the Israelites sent tribute with a delegation led by Ehud shows how oppressed peoples had to deliver economic payments to their overlords. Tribute was typically carried by several men, providing cover for Ehud's mission.

**The Upper Cooling Room** (עֲלִיַּת הַמְּקֵרָה, v. 20) was a cool room on the roof, typical of wealthy homes in the Middle East. These rooms often had lattice openings for ventilation and were used for rest during the hot season. The fact that Eglon was alone there was part of normal afternoon practice, giving Ehud an opportunity for a private audience.

**The expression "to cover one's feet"** (מֵסִיךְ אֶת־רַגְלָיו, v. 24) is a euphemism for relieving oneself. The servants assumed the king needed privacy, which explains their hesitation to open the door.

## Key Terms

**"Seren"** (סֶרֶן, v. 3) is the title of Philistine rulers and is used only for Philistine leaders in the Old Testament. The word is likely of non-Semitic origin and may be related to the Greek "tyrannos." The five Philistine lords ruled the city-states of Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, and Gath.

**"Gomed"** (גֹּמֶד, v. 16) is a unit of length that appears only here in the Bible. It is thought to be approximately a short cubit (about 30–38 cm), making the dagger short enough to hide under clothing but long enough to be deadly.

**Baalim and Asheroth** (v. 7) represent male and female Canaanite deities respectively. Baal was the god of fertility and storms, while Asherah was the goddess of fertility, often represented by wooden poles or stylized trees at cult sites.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Moabite Stone** (Mesha Stele, ca. 840 BC) from Dibon east of the Dead Sea confirms the long-standing hostility between Moab and Israel, although it dates to a later period than Ehud's time.

**The Ox-Goad** (מַלְמַד הַבָּקָר, v. 31) used by Shamgar was a common agricultural tool with a metal tip, typically 2–2.5 meters long. Archaeological finds of such tools show they could be effective improvised weapons. The use of farming implements as weapons may reflect limited access to metal weapons during this period.
