# Historical and Cultural Context for Revelation 2

## Places

**Ephesus** (vv. 1–7) was the largest city in the Roman province of Asia, with an estimated population of 200,000–250,000. The city was known for its famous Temple of Artemis, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, and served as a major religious and commercial center. Paul had been instrumental in establishing the church there and ministered in the city for about three years (Acts 19). The city also had a significant Jewish community and was a center for emperor worship.

**Smyrna** (vv. 8–11) was located about 55 km north of Ephesus and was a prosperous port city known for its loyalty to Rome. The city earned the honor of building a temple for emperor worship as early as 26 AD. The Jewish community in Smyrna was known for its hostility toward Christians, as confirmed by later sources such as the martyrdom account of Polycarp (c. 156 AD).

**Pergamum** (vv. 12–17) was the capital of the province of Asia and a religious center with impressive temples dedicated to Zeus, Athena, Dionysus, and Asclepius (the god of healing). The city had the first temple for emperor worship in Asia (29 BC), dedicated to Augustus and Rome. The expression "Satan’s throne" (v. 13) may refer to the massive altar of Zeus on the acropolis, the cult of Asclepius (whose symbol was the serpent), or emperor worship.

**Thyatira** (vv. 18–29) was the smallest of the seven cities but an important commercial center known for its many guilds (craftsmen associations). Lydia, the purple cloth merchant from Acts 16:14, came from this city. The guilds held regular meals in temples where meat sacrificed to idols was served—this created a real dilemma for Christian craftsmen who depended on guild membership for their livelihood.

## People

**Antipas** (v. 13) is mentioned as "my faithful witness" who was killed in Pergamum. He is the only named martyr in Revelation. Although we have no extra-biblical sources confirming his existence, his death illustrates that persecution during this period could be fatal. Later church traditions claim he was burned inside a bronze bull, but this cannot be verified historically.

**"Jezebel"** (v. 20) is likely a symbolic name for an actual woman in the church at Thyatira who called herself a prophetess. The name is a deliberate reference to Queen Jezebel in the Old Testament (1 Kings 16–21), who promoted Baal worship in Israel. This woman appears to have taught that Christians could participate in idol-feasts and sexual immorality.

## Events and Customs

**Guild meals and food sacrificed to idols** constituted a central social and economic problem for early Christians. In the cities of Asia Minor, craftsmen were organized into guilds that regularly held communal meals in temples where the food had been offered to gods. Refusing to participate could mean financial ruin and social ostracism. The reference to Balaam (v. 14) and the criticism of Jezebel (v. 20) address precisely this issue: Could Christians eat "food sacrificed to idols" (εἰδωλόθυτα) in order to maintain their place in society?

**"The synagogue of Satan"** (v. 9) reflects intense conflict between Christians and Jewish communities in Smyrna. Jews who rejected Jesus as the Messiah and possibly accused Christians before Roman authorities are described here with this sharp expression. This must be understood in light of an internal family conflict within Judaism, not as later anti-Jewish rhetoric.

## Key Terms

**"The Nicolaitans"** (Νικολαϊτῶν, vv. 6, 15) were a group that both Ephesus and Pergamum had dealings with. The exact teaching of the group is unknown, but the context associates them with eating food sacrificed to idols and sexual immorality. The name may come from a leader named Nicolas, or it could be a symbolic construction from Greek νικάω (to conquer) and λαός (people)—"those who conquer the people." Irenaeus (c. 180 AD) identified them with Nicolas of Antioch (Acts 6:5), but this is uncertain.

**"The white stone"** (ψῆφον λευκήν, v. 17) has several possible backgrounds: In legal proceedings, people used white and black stones for acquittal and conviction. White stones also served as tickets to festive meals and as amulets bearing secret names. The promise likely combines several of these associations: acquittal, access to the heavenly meal, and a new, secret name expressing intimate fellowship with Christ.

**"The crown of life"** (τὸν στέφανον τῆς ζωῆς, v. 10) uses the Greek word στέφανος, which referred to wreaths given to athletic victors and heroes. Smyrna was known for its athletic competitions, and the city’s acropolis was called "the crown of Smyrna" because of its beautiful buildings. The image would thus have had special resonance for members of the church.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Extensive archaeological excavations have shed light on all four cities. In **Ephesus**, researchers have uncovered the enormous Library of Celsus, the main street known as the Arcadian Way, and remains of the Temple of Artemis. In **Pergamum**, the massive altar of Zeus (now in Berlin) and the sanctuary of Asclepius ("Asklepieion") are particularly relevant—the latter was a famous healing center where snakes were kept as sacred animals. The ancient ruins of **Smyrna** lie largely beneath modern Izmir, but the old agora has been partially excavated. In **Thyatira** (modern Akhisar), inscriptions have been found mentioning many of the city’s guilds, including dyers, bronze workers, bakers, and wool merchants—confirming the picture of a city dominated by craft guilds.
