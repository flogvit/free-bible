# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 3

## People

**Og, king of Bashan** (עוֹג מֶלֶךְ־הַבָּשָׁן) appears in this chapter as the last survivor of the *Rephaim* (רְפָאִים, v. 11), a people known in Old Testament tradition for their unusual size. The text specifically mentions his iron bed (עֶרֶשׂ בַּרְזֶל) – possibly a sarcophagus – which measured about 4 meters in length and 1.8 meters in width, and which, according to the text, could be seen in Rabbah, the capital of the Ammonites. This served as concrete testimony to his existence and size for later generations.

**Jair, son of Manasseh** (יָאִיר בֶּן־מְנַשֶּׁה, v. 14) and **Machir** (מָכִיר, v. 15) are mentioned as leaders who received territories east of the Jordan. Jair gave his name to "the towns of Jair" (חַוֺּת יָאִיר), an area in Bashan that retained this name for a long time.

**Joshua** (יְהוֹשׁוּעַ, v. 21, 28) receives Moses' encouragement and commissioning as leader in this chapter. Moses commands him not to fear, for the Lord will fight for the people (v. 21-22), and God instructs Moses to strengthen and encourage Joshua, who is to lead the people into the land (v. 28).

## Places

**Bashan** (הַבָּשָׁן) was a fertile highland area east of the Sea of Galilee. The region included **Edrei** (אֶדְרֶעִי, v. 1, 10), the place where Og came out to meet Israel in battle, and **Salkah** (סַלְכָה, v. 10), which marked the eastern boundary of the area. Edrei is usually identified with today's Dar'a in Syria.

**The district of Argob** (חֶבֶל אַרְגֹּב, v. 4, 13-14) is described as an area containing 60 fortified cities with high walls, gates, and bars (v. 5). This testifies to a highly developed urban culture in the region.

**Mount Hermon** (חֶרְמוֹן, v. 8-9) marked the northern boundary of the territory and was known by several names: the Sidonians called it **Sirion** (שִׂרְיֹן), while the Amorites called it **Senir** (שְׂנִיר).

**Pisgah** (הַפִּסְגָּה, v. 17, 27) is mentioned both as a geographical marker ("the slopes of Pisgah", אַשְׁדֹּת הַפִּסְגָּה) and as the mountain peak where Moses was told to go up to see the land in all directions.

**Kinnereth** (כִּנֶּרֶת, v. 17) and **the Arabah Sea/the Salt Sea** (יָם הָעֲרָבָה יָם הַמֶּלַח, v. 17) mark the geographical boundaries of the allotted area.

**Beth-peor** (בֵּית פְּעוֹר, v. 29) was the place where Israel camped in the valley opposite, marking the conclusion of Moses' geographical overview.

## Events and Customs

**The practice of herem** (הַחֲרֵם, v. 6) – the total destruction of conquered cities including their inhabitants – is also mentioned in this chapter, just as it was regarding Sihon (chapter 2). Livestock and plunder could be kept (v. 7), but the people and the cities themselves were devoted to destruction. This was a religious act of war where the spoil was "consecrated" to God through destruction.

**The allocation of land** (v. 12-17) describes the first distribution of inheritance land to the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh east of the Jordan. The agreement stipulated that fighting men had to cross the Jordan to help the other tribes before they could return to their families (v. 18-20) – an early example of military mobilization duty that bound the tribes together.

**Moses' prayer and God's answer** (v. 23-28) describe Moses' earnest desire to enter the promised land, and God's refusal. Instead, Moses was told to view the land from Pisgah and to commission Joshua as his successor.

## Key Terms

The expression **"eretz refaim"** (אֶרֶץ רְפָאִים, "land of the Rephaim", v. 13) links the area to an earlier population. The Rephaim are mentioned several times in the OT as a people of unusually large stature who inhabited areas both east and west of the Jordan before the arrival of the Israelites.

**"Amat ish"** (בְּאַמַּת־אִישׁ, "by a man's cubit", v. 11) indicates that the measurements of Og's bed were based on the standard cubit – about 45 cm – thus not some special "giant size". This gives the bed dimensions of approximately 4 x 1.8 meters.

Moses' prayer uses the double divine name **"Adonai JHVH"** (אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה, v. 24), a solemn expression that emphasizes the earnest character of the prayer.
