# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 18

## Places

**The oaks of Mamre** (אֵלֹנֵי מַמְרֵא) are located near Hebron, about 30 kilometers south of Jerusalem. The site was already established as Abraham’s base in Genesis 13:18, and the terebinth trees provided valuable shade during the hot season—explaining why Abraham sat at the tent entrance "in the heat of the day" (v. 1).

**Sodom** is mentioned in this chapter as the target of God’s judgment. The city was likely situated in the southern part of the Dead Sea plain, an area that geological studies show has been subject to seismic activity and asphalt deposits.

## Events and Customs

**Abraham’s hospitality customs** reflect fundamental values in the ancient Near East. Offering water for foot washing, rest under trees, and food for travelers was not merely politeness but a sacred duty. Similar hospitality protocols are documented in Ugaritic and Mesopotamian texts from the same period. To refuse hospitality was a serious social offense, which later becomes central to the judgment against Sodom.

**The meal Abraham prepared** was extraordinarily lavish: three seahs (about 22 liters) of fine flour for bread, a whole calf, as well as curds and milk. This far exceeded normal hospitality and signaled that Abraham recognized his guests’ special status. The fact that he himself stood and served them while they ate was a sign of deep respect—the host’s role as servant underscored the honor due to the guests.

## Key Terms

**"Adonai"** (אֲדֹנָי) in verse 3 is textually interesting. The Masoretic Text marks this term as holy, indicating that Abraham was addressing the Lord Himself, not merely being polite to one of the men. The shifting references between the three men and the Lord (YHWH) throughout the chapter have given rise to various interpretations—both as a theophany (divine manifestation) and as an angelic visitation.

**"Dust and ashes"** (עָפָר וָאֵפֶר) in verse 27 is Abraham’s self-description when he negotiates with God. The expression combines two words for what is most worthless and perishable, representing extreme humility. This rhetoric—humbling oneself before making a bold request—is well known from Mesopotamian prayer texts and diplomatic correspondence of the period.

**"Righteousness and justice"** (צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט) in verse 19 is a word pair that becomes central in prophetic literature. Here it is used for the first time in the Bible to describe the way of life Abraham is to pass on to his descendants, establishing a connection between ethical conduct and the covenant relationship with God.
