# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 29

## Key Concepts

The expression **"bene elim"** (בְּנֵי אֵלִים, "sons of God") in verse 1 is a significant term referring to the heavenly assembly or angelic host surrounding God's throne. In Canaanite mythology, similar expressions referred to lesser deities within a pantheon, but in Israelite theology, these are beings who worship the one true God. The psalm thus transforms language from a polytheistic context into monotheistic praise.

The phrase **"qol JHVH"** (קוֹל יְהוָה, "the voice of the Lord") is repeated seven times in the psalm and describes thunder as God's voice. The number seven can symbolize perfection and divine completeness. In the ancient Near East, thunder was often associated with storm gods, but here all the power of nature is attributed exclusively to Israel's God.

The word **"mabbul"** (מַבּוּל) in verse 10 is the specific Hebrew term for the flood waters in Noah's time and is used only here and in Genesis 6–11. This connects God's dominion over the storm with His cosmic sovereignty over the chaotic forces from creation and the flood.

## Places

**Lebanon** (הַלְּבָנוֹן) and **Sirion** (שִׂרְיֹן) are mentioned in verses 5–6. The Lebanon mountains north of Israel were famous for their mighty cedar trees, symbols of strength and splendor. Sirion is the Sidonian name for Mount Hermon (Deuteronomy 3:9). That these mighty mountains shake and leap like calves emphasizes God's overwhelming power.

**The wilderness of Kadesh** (מִדְבַּר קָדֵשׁ) in verse 8 is likely located in the Syrian region north of Israel, not Kadesh-Barnea in the south. This places the entire movement of the storm from Lebanon in the north through the wilderness, showing that God's rule extends over all lands.

## Events and Customs

Psalm 29 has clear parallels to Canaanite literature, especially hymns to the storm god Baal found at Ugarit. Some scholars believe the psalm may have been an older Canaanite text adapted for Yahweh worship, while others see it as a deliberate polemic: Everything attributed to Baal—thunder, rain, fertility—actually belongs to the Lord alone. The psalm's use of its competitors' own genre and imagery to proclaim the supremacy of Israel's God is a powerful theological strategy.
