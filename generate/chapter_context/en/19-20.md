## Events and Customs

Psalm 20 is a royal prayer of intercession, likely used in a cultic setting before the king went out to war. The structure suggests a temple liturgy where the people or priests pray for the king (vv. 2–6), followed by an expression of trust (vv. 7–9) and a concluding prayer (v. 10). The reference to "grain offering" (מִנְחֹת, *minḥot*) and "burnt offering" (עוֹלָה, *olah*) in verse 4 indicates that the psalm was sung in connection with sacrifices at the temple, where the king sought God’s blessing before a military campaign. The expression "make fat" (יְדַשְּׁנֶה, *yedašneh*) refers to God accepting the burnt offering—when the fat was consumed by fire, it was a sign of divine approval.

## Places

"The sanctuary" (קֹדֶשׁ, *qodeš*) and "Zion" (צִיּוֹן, *tsiyon*) in verse 3 point to the temple in Jerusalem as the center of God’s presence and help. The parallelism emphasizes that God’s support comes from his dwelling on Mount Zion. "His holy heavens" (שְׁמֵי קָדְשׁוֹ, *šemey qodšo*) in verse 7 expands the perspective to the heavenly sanctuary, showing the connection between the earthly temple and God’s heavenly throne.

## Key Terms

The word "משיח" (*mashiaḥ*, "his anointed") in verse 7 refers to the king who was anointed with oil at his installation. In Old Testament times, kings, priests, and prophets were anointed as a sign of divine selection and empowerment. The term later took on messianic significance. The contrast in verse 8 between "chariots and horses" versus "the name of the Lord" reflects a fundamental theological conviction: Israel’s security lay not in military strength but in trust in God. The verb "נַזְכִּיר" (*nazkir*, "call upon/mention") means to proclaim the name of the Lord as the source of strength.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian and Egyptian reliefs and texts thoroughly document military preparations in the ancient Near East, including religious rituals before war. Kings sought oracles and offered sacrifices to their gods before campaigns. Psalm 20 reflects a similar Israelite practice, but with the crucial difference that trust was placed not in military capacity such as chariots and horses—the weapons for which the great powers Egypt and Assyria were known—but in the name of the Lord alone.
