# Historical and Cultural Context for Proverbs 17

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains several references to legal and social practices in ancient Israel. Verses 8 and 23 mention bribes (שֹׁחַד, *shochad*), which were a widespread problem in the ancient Near East. Israelite law explicitly forbade judges from accepting bribes (Exodus 23:8; Deuteronomy 16:19), but Proverbs reflects a pragmatic observation of how bribes actually functioned in society—as a "magic charm" in the eyes of the giver.

Verse 18 describes the custom of "striking hands" (תּוֹקֵעַ כָּף, *tokea kaf*) as a guarantee for another person's debt. This was a binding legal act where one physically struck their hand against another person's hand to secure a loan. This practice is well documented in the ancient Near East and could lead to financial ruin if the borrower failed to repay.

## Key Terms

The term "זִבְחֵי־רִיב" (*zivche-riv*) in verse 1, "sacrifices of strife", refers to peace offerings where the meat was eaten by the family in a festive meal. The irony is that these were meant to celebrate fellowship with God and family, but here they are ruined by quarreling—a quiet, dry piece of bread in peace is better.

The expression "מַצְרֵף" (*matsref*, "crucible") and "כוּר" (*kur*, "furnace") in verse 3 describe metallurgical technology used to refine silver and gold. The crucible separated impurities from precious metal at high temperatures. This image is applied to how the Lord tests human hearts—a process that reveals true character.

Verse 12 mentions "דֹּב שַׁכּוּל" (*dov shakkul*), a she-bear that has lost her cubs. The Syrian brown bear (Ursus arctos syriacus) existed in Israel's mountain and forest regions in biblical times and was known to be extremely dangerous when protecting or seeking its young. This image emphasizes that it is safer to meet an enraged she-bear than a fool in his folly.
