# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 1

## People

**Timothy** is mentioned as a co-sender of the letter (v. 1). He was known to the church in Corinth from previous visits. His inclusion in the opening of the letter signals continuity and shared apostolic authority.

**Silvanus** (v. 19), also known as Silas, was one of Paul's coworkers who had preached the gospel in Corinth together with Paul and Timothy during their first stay in the city. By mentioning all three as preachers, Paul emphasizes that the message about Christ was consistent from the beginning.

## Places

**Achaia** (v. 1) was the Roman province that included southern Greece, with Corinth as its capital. The letter is addressed to the church in Corinth as well as all the saints throughout Achaia, suggesting that it was intended to circulate among several churches in the region.

**Asia** (v. 8) refers to the Roman province of Asia, which covered western Asia Minor with Ephesus as its capital. Paul describes a severe affliction he experienced there, so extreme that he "lost all hope of life." The exact event is debated—possible explanations include the riot in Ephesus (Acts 19:23-41), imprisonment, serious illness, or persecutions not recorded elsewhere. The expression "the sentence of death" (ἀπόκριμα τοῦ θανάτου) in verse 9 uses a rare word (a hapax legomenon in the New Testament) that can mean "sentence" or "answer"—possibly indicating an inner conviction of impending death rather than a formal legal judgment.

**Macedonia and Judea** (v. 16) are mentioned as part of Paul's travel plans. Macedonia was the northern part of the Greek mainland, while Judea was the Jewish heartland. The expression "to be sent on" (προπεμφθῆναι) was a technical term for the practical and financial support that churches provided to traveling preachers—an important aspect of hospitality and missionary networks in the early church.

## Key Terms

**Paraklesis** (παράκλησις) and its related verb παρακαλέω are key words in this chapter, appearing together ten times in verses 3-7. The word can be translated as "comfort," "encouragement," or "exhortation" and carries a rich range of meanings. It shares the same root as "Paraclete" (παράκλητος), the title for the Holy Spirit in John's Gospel. In Greco-Roman culture, paraklesis was associated with rhetoric meant to strengthen and encourage, but Paul gives it theological depth by grounding all comfort in God, who is called "the God of all comfort" (v. 3).

**Arrabon** (ἀρραβών, v. 22), translated as "guarantee" or "down payment," was a loanword from Semitic (Hebrew/Phoenician) commercial language. It referred to a partial advance payment that guaranteed the completion of a transaction. In papyrus documents from Egypt, the word is used for earnest money in purchases. Paul applies this well-known business term to the Spirit as God's guarantee of future complete salvation—a powerful metaphor that would have been immediately recognizable to the Corinthians' commercial culture.

**Seal and anointing** (vv. 21-22) combine two images: "χρίσας" (anointed) alludes to Old Testament consecration of kings and priests, while "σφραγισάμενος" (sealed) refers to sealing practices that marked ownership and authenticity in antiquity. Seals were used on documents, goods, and even slaves to show who they belonged to.
