# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 17

## Characters

**Elijah (אֵלִיָּהוּ)** is introduced here for the first time in the Bible, without any formal background story. His name means "My God is JHVH" – a programmatic name in the conflict with Baal worship. The designation "the Tishbite" (הַתִּשְׁבִּי) is debated; it may refer to a place called Tishbe, or it could mean "immigrant" from the root ישׁב. The addition "from the inhabitants of Gilead" places him in the Transjordanian highlands, an area known for its rural character and distance from the central centers of power.

**The widow at Zarephath** remains nameless, but her role is remarkable. As a Phoenician woman and a widow, she represents one of the most vulnerable groups in ancient society. That JHVH sends his prophet to pagan territory to be supported by a poor widow challenges expectations about where God's care and power reach.

## Places

**The Kerith Ravine** (נַחַל כְּרִית) is located "east of the Jordan" in the Gilead region. The Hebrew word נַחַל denotes a seasonal stream (wadi) that fills during the rainy season but dries up in summer. This geographical fact makes it naturally understandable that the brook dried up (v. 7). Its exact location is unknown, but the area offered hiding places in the many ravines leading down toward the Jordan Valley.

**Zarephath** (צָרְפַת) was located on Phoenician territory between Tyre and Sidon, about 13 km south of Sidon. Archaeological excavations at Sarafand in Lebanon (the modern name) have revealed a significant settlement with continuous habitation through the Bronze Age and Iron Age. The finds include pottery, loom weights, and remains of metalworking industries. The city was subject to Sidon and thus within the domain of King Ethbaal – Jezebel's father. That Elijah is sent here, to the very heartland of Baal worship, underscores the theological confrontation.

## Events and Customs

**Drought as divine judgment** was a well-known motif throughout the ancient Near East. In Mesopotamia and Canaan, rain was associated with fertility gods, especially Baal as "the one who rides on the clouds" and brings rain. Elijah's proclamation of drought is therefore a direct challenge to Baal's claimed power. The formula "as surely as JHVH lives" (חַי־יְהוָה) is an oath-like declaration that binds the statement to JHVH's own existence.

**The ravens' provision** (vv. 4-6) has sparked discussion. The Hebrew עֹרְבִים can be read as "ravens" or, with different vowels, as "Arabians" (עֲרָבִים). Most translations retain "ravens," which creates a paradox: unclean birds (Lev 11:15) bring food to the prophet. The meals of "bread and meat morning and evening" may recall God's provision for Israel in the wilderness, although the pattern there was somewhat different.

**The raising of the dead boy** (vv. 17-24) is the first resurrection account in the Old Testament. Elijah's action of "stretching himself out over" (וַיִּתְמֹדֵד) the child three times is a unique act later repeated by Elisha (2 Kgs 4:34-35). The widow's reaction about "remembering my sin" (v. 18) reflects the common belief that sickness and death were punishment for sin.

## Key Terms

**"To stand before JHVH"** (עָמַדְתִּי לְפָנָיו) in verse 1 is a technical expression for prophetic service. It implies that Elijah has access to the heavenly council, like a servant standing ready before his master. The phrase establishes Elijah's authority as JHVH's messenger.

**The vessel terms** in the Zarephath story use specific words: כַּד is a jar or jug for flour, while צַפַּחַת is a smaller bottle or pitcher for oil. These everyday household items become carriers of the enduring miracle.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Excavations at Zarephath (Sarafand), led by James B. Pritchard in the 1970s, uncovered a sanctuary dedicated to the Phoenician goddess Tanit-Astarte. The find confirms the site's Phoenician religious character during Elijah's time. Pottery finds from the 8th century BC show a wealthy coastal city with extensive trade activity, although the widow's poverty shows that not all shared in this prosperity. A small sanctuary room with votive offerings provides insight into the Phoenician religion that Elijah confronted.
