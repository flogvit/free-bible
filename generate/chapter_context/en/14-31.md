# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 31

## People

**Hezekiah** appears in this chapter as an administrator and reformer, not only the one who restored temple service (chapters 29–30) but also the organizer of the economic foundation for lasting religious practice. His systematic approach to temple finances reflects a well-developed state administration.

**Azariah, the chief priest from the house of Zadok** (verse 10), represents the Zadokite priestly line that had dominated in Jerusalem since Solomon’s time. His title "הַכֹּהֵן הָרֹאשׁ" (chief priest) and his connection to the house of Zadok underscore the legitimacy of temple leadership.

**Conaniah** (כָּֽנַנְיָ֣הוּ) and his brother **Shimei** (verses 12–13) served as overseers over the storehouses, with ten subordinate officials under them—an administrative structure that testifies to the scale of the collection.

**Kore, son of Imnah** (verse 14), was a Levite and gatekeeper at the east gate. He had responsibility for the voluntary offerings to God and for distributing the Lord’s offering and the most holy gifts.

## Events and Customs

**The destruction of cult objects** (verse 1) describes a thorough cleansing that extended throughout Judah and Benjamin, and further into Ephraim and Manasseh. The terms used—"מַצֵּבוֹת" (pillars), "אֲשֵׁרִים" (Asherah poles), "בָּמוֹת" (high places)—represent Canaanite religious practices that had infiltrated Israelite worship. Such iconoclasm was radical, since many of these cult sites had long traditions.

**The priestly division system** (verse 2) was based on David’s original organization of twenty-four priestly divisions. The expression "בְּשַׁעֲרֵי מַחֲנוֹת יְהוָה" (at the gates of the camps of the Lord) is unusual and may refer to the temple as a holy military camp, with parallels to the tabernacle during the wilderness wanderings.

**The tithe and firstfruits system** (verses 5–6) followed the regulations in the books of Moses. "רֵאשִׁית" (firstfruits) and "מַעְשַׂר" (tithe) were brought in such quantities that they formed "עֲרֵמוֹת" (piles). The collection period from the third to the seventh month (verse 7) corresponds with the agricultural harvest calendar—from the wheat and barley harvests in spring to the grape and olive harvests in autumn.

## Key Terms

The expression "לְמַעַן יֶחֶזְקוּ בְּתוֹרַת יְהוָה" (verse 4)—"so that they might be strengthened in the law of the Lord"—reveals an important connection: financial support for the priesthood was not merely welfare, but a prerequisite enabling them to devote themselves fully to Torah study and teaching rather than having to engage in farming themselves.

**Storehouses** ("לְשָׁכוֹת", verse 11) were rooms within the temple complex designated for storage. The Hebrew word "בֶּאֱמוּנָה" (verses 12, 15, 18)—"in faithfulness"—is repeated several times and emphasizes that managing holy funds required integrity.

**Genealogical records** ("הִתְיַחֵשׂ", verses 16–19) were crucial for verifying priestly and Levitical legitimacy. The registration included male priestly descendants from the age of three upward who came to the temple for daily service (verse 16), while Levites were registered from the age of twenty upward for active service (verse 17). This testifies to a comprehensive registration system.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations have uncovered several "למלך" stamp impressions (lmlk seals, "belonging to the king") from Hezekiah’s time, attesting to a centralized royal administration. These seals on storage jars support the picture of Hezekiah as a king who systematized the collection and distribution of goods. The discovery of the so-called "Hezekiah bulla" in Jerusalem also confirms his historicity and administrative activity.
