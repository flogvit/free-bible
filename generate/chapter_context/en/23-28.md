# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 28

## Places

**Samaria and "the crown of the rich valley" (v. 1, 4):** Samaria was the capital of the Northern Kingdom (Ephraim/Israel) and stood on a hill surrounded by fertile valleys. The city was founded by King Omri around 875 B.C. and was known for its wealth and beauty. The expression "the crown of the rich valley" (רֹאשׁ גֵּיא־שְׁמָנִים) describes the city's location atop a hill overlooking fertile areas. The first part of the chapter (v. 1–6) was likely proclaimed shortly before Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 B.C.

**Mount Perazim and the Valley of Gibeon (v. 21):** These places refer to David's victories over the Philistines. Baal-Perazim is mentioned in 2 Samuel 5:20, while the event at Gibeon is found in 1 Chronicles 14:16 (see also Joshua 10:10–12 for an earlier event at Gibeon). Isaiah uses these historical locations to illustrate that God will now act against His own people—a "strange work"—in the same way He previously fought on their behalf.

## Events and Customs

**Drinking culture (v. 1, 7–8):** The description of drunk priests and prophets with tables full of vomit reflects an extravagant banquet culture among the Israelite upper class. Archaeological finds from this period, including luxury items and wine jars, confirm the wealth of the elite in both Samaria and Jerusalem. Isaiah criticizes religious leaders who are so intoxicated that they cannot carry out their duties to teach (v. 9) and judge fairly.

**Covenant with death (v. 15, 18):** The leaders of Jerusalem had likely entered into political alliances—probably with Egypt—which they relied on for protection against Assyria. Isaiah describes this trust in human alliances rather than God as a "covenant with death" and "a refuge of lies." This was a sharp critique of the political strategy under King Hezekiah.

## Key Terms

**"Tsav latsav, kav lakav" (v. 10, 13):** This Hebrew phrase (צַו לָצָו קַו לָקָו) has been much discussed. It may imitate stammering, unintelligible speech—either childlike language or the speech of drunkards—which the leaders used to mock Isaiah's repetitive preaching. God turns their mockery back on them: they will hear "unintelligible speech" when the Assyrians come (v. 11).

**Cornerstone (v. 16):** The Hebrew terms אֶבֶן בֹּחַן (tested stone) and פִּנַּת יִקְרַת (precious cornerstone) use building terminology to describe a sure foundation that God lays in Zion. This stands in contrast to the false security found in political alliances. The concept later became central to Jewish and Christian messianic interpretation.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

**Farming tools (v. 24–29):** Isaiah's detailed parable about the farmer who adapts his methods to different crops—black cumin (קֶצַח), cummin (כַמֹּן), wheat, barley, and spelt—reflects actual agricultural practices in ancient Israel. Archaeological discoveries of threshing sledges (חָרוּץ) and threshing wheels confirm the techniques Isaiah describes. The point is that God, like the wise farmer, adapts His discipline to the situation and does not continue punishment indefinitely.
