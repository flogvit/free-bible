# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 45

## People

**The Prince** (נָשִׂיא, *nasi*) appears as a central but clearly limited figure. His main responsibility is to finance the public offerings at the festivals (v. 17), but he shall no longer have power to seize the people's land (v. 8). This reflects a reaction against the abuses of pre-exilic kings, such as Ahab, who confiscated property from their subjects. The prince thus becomes more of a liturgical benefactor than an absolute ruler.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes an extensive allocation of land in which a "holy portion" (תְּרוּמָה, *terumah*) was to be set apart for the Lord. The concept of "lifting up" or "setting aside" (רום) a portion as holy was known from the sacrificial laws, but here it is applied to the land itself. The dimensions given (25,000 by 10,000 cubits) create a sacred district intended to house the temple, the priests, and the Levites. In contrast to the pre-exilic period, where royal and religious domains often came into conflict, this vision establishes clear boundaries between the prince's territory and the holy area.

The chapter also contains detailed regulations for just measures and weights. The criticism of Israel's leaders for "violence and oppression" (v. 9) and the command for "just scales" reflect frequent prophetic accusations against economic exploitation (cf. Amos 8:5, Micah 6:10-11). In the ancient Near East, standardized measures were a royal responsibility, and manipulation of weights was a widespread form of oppression.

The celebration of Passover (פֶּסַח, *pesach*) and the Feast of Tabernacles is described with sacrificial regulations that differ somewhat from the laws in the books of Moses. Passover is placed on the fourteenth day of the first month, followed by seven days of unleavened bread. Notably, Pentecost (the Feast of Weeks/שָׁבֻעוֹת) is not mentioned in this chapter. The festival of the seventh month (v. 25) corresponds to the Feast of Tabernacles, but Ezekiel's regulations for the number of sacrificial animals (seven bulls and seven rams daily) are more extensive than those in Numbers 29. The purification ritual on the first and seventh days of the first month (vv. 18-20) has no direct parallel in the Torah and seems to represent an expanded understanding of the sanctuary's need for cleansing.

## Key Terms

The word **נָשִׂיא** (*nasi*, "prince") is used consistently in Ezekiel's vision instead of **מֶלֶךְ** (*melekh*, "king"). This is likely intentional to mark a difference from the earlier monarchical model that had failed. The prince is given a clearly limited role—he is to provide for the offerings but has no access to the sanctuary itself.

**חֹמֶר** (*homer*) and **אֵיפָה** (*efa*) were standard measures: a homer corresponded to approximately 220-450 liters, while an efa was one-tenth of that amount. **בַּת** (*bat*) was the liquid measure corresponding to the efa for dry goods. The standardization of these measures in verses 10-12 emphasizes that justice in the restored nation begins with everyday commercial practices.
