## Places

### The Temple and Its Courts
The psalm is permeated with longing for the temple in Jerusalem, referred to as God’s "dwelling" (מִשְׁכְּנוֹתֶיךָ, *mishkenotekha*) and "courts" (חַצְרוֹת, *khatsrot*). The courts were the open areas surrounding the temple building itself where ordinary Israelites could gather for worship. The temple had an inner court and an outer court, and it was in these areas that people experienced fellowship with God and the Israelite community. Verse 4 also mentions "your altars" (מִזְבְּחוֹתֶיךָ, *mizbekhotekha*), which includes the altar of burnt offering in the court.

### The Valley of Baca
Verse 7 mentions the "Valley of Baca" (עֵמֶק הַבָּכָא, *emek ha-bakha*). The name may mean "valley of weeping" or "balsam valley" (after a tree that grew in dry areas). Its exact location is unknown, but the expression likely describes a dry and barren landscape that pilgrims had to cross on their way to Jerusalem. The passage depicts how the pilgrims turn this dry land into a spring, and how the early rain (מוֹרֶה, *moreh*) covers it with blessings.

## Events and Customs

### Pilgrimages to the Temple
The psalm reflects the Israelite custom of making pilgrimages to Jerusalem during the three major annual festivals: Passover, the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost), and the Feast of Tabernacles. The expression "they go from strength to strength" (verse 8) describes the pilgrims’ journey through the landscape toward Zion. Roads were often difficult and dangerous, but fellowship with other pilgrims and the expectation of meeting God in the temple gave them strength. The psalm’s strong emotional tone—longing, tears, joy—reflects the deep significance these pilgrimages held in Israelite piety.

## Key Terms

### "Gittith" (הַגִּתִּית)
The heading mentions *ha-gittit*, a musical term that likely refers to a melody or an instrument from Gath, one of the Philistine cities. Alternatively, it may be connected with winepressing (*gat* means winepress), which could indicate a festive character.

### "The Living God" (אֵל־חָי)
In verse 3, the expression *el khai* ("the living God") is used. This underscores the contrast to the lifeless idols of surrounding cultures and emphasizes that Israel’s God is active, present, and life-giving.

### "Your Anointed One" (מְשִׁיחֶךָ)
Verse 10 asks God to look upon "your anointed one" (*meshikhekha*). In its original context, this refers to the reigning king from David’s line, who was anointed with oil at his installation. The king had a special role as God’s representative and mediator between God and the people.
