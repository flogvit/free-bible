# Historical and Cultural Context for Colossians 4

## People

This chapter is unusually rich in personal names and offers a rare glimpse into the early Christian network around Paul. **Tychicus** (v. 7) was a faithful coworker from the province of Asia, also mentioned in Acts 20:4 and Ephesians 6:21. He served as Paul's letter carrier and personal representative. **Onesimus** (v. 9), described as "one of your own," was the runaway slave who is the central figure in the Letter to Philemon—his presence here confirms the connection between the two letters.

**Aristarchus** (v. 10) was from Thessalonica (Acts 20:4) and had accompanied Paul during the riots in Ephesus (Acts 19:29) and the journey to Rome (Acts 27:2). The term "fellow prisoner" (συναιχμάλωτος) could mean either literal imprisonment or a voluntary sharing of Paul's circumstances. **Mark** (v. 10), Barnabas's cousin, is likely the same person who had left Paul and Barnabas during their first missionary journey (Acts 13:13; 15:37–39). Paul's recommendation here shows reconciliation. **Jesus Justus** (v. 11) is otherwise unknown, but his double name (Hebrew and Latin) was common among Diaspora Jews. Paul notes that these three are the only Jewish Christians ("of the circumcision") among his close coworkers.

**Epaphras** (vv. 12–13) was probably the founder of the church in Colossae and also had responsibility for the churches in Laodicea and Hierapolis. **Luke** (v. 14), called "the beloved physician," is traditionally identified as the author of the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles. **Demas** (v. 14) is later mentioned negatively in 2 Timothy 4:10 as one who deserted Paul.

## Places

**Laodicea** (vv. 13, 15–16) lay only 16 kilometers west of Colossae in the Lycus Valley. The city was a prosperous commercial and banking center, known for its textile industry and medical school that produced eye salve. The close proximity explains the tight connection between the churches and the instruction to exchange letters. **Hierapolis** (v. 13), 10 kilometers north of Laodicea, was known for its hot springs and healing cults associated with the god Apollo. The fact that Epaphras worked among all three cities shows that the Christian communities in the Lycus Valley functioned as a network.

## Events and Customs

The instruction regarding letter exchange between Colossae and Laodicea (v. 16) provides valuable insight into how apostolic letters circulated in the early church. The letters were read aloud during the congregation's gathering (ἀναγνωσθῇ), reflecting both Jewish synagogue practice and the low literacy rates of antiquity. "The letter from Laodicea" mentioned has been the subject of much speculation—it may be a lost Pauline letter, or possibly what we know as Ephesians.

**Nympha and the house church** (v. 15) illustrates that early Christians met in private homes. The grammar of the verse is ambiguous regarding Nympha's gender—manuscripts vary between αὐτῆς (her), αὐτοῦ (his), and αὐτῶν (their). Several important manuscripts have the feminine form, which may suggest a woman who hosted a church—a significant leadership role in the early church.

## Key Terms

The expression **τὸ δίκαιον καὶ τὴν ἰσότητα** ("what is right and fair," v. 1) in the exhortation to slave owners is remarkable. The word ἰσότης (equality/fairness) was a rare concept applied to master-slave relationships and suggests a radical reevaluation of social relations within the Christian community, even though Paul does not directly challenge the institution of slavery.

**"Those outside" (τοὺς ἔξω)** (v. 5) was an established term in early Christianity for non-believers, borrowed from Jewish usage. The exhortation to "make the most of every opportunity" (τὸν καιρὸν ἐξαγοραζόμενοι) uses a commercial term and suggests wisely seizing opportunities when interacting with the outside world.

**Ἀσπασμὸς τῇ ἐμῇ χειρὶ** ("greeting in my own handwriting," v. 18) indicates that Paul used a secretary (amanuensis) for most of the letter but wrote the final greeting himself. This was common practice in antiquity and served as a form of authentication. The reference to his "chains" (δεσμῶν) confirms that the letter was written while he was imprisoned.
