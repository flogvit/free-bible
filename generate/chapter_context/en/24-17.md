# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 17

## Places

**The Gates of Jerusalem (vv. 19-27):** The chapter specifically mentions the "gate of the people" (שַׁעַר בְּנֵי־הָעָם) where the kings of Judah entered and exited, as well as all the gates of Jerusalem. City gates were not merely entrances to the city but served as central meeting places for commerce, legal proceedings, and public announcements. Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered remains of several gates from the Iron Age. The gate areas were natural locations for prophetic preaching because people from all walks of life passed through—from kings to ordinary laborers carrying goods.

**Geographic Regions of Judah (v. 26):** The text describes four regions bringing offerings to the temple: the cities of Judah, the areas around Jerusalem, the land of Benjamin, the lowland (שְׁפֵלָה/Shefela), the hill country, and the Negev. This reflects the administrative division of the kingdom of Judah and shows that the Sabbath message applied to the entire nation, not just the capital city.

## Events and Customs

**Sabbath Trading at the City Gates (vv. 21-27):** The prohibition against carrying burdens (מַשָּׂא) on the Sabbath and bringing them through the gates testifies to widespread commerce on the day of rest. In the ancient Near East, weekly market days were common, and Jerusalem as a religious center attracted merchants. The prophet's exhortation indicates that economic activity had displaced Sabbath observance. This problem persisted and reappeared in Nehemiah 13:15-22 after the exile.

**Asherah Worship and High Place Cults (vv. 1-3):** The mention of altars (מִזְבְּחוֹת) and Asherah poles (אֲשֵׁרִים) by green trees on high places reflects the syncretistic religious practice that characterized Judah. Asherah was a Canaanite goddess often worshiped alongside Baal, and her cult involved sacred wooden poles or living trees. Archaeological finds from Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom have uncovered inscriptions mentioning "Yahweh and his Asherah," confirming that such mixed worship was widespread in Israel and Judah.

## Key Terms

**"Written with an iron stylus, with a diamond point" (v. 1):** The Hebrew expression בְּעֵט בַּרְזֶל בְּצִפֹּרֶן שָׁמִיר describes writing tools: an iron stylus (עֵט בַּרְזֶל) and a point of "shamir" (צִפֹּרֶן שָׁמִיר), likely a very hard mineral such as emery (corundum). This metaphor expresses that Judah's sin is permanently engraved, impossible to erase. Iron styluses were used to carve inscriptions into stone or clay tablets, and the image communicates the indelible nature of sin.

**"The Partridge" (v. 11):** The bird קֹרֵא (qore) is likely a partridge or sandgrouse. The verb דָּגַר means "to gather/sit on eggs," and יָלָד means "to give birth/hatch." The image describes a bird that gathers eggs but does not hatch them—they never become its own. This illustrates how wealth acquired unjustly eventually disappears. Partridges were common in the desert regions of Judea, making this imagery familiar to the listeners.

**"The Deceitfulness of the Heart" (vv. 9-10):** The word עָקֹב ("deceitful, treacherous") is used regarding the heart and shares the same root as the name Jacob (יַעֲקֹב), which means "he who grasps the heel" or "deceiver." Hebrew לֵב (heart) referred not primarily to emotions but to the will, understanding, and inner decision-making center. The term "kidneys" (כְּלָיוֹת), which God tests, was considered the seat of the deepest motives and feelings.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Seals and Inscriptions with Sabbath References:** Although direct archaeological evidence for Sabbath violations is difficult to find, ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from Arad and Lachish have been discovered that document trade and supply activities in Judah during this period. These administrative texts reveal the active commercial life that the prophet criticizes.

**Altar Fragments with Horns:** Verse 1 mentions "the horns of your altars" (קַרְנוֹת מִזְבְּחוֹתֵיכֶם). Archaeologists have found several horned altars from the Iron Age in Israel and Judah, including at Be'er-Sheva and Megiddo. The horns were the most sacred parts of the altar, and the fact that sin is engraved there underscores how deeply idolatry had penetrated worship.
