# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 7

## Persons

The superscription mentions **Cush, son of Benjamin** (כּוּשׁ בֶּן־יְמִינִי), a person not mentioned elsewhere in the Bible. The name "Cush" means "Ethiopian" or "dark" and could be either a proper name or a nickname. The expression "son of Benjamin" (בֶּן־יְמִינִי) is the same designation used for King Saul and his clan (1 Sam 9:1; 2 Sam 16:11). Many scholars have therefore suggested that Cush may be one of Saul's supporters who pursued David, or possibly a code name for Saul himself or someone in his close circle. The historical event to which the psalm refers is not preserved in the biblical narratives, but the context fits the period when David fled from Saul.

## Key Terms

**Shiggaion** (שִׁגָּיוֹן) is a rare musical or literary term that appears only here in the book of Psalms (and in the plural in Hab 3:1). The meaning of the word is uncertain, but it may be derived from the root שׁגה, which means "to wander" or "to stagger," leading to interpretations such as "lament," "passionate song," or a psalm with an irregular rhythm expressing strong emotions.

**Selah** (סֶלָה) in verse 6 is a liturgical marker whose exact meaning is debated. It may indicate a musical pause, an interlude, or a call to reflection.

## Events and Customs

The structure of the psalm reflects an ancient Near Eastern **declaration of innocence** or oath of cleansing. The psalmist calls down a curse upon himself if he is guilty (vv. 4–6), a practice known from legal procedures in the ancient Middle East. This form of self-cursing also appears in other biblical texts (Job 31) and in Mesopotamian texts where the accused swears their innocence before a deity acting as judge.

Verses 13–14 describe the preparation of deadly weapons—sword, bow, and arrows. The subject in these verses is debated: some interpret it as God preparing judgment against the wicked, while others see it as the wicked person themselves preparing an attack. The latter interpretation is supported by the context of vv. 15–17, which describes how the plans of the evil one turn back upon himself. The expression לְדֹלְקִים יִפְעָל (v. 14) can be translated as "he makes [the arrows] burning" or "he makes [the arrows] for pursuers," since the Hebrew דֹּלְקִים can be derived from both "to burn" and "to pursue."
