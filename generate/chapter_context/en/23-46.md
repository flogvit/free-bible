# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 46

## People

The chapter mentions two Babylonian deities: **Bel** (בֵּל) and **Nebo** (נְבוֹ). Bel (from Akkadian Bēlu) was an honorific title meaning "lord" that was used for Marduk, the chief god and national deity of Babylon. In Babylonian mythology, Marduk was the creator god and regarded as the king of the gods. **Nebo** (Akkadian Nabu) was Marduk's son, the god of wisdom and writing. Nabu grew increasingly popular during the Neo-Babylonian period, and several royal names included his name—such as Nebuchadnezzar (Nabu-kudurri-usur, "Nabu, protect my boundary") and Nabonidus. These two gods represented the pinnacle of the Babylonian pantheon, and Isaiah's polemic therefore targets the very heart of Babylonian religion.

## Events and Customs

The chapter directly alludes to the Babylonian **Akitu festival** (New Year celebration), during which cult images of Marduk and Nebo were carried in procession through the streets of Babylon. Verses 1–2 and 7 describe how the gods must be "carried" (נְשֻׂאֹתֵיכֶם עֲמוּסוֹת)—an image that turns the festival's grandeur upside down. Instead of a triumphant procession, the prophet envisions the gods loaded onto exhausted animals during flight or deportation. Verses 6–7 also describe the **making of cult images**: gold and silver are weighed out, a goldsmith (צוֹרֵף) is hired, the image is set in place—and there it stands motionless. This accurately reflects the documented process for creating divine statues in Mesopotamia.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **עַיִט** (ajit, "bird of prey") in verse 11 is used as a metaphor for a conqueror from the east. In the context of Isaiah 40–48, this points to Cyrus the Great of Persia. The bird of prey was a common symbol for swift military conquerors in ancient Near Eastern literature, and images of birds of prey were frequent in Persian royal iconography. The contrast between the living God who "carries" (נָשָׂא/אֶשָּׂא) his people from the womb to old age (verses 3–4), and the dead idols that must be carried by humans and animals, forms the theological core of the chapter. The verb סָבַל ("to bear a burden") is deliberately used for both God and the idols to emphasize this contrast.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries confirm details in Isaiah's description. From Babylon and Nineveh, numerous reliefs and texts have been found describing divine processions, in which cult images were carried on special litters. The Nabonidus Chronicle (c. 540 BC) actually documents that cult images from several Babylonian cities were transported to Babylon just before Cyrus's conquest—possibly for protection. The Cyrus Cylinder (539 BC) states that Cyrus returned these divine statues to their original temples. These texts provide a striking historical background for Isaiah's vision of Babylonian gods going "into captivity" (verse 2: וְנַפְשָׁם בַּשְּׁבִי הָלָכָה).
