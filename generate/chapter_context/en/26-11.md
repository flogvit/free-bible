# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 11

## People

The chapter names two specific leaders: **Jaazaniah son of Azur** (יַאֲזַנְיָה בֶן־עַזֻּר) and **Pelatiah son of Benaiah**, both described as "leaders of the people" (שָׂרֵי הָעָם). These were likely members of Jerusalem’s advisory council who held significant political influence in the years before the city fell. The Jaazaniah mentioned here must not be confused with Jaazaniah son of Shaphan, who is mentioned in Ezekiel 8:11. Pelatiah’s sudden death during Ezekiel’s prophecy (verse 13) underscored the seriousness of God’s judgment and caused deep horror in the prophet. The fact that these leaders were gathered at the east gate of the temple indicates they held an official position with access to the temple area.

## Places

**The East Gate** of the temple was the main entrance and held special significance as the place where God’s glory traditionally entered. That the twenty-five men gathered there suggests either council meetings or an ironic contrast between their wickedness and a holy place. **The mountain east of the city** (verse 23), where God’s glory stopped, is the Mount of Olives. This mountain lies directly east of the temple complex, separated by the Kidron Valley, and offers a view over all of Jerusalem. The fact that the glory paused here before leaving the city represents an intermediate stage in God’s gradual withdrawal from His people.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the pot and the meat"** (הַסִּיר וְהַבָּשָׂר) in verse 3 is a proverb used by the leaders. They saw themselves as the valuable meat protected by the pot (Jerusalem), safe from danger. God turns this image upside down: those killed in the city are the real "meat," while the leaders themselves will be cast out of the "pot" (verse 7).

The concept **"a sanctuary for a little while"** (לְמִקְדָּשׁ מְעַט) in verse 16 is theologically groundbreaking. It suggests that God Himself will serve as a kind of sanctuary for those taken into exile, even without the temple—a radical idea that laid the foundation for later Jewish understanding of God’s presence outside the land.

The promise of **"one heart"** and **"a heart of flesh"** instead of **"a heart of stone"** (verse 19) uses Hebrew terms that distinguish between an insensitive, disobedient heart (stone) and a receptive, obedient heart (flesh/living tissue). This points toward an inner renewal that makes true covenant obedience possible.

## Events and Customs

The attitude of Jerusalem’s leaders toward the exiles (verse 15) reflects a widespread theology at that time: that exile was God’s punishment, and those who remained in the land were the "righteous" who inherited the promises. The exiles were regarded as "removed from the Lord." Ezekiel completely reverses this perspective by declaring that it is among the exiles that God will carry out His restoring work.
