# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 26

## Events and Customs

### Firstfruits Ritual (bikkurim)
The chapter describes the firstfruits offering, called in Hebrew *bikkurim*. This ritual required Israelites to bring the first ripe fruits of the harvest to the sanctuary. According to the Mishnah (Bikkurim 1:3), this applied especially to the seven species for which the land of Canaan was known: wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and honey (dates). The farmer was to place the fruits in a basket (*tene*) and present them to the priest. Similar firstfruits offerings were widespread throughout the ancient Near East, where people expressed gratitude to their deity for the harvest and sought continued fertility.

### The Historical Confession (verses 5–10)
The liturgical text to be recited is often called "the little creed" or "the historical confession." This is one of the oldest concise retellings of Israel's salvation history in the Old Testament. The text follows a fixed pattern: the patriarch as a wandering Aramean, slavery in Egypt, deliverance, and the gift of the land. Such ritual recitation of the people's history was common in ancient Near Eastern cultic practice and served to anchor each worshiper's identity within the community's story.

### The Tithe Declaration (verses 12–15)
Every third year, the tithe was to be given locally to Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows, rather than brought to the central sanctuary. The Israelite was then to make a solemn declaration that the tithe had been handled correctly. This "oath of purity" confirms that none of the holy portion had been eaten in mourning (*be'oni*), used in an unclean way, or given to the dead. The last point may refer to pagan customs where food was offered to ancestors or gods of the underworld, a practice widespread in Canaanite and Egyptian religion.

## Key Terms

### "A wandering Aramean was my father" (*arami oved avi*)
The expression in verse 5 is debated. The Hebrew *oved* can mean "wandering," "lost," or "on the way to ruin." Tradition has understood this as a reference to the patriarch Jacob, who had connections to Aram through his uncle Laban and his wives. The term "Aramean" points to the ancestors' origin in the Aramean region (Paddan-Aram/Haran) before they settled in Canaan.

### "A people for his own possession" (*am segulla*)
The term *segulla* in verse 18 denotes a special, valuable possession. In Akkadian texts, the related word *sikiltum* refers to the king's personal treasure or private property, as distinct from the kingdom's general resources. When Israel is called God's *segulla*, it expresses that the people hold a unique status as God's precious possession among all the peoples of the earth.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of baskets from ancient Israel confirm the use of woven containers for transporting agricultural products. Inscriptions from the Second Temple in Jerusalem mention *bikkurim* processions, and the Mishnah tractate Bikkurim provides detailed descriptions of how pilgrims came to the temple with baskets decorated with fruit. Ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from Samaria and Arad dating to the 700s BCE document tithe systems and deliveries to cultic centers, confirming that such levy systems were well established in Israelite society.
