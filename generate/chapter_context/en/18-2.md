# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 2

## Characters

**Job’s wife** is mentioned for the first time in this chapter, but remains unnamed throughout the book. In the ancient Near East, a woman’s identity was often tied to her husband and children. Her urging of Job to "curse God and die" (v. 9)—where "curse" (בָּרֵךְ, *barekh*) is a euphemism for "curse"—perhaps reflects desperation over having lost all her children and seeing her husband suffer. Some Jewish traditions give her the name Sitidos or Dina, but this has no biblical basis.

**The three friends** are introduced: Eliphaz from Teman, Bildad from Shuhah, and Zophar from Naamah (צוֹפַר הַנַּעֲמָתִי). Teman was known as a center of wisdom in Edom (Jer 49:7), and Eliphaz thus represents the Edomite wisdom tradition. Shuhah may be linked to a tribe mentioned in Gen 25:2 as descendants of Abraham and Keturah. The location of Naamah is uncertain, but it may lie in northwest Arabia. All three come from areas east or southeast of Israel, which underscores the book’s non-Israelite setting.

## Events and Customs

**Mourning rituals** described in verses 12–13 were widespread in the ancient Near East: tearing one’s clothes, throwing dust on the head toward the sky, and sitting silently on the ground. The seven-day period of silence (later known as *shiva* in Jewish mourning tradition) has its roots in such ancient customs. Sitting in ashes (v. 8) symbolized deep humility and grief. These rituals are also attested in both Mesopotamian and Canaanite cultures.

## Key Terms

**"Skin for skin"** (עוֹר בְּעַד־עוֹר, *ʿor beʿad-ʿor*, v. 4) is a proverb likely originating from barter trade, where animal skins were valuable commodities. Its meaning is that a person will give up everything to protect their own life.

**"Boils"** (שְׁחִין רָע, *sheḥin raʿ*, v. 7) describes a severe skin disease. The same Hebrew word is used for the boil plague in Egypt (Exod 9:9) and Hezekiah’s illness (2 Kgs 20:7). The exact diagnosis is unknown, but the symptoms—itching that requires scraping with broken pottery shards—suggest a painful, widespread skin condition.

**"One of the foolish women"** (אַחַת הַנְּבָלוֹת, *aḥat hannevalot*, v. 10) uses the word *naval*, which denotes moral and spiritual folly rather than intellectual weakness. This term is associated with godlessness and appears in the story of Nabal (1 Sam 25).
