# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 40

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical references with symbolic meaning. **Jerusalem** (verses 2, 9) stands central as the recipient of the message of comfort. The **wilderness** (מִדְבָּר, *midbar*) and the **desert plain** (עֲרָבָה, *aravah*) in verses 3–4 carry both literal and symbolic significance. Geographically, they may refer to the desert region between Babylon and Judah—the area exiles had to cross to return home. This was the same route used by trade caravans traveling between Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean coast. **Lebanon** (verse 16) was known for its cedar trees and abundant wildlife, and here serves as an example of the most lavish offerings humans could provide—yet even these would be insufficient as a sacrifice to God.

## Key Terms

The chapter opens with the double imperative **נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ** (*nachamu nachamu*, "comfort, comfort"), where repetition expresses intensity and urgency. The form is plural imperative, indicating that multiple messengers (possibly the heavenly assembly) are commissioned to comfort God’s people. The word implies not only emotional consolation but also restoration and renewed strength. The term **צְבָאָהּ** (*tseva'ah*, verse 2) is often translated as "struggle" or "service," and may refer either to military service or forced labor—both relevant to the experiences of exile.

The expression **כִּפְלַיִם** (*kiflayim*, "double") in verse 2 has been widely discussed. It could mean that Jerusalem has received double punishment, or it may refer to a legal document that was "folded double" once a debt was paid—indicating that the debt is now settled.

The phrase **קוֹל קוֹרֵא** (*qol qore'*, "a voice crying out") in verse 3 was later interpreted messianically and is quoted in all four Gospels in connection with John the Baptist. The expression **חוּג הָאָרֶץ** (*chug ha'arets*, "the circle of the earth") in verse 22 reflects the cosmology of that time, where the earth was imagined as a flat disk with the sky stretched over it like a tent.

## Events and Customs

The image of road preparation in verses 3–4 reflects an ancient custom in which workers were sent ahead to improve roads before a king’s arrival. This included filling valleys, leveling hills, and straightening curves. Several Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions describe such road-building projects in connection with military campaigns or royal processions.

The description of idol production in verses 19–20 offers insight into the craftsmanship traditions of that era. The process involved several specialized artisans: a metalworker (חָרָשׁ, *charash*) cast the core, a goldsmith hammered out a gold overlay, and a silversmith made silver chains to secure and stabilize the statue. For poorer worshippers (verse 20), wood that would not rot was used—likely acacia or cedar.
