# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 6

## Places

The chapter opens with a command to prophesy against "the mountains of Israel" (הָרֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל), a designation that in this context encompasses the entire land as the recipient of the prophecy. Verse 14 mentions a geographical extreme: "from the wilderness to Diblah" (מִמִּדְבַּר דִּבְלָתָה). Most scholars believe this is a scribal variation of Riblah, a strategically important city in the Hamath region of Syria, where Nebuchadnezzar had his headquarters and among other things judged King Zedekiah (2 Kings 25:6–7). The expression thus describes the total devastation of the land from the south (the wilderness) to the north (Riblah).

## Events and Customs

The chapter strongly criticizes high-place worship (בָּמוֹת, *bamot*), cult sites often located on heights and hills, which were linked to Canaanite-inspired idolatry. These places included altars, incense altars, and sacred trees. Verse 13 describes typical cult sites: "on every high hill, on all the mountain tops, under every green tree, and under every leafy terebinth (אֵלָה עֲבֻתָּה)." This corresponds with descriptions in Deuteronomy 12:2 and reflects syncretistic practice where Israelites mixed Yahweh worship with Canaanite fertility cults. Archaeological finds from cult sites in Israel and Judah confirm the widespread nature of such high-place sanctuaries in the Iron Age.

## Key Terms

The word **גִּלּוּלִים** (*gillulim*, "idols") occurs six times in this chapter (verses 4, 5, 6, 9, and 13 [twice]). The word is likely a derogatory term derived from the root גלל which can mean "dung" or "excrement," expressing the prophet's contempt for idols.

**חַמָּנִים** (*ḥammanim*, verses 4 and 6) denotes "sun pillars" or "incense altars." Archaeological finds from Phoenician and Canaanite cult sites have uncovered small stone altars that likely served this purpose, often associated with sun worship or Baal cult.
