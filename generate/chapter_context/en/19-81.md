# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 81

## People

Verse 6 uses the unusual form "Jehoseph" (יְהוֹסֵף) for Joseph, a poetic or archaic name form in which the divine name (יְהוֹ-) is embedded. This links Israel's stay in Egypt directly to the story of Joseph and emphasizes God's sovereign guidance throughout history.

## Events and Customs

Psalm 81 is closely connected to Israel's festival calendar, especially the new moon festival and the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot). Verse 4 mentions blowing the shofar "at the new moon" (בַחֹדֶשׁ) and "at the full moon" (בַּכֵּסֶה), pointing toward the festivals of the seventh month (Tishri). According to Leviticus 23:24, the first day of this month was to be marked with shofar blowing, and the Feast of Tabernacles began at the full moon on the fifteenth day. The Feast of Tabernacles was a pilgrimage festival in which the people remembered their deliverance from Egypt and their journey through the wilderness—themes that stand central in this psalm.

The second part of the psalm (verses 6–17) has the character of a prophetic speech in which God Himself speaks through a Levitical singer or prophet. This form of "divine oracle" within worship was an established practice in the temple, where priests or prophets could convey God's word directly to the assembly.

## Places

The psalm mentions "the waters of Meribah" (מֵי מְרִיבָה) in verse 8, referring to the event described in Exodus 17:1–7 and Numbers 20:1–13, where God gave the people water from the rock in the wilderness. The name means "waters of strife" or "waters of contention" (from רִיב, to strive) and recalls Israel's complaints against God despite His care. Egypt (מִצְרָיִם) is mentioned twice (verses 6 and 11) as the place of slavery from which God delivered His people.

## Key Terms

The word "Gittith" (הַגִּתִּית) in the heading is disputed. It may refer to a musical instrument from Gath, a particular melody, or possibly to the winepress (גַּת), thus linking the psalm to the harvest festival. The expression "pot" (דּוּד) in verse 7 refers to a basket or pot, likely carrying baskets used for heavy labor during slavery in Egypt—a concrete image of the slave labor from which the Israelites were freed. The phrasing "open your mouth wide" (הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ) in verse 11 uses the image of a young bird opening its beak to be fed, expressing God's desire to provide abundantly for His people.
