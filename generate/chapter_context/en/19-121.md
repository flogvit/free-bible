# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 121

## Places

The psalm opens with a gaze toward "the mountains" (הֶהָרִים, *heharim*), an image carrying multiple layers of possible meaning. For pilgrims traveling up to Jerusalem, the mountains were both a concrete geographical reality and a symbol. Jerusalem lies in the Judean hill country, about 750 meters above sea level, and the temple was located on Mount Zion. At the same time, in Canaanite religion, mountains were often places of idolatrous worship at "the high places," giving the question "From where does my help come?" a polemical undertone. The psalmist rejects mountain shrines and affirms that help comes from the LORD alone.

## Events and Customs

As one of the "Songs of Ascents" (שִׁיר לַמַּעֲלוֹת, *shir lama'alot*), this psalm was likely sung by pilgrims on their way to the three major pilgrimage festivals in Jerusalem: Passover, Weeks, and Tabernacles. The journey was often dangerous, with steep paths, robbers, and extreme weather conditions. The psalm's focus on protection during travel—day and night, going out and coming in—reflects the real dangers pilgrims faced. It was also common for priests or Levites to bless pilgrims upon departure or arrival, and the psalm's dialogical form (verses 1–2 personal, verses 3–8 addressing) may reflect such a liturgical practice.

## Key Terms

The verb **שָׁמַר** (*shamar*, "to keep/watch over") appears six times in this short psalm and forms its theological core. The word carries connotations of a guard, a shepherd protecting the flock, or a gatekeeper. The expression **צֵאתְךָ וּבוֹאֶךָ** (*tsetekha uvo'ekha*, "your going out and your coming in") is a merism—a figure of speech that uses two opposites to express totality. It covers all movements and activities of life. The image of God as **צִל** (*tsel*, "shadow") at the right hand draws on the warm climate of Israel, where shade was essential protection against the scorching sun.
