## People

**Adoni-Bezek** (vv. 5–7) was a Canaanite king who ruled over Bezek. His name means "Lord of Bezek" and was a common title for local rulers in Canaan. His own confession of having mutilated seventy kings by cutting off their thumbs and big toes reveals the brutal power struggles among petty kings in the region. The number "seventy" may be a round figure indicating "very many," which was common in Semitic rhetoric.

**Caleb** (vv. 12–15, 20) was a prominent figure from the conquest period. He was of Kenite origin (Joshua 14:6), showing that non-Israelites could be incorporated into Israel. His allocation of Hebron is confirmed as the fulfillment of Moses' promise (Numbers 14:24; Joshua 14:6–14). **Othniel's** conquest of Debir and marriage to Achsah are repeated from Joshua 15:16–19, establishing him as an important figure—he later becomes Israel's first judge (Judges 3:9).

**The Kenites** (v. 16) were descendants of Moses' father-in-law. The Hebrew text says חֹתֵן מֹשֶׁה (Moses' father-in-law), traditionally identified with Hobab or Jethro. They maintained a nomadic lifestyle and allied themselves with Judah, which explains their later role in Judges (4:11, 17).

## Places

**Bezek** (vv. 4–5) was likely located in the central highlands, perhaps near Shechem. This place must not be confused with Bezek in 1 Samuel 11:8, which lay farther north.

**Hebron** (vv. 10, 20), formerly called Kiriath-Arba ("City of Arba"), was one of the oldest and most important cities in the Judean highlands. The city was associated with the Anakim, a people group described as giants (Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai, v. 10).

**Debir** (vv. 11–12), formerly called Kiriath-Sepher ("City of Books"), has been suggested to be identified with Tell Beit Mirsim or Khirbet Rabud southwest of Hebron, but the identification remains uncertain. The name "City of Books" may suggest that the place was an administrative or religious center.

**Jerusalem** (vv. 7–8, 21) is mentioned as conquered by Judah (v. 8), but the Jebusites remained living there (v. 21). This apparent contradiction is explained by the fact that Judah carried out a raid without permanent occupation, while the city lay within the territory of the tribe of Benjamin.

**The City of Palms** (v. 16) was the ancient name for Jericho, known for its date palm oases. The Kenites used this as a starting point before settling in the Negev.

**Bethel/Luz** (vv. 22–26) lay in the territory of Ephraim and was an important cult site from the patriarchal period. Bethel is traditionally identified with Beitin, although some scholars propose alternative locations.

## Events and Customs

**The practice of mutilation** (vv. 6–7), where thumbs and big toes were cut off, was a known custom in the ancient Near East. The purpose was to disable warriors (without thumbs one could not grasp a sword; without big toes balance and running were difficult) without killing them. Adoni-Bezek's own practice of letting kings "gather crumbs under the table" was a humiliating treatment of defeated enemies.

**"Herem"** or ban (v. 17) was a religious practice in which conquered cities and their inhabitants were devoted to total destruction as an offering to God. The city of Zephath then received the name Hormah ("Destruction").

## Key Terms

**Iron chariots** (רֶכֶב בַּרְזֶל, v. 19) represented advanced military technology that the Israelites could not overcome. Iron technology was relatively new in this period, and the Canaanites on the plains had access to this technology, giving them significant military superiority.

**"Mas"** (מַס, vv. 28, 30, 33, 35) denotes forced labor or tribute. Instead of driving out the Canaanites completely, the Israelites subjected them to service—a practice that broke with the Lord's command and which the chapter implicitly criticizes.

**Gullot mayim** (גֻּלֹּת מַיִם, v. 15) means "springs of water" or "pools." In the dry Negev region, access to water was crucial for agriculture and survival, which explains Achsah's bold request.
