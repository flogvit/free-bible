# Historical and Cultural Context for Luke 20

## People

The chapter presents several distinct groups confronting Jesus. **Chief priests** (ἀρχιερεῖς) constituted the temple's highest leadership, including the current high priest and former high priests who still held influence. **Scribes** (γραμματεῖς) were experts in the Law of Moses and served as legal advisors. **Elders** (πρεσβύτεροι) represented prominent laypeople from influential families. Together, these three groups formed the Sanhedrin, the Jewish council of 70 or 71 members that had limited self-governance under Roman rule.

**Sadducees** (v. 27) were an aristocratic religious group with close ties to the temple priesthood. They accepted only the five books of Moses as authoritative and rejected belief in resurrection, angels, and spirits. This theological position made them natural opponents of the Pharisees and placed them in conflict with Jesus' teaching.

## Places

The events take place in **the temple** (τὸ ἱερόν, v. 1), specifically in the public courts where teaching was permitted. The temple complex functioned as Jerusalem's religious, economic, and political center. Solomon's Colonnade and other areas of the outer court were common locations for rabbinic debate and instruction. It was here that Jesus taught the people daily during the week before Passover.

## Events and Customs

**Levirate marriage** (v. 28) was an ancient Israelite custom rooted in Deuteronomy 25:5-6. When a man died childless, his brother was to marry the widow to secure the deceased's name and property through an heir. This practice was still known in Jesus' time. The Sadducees' hypothetical example involving seven brothers was likely a standard argument they used against belief in resurrection.

**The Parable of the Tenants** (vv. 9-16) builds on a well-known practice where landowners leased estates to farmers who paid part of the harvest as rent. At harvest time, the owner sent servants to collect his share. The image of Israel as God's vineyard was deeply rooted in prophetic tradition, especially Isaiah 5:1-7, and listeners would have immediately understood the reference.

## Key Terms

**Ἐξουσία (exousia)** – "authority/power" – is a central word at the beginning of the chapter. The question of who gave Jesus his authority concerned religious legitimacy: Did his authority come from God, from human appointment, or did he seize it unlawfully?

**Δηνάριον (denarion)** – the Roman silver coin shown in the question about taxes. The coin bore the emperor's image (εἰκών) and inscription (ἐπιγραφή), which typically included the emperor's titles.

**Κεφαλὴν γωνίας (cornerstone)** – the quotation from Psalm 118:22 refers to the main stone that holds a building together, either as a foundation or capstone. This was a well-known messianic text in Jewish tradition.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries of denarii from Tiberius' reign (14-37 AD) confirm the gospel's description. These coins show the emperor's portrait with the inscription "TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS" (Tiberius Caesar Augustus, son of the divine Augustus). Such coins have been found in large numbers in Israel and illustrate the theological dilemma created by the question about taxes: Using the coin implied at one level acceptance of the emperor's divine claims, while refusing to pay tax was rebellion against Rome.
