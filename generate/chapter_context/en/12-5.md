# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 5

## Characters

**Naaman** (נַעֲמָן) was the commander of the army of Aram (Syria). The title "שַׂר־צְבָא" (sar-tsava, "commander of the army") denotes the highest military position in the kingdom. That the text states "through him the Lord gave victory to Aram" (v. 1) is theologically remarkable – the God of Israel is acknowledged as active even through foreign nations. Naaman's leprosy (צָרַעַת) did not exclude him from service in Aram as it would have done in Israel, showing differences in purity laws between cultures.

**Gehazi** (גֵּיחֲזִי) was Elisha's servant and appears several times in the stories about Elisha. In this chapter, he represents the contrast between the prophet's integrity and the servant's greed.

## Places

**Damascus** was the capital of Aram and an important trading center. The rivers **Amana/Abana** (אֲמָנָה/אַבָנָה) and **Parpar** (פַרְפַּר) that Naaman mentions (v. 12) can be identified with the Barada River (ancient Chrysorrhoas) and its tributaries flowing from the Anti-Lebanon mountains through Damascus. These rivers were known for their clear, fresh water – in sharp contrast to the muddy, brackish waters of the Jordan.

**Ophel** (הָעֹפֶל) in verse 24 refers to a fortified height or hidden place where Gehazi could hide his plunder safely.

## Events and Customs

**Diplomatic protocol** is illustrated by Naaman arriving with a letter from his king to the king of Israel (vv. 5-6). The gifts brought – ten talents of silver (about 340 kg), six thousand shekels of gold, and ten changes of clothing – represented enormous wealth and were standard in diplomatic approaches. That the king of Israel tore his clothes (v. 7) shows his despair over what he perceived as an impossible request or a provocation of war.

**Ritual aspects** of the healing include the symbolic number seven (שֶׁבַע פְּעָמִים) for dipping in the Jordan. Naaman's expectation that the prophet should "come out, stand there, call on the name of the Lord and wave his hand over the spot" (v. 11) reflects how healing rituals were typically performed in the ancient Near East, with dramatic gestures and incantations.

## Key Terms

**Rimmon** (רִמּוֹן) in verse 18 is the Aramean storm and weather god, also known as Hadad-Rimmon. His temple in Damascus was a central place of worship. Naaman's dilemma – having to bow in this temple as part of his official duties while supporting the king – illustrates the practical difficulties for monotheistic faith in a polytheistic society.

**"Enough earth for two mules to carry"** (v. 17) reflects the ancient belief that deities were tied to their land. Naaman wanted Israelite soil so he could worship the God of Israel on proper ground when he returned home – an understanding of divinity that was territorially bound.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Damascus have uncovered remains of the great Hadad temple (Rimmon's temple) beneath the present Umayyad Mosque, confirming the existence of the place of worship Naaman mentions. Assyrian inscriptions from this period also mention Aram's military strength and confirm the historical context of repeated conflicts between Aram and Israel.
