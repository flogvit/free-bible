# Chapter-Specific Context for 2 Samuel 13

## Characters

**Amnon** was David’s firstborn son, born to Ahinoam of Jezreel (2 Sam 3:2). As the eldest son, he held a prominent position in the line of succession to the throne. **Tamar** was the daughter of David and Maacah, princess from Geshur, and thus Amnon’s half sister. Her name means "palm tree" and was associated with beauty and fruitfulness. **Absalom** was Tamar’s full brother, also born to Maacah (2 Sam 3:3). **Jonadab**, the son of David’s brother Shimeah, is described as "very wise" (חָכָם מְאֹד), but his "wisdom" proves to be cunning manipulation rather than true wisdom.

**Talmai, son of Ammihud** was king of Geshur and Absalom’s maternal grandfather (v. 37). This explains why Absalom fled specifically there—he had family ties and could expect protection.

## Places

**Baal-hazor** (v. 23) is usually identified with modern Tell Asur, about 25 kilometers north of Jerusalem, near the border of Ephraim’s territory. The place was situated on a hill (1016 meters above sea level) and was well suited for sheep farming. That Absalom held a sheep-shearing feast there indicates that he owned considerable land in this district. **Geshur** was a small Aramean kingdom east of the Sea of Galilee, in today’s Golan Heights. It lay outside Israelite jurisdiction, making it a safe refuge for Absalom.

## Events and Customs

**Sheep shearing** (גֹזְזִים) was an important annual event in ancient Israel, associated with celebration and festivity (cf. 1 Sam 25:2–8). It was a time when wealth became visible and hospitality was expected. Inviting the royal family to a sheep-shearing feast was a significant gesture that signaled prosperity and honor.

**Preparation of special food for the sick** (vv. 5–8): The Hebrew word לְבִבוֹת (levivot, "heart cakes") refers to a special type of baked good, possibly heart-shaped or prepared in a particular way to strengthen a sick person. The custom of family members personally preparing food for the sick was widespread and emphasized care and closeness.

**The garment** described in verse 18 – כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים (ketonet passim) – is the same type of special robe that Joseph wore (Gen 37:3). This was a long-sleeved robe worn by royal virgin daughters, symbolizing both high status and virginity. That Tamar tears this garment dramatically signals the loss of both.

## Key Terms

**נְבָלָה** (nevalah, "outrageous act," v. 12) is a strong expression in Hebrew denoting actions that violate fundamental moral norms in Israel. The word is used especially concerning sexual abuse (cf. Gen 34:7; Judg 19:23–24; 20:6) and implies that the act is an offense not only against the victim but against the entire community.

**עִנָּה** (innah, "humiliated/raped," vv. 14, 22, 32) is the technical Hebrew term for sexual violation. The law in Deuteronomy 22:28–29 required that a man who raped an unmarried woman must marry her and never be allowed to divorce her. Tamar’s suggestion in verse 13 that Amnon should ask the king for her hand may reflect her attempt to find a solution within this framework—or it could have been a desperate strategy to delay the assault.

**שֹׁמֵמָה** (shomemah, "desolate/abandoned," v. 20) describes Tamar’s condition after the assault. The word is otherwise used for desolate places and ruins, suggesting that Tamar lived the rest of her life in social isolation, with no possibility of marriage.
