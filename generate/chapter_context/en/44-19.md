# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 19

## Places

**Ephesus** was one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire, with an estimated population of 200,000–250,000 inhabitants. The city served as the capital of the Roman province of Asia and was a significant center for trade and religion. Ephesus was strategically located on the coast of western Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey) and was known for its famous Temple of Artemis, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Paul's extended ministry there (over two years according to verse 10) made the city a crucial hub for spreading the gospel throughout the region.

**Tyrannus' school** (verse 9, Greek: σχολῇ Τυράννου) was likely a lecture hall or philosophical school where Paul taught daily. According to a Western text variant, teaching took place "from the fifth hour until the tenth" (about 11 a.m.–4 p.m.), which was the hottest part of the day when people took a break from work. This was a common arrangement in antiquity where philosophers and teachers rented rooms for instruction.

**The theater at Ephesus** (verse 29) was the city's large amphitheater, capable of seating about 24,000 spectators. It was used for both entertainment and public assemblies (ἐκκλησία), and it was the place where the riot against Paul and his companions took place.

## People

**Sons of Sceva** (verse 14) are presented as sons of a Jewish "high priest." The title "high priest" (ἀρχιερέως) is historically problematic, since there is no known record of a high priest by that name in Jerusalem. The title may have been self-appointed or referred to membership in a priestly family, possibly used to lend legitimacy to their exorcism activities.

**Demetrius** (verse 24) was a silversmith who led the guild of craftsmen who made small silver shrines or temples (ναοὺς ἀργυροῦς) of Artemis. Such miniature shrines were popular souvenirs and votive offerings purchased by pilgrims, constituting a major industry in Ephesus.

**Gaius and Aristarchus** (verse 29) were Macedonian traveling companions (συνεκδήμους) of Paul who were seized by the crowd during the riot in the theater.

**Timothy and Erastus** (verse 22) were coworkers (τῶν διακονούντων αὐτῷ) whom Paul sent ahead to Macedonia while he remained in Asia for a time.

**Alexander** (verse 33) was a Jew who was pushed forward from the crowd, possibly to distance the Jewish community from the Christians. He was not allowed to speak once the crowd recognized him as a Jew.

**Asiarchs** (verse 31, Greek: Ἀσιαρχῶν) were prominent men from the province of Asia who had responsibility for the imperial cult and public festivals. The fact that some of these were Paul's friends shows that the gospel had reached higher social classes and that not all opposition to Paul was official.

**The city clerk** (verse 35, Greek: γραμματεύς) was the highest local official in Ephesus and served as a link between the city and Roman authorities. He calmed the crowd and dissolved the assembly.

## Events and Customs

**John's disciples in Ephesus** (verses 1–7) were a group of about twelve men (ὡσεὶ δώδεκα) who knew only John's baptism (τὸ Ἰωάννου βάπτισμα) and had not heard about the Holy Spirit. After Paul's teaching, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus, and when Paul laid hands on them, the Holy Spirit came upon them, and they spoke in tongues and prophesied.

**Jewish traveling exorcists** (verse 13) were a known phenomenon in antiquity. Jewish exorcism held high esteem in the Greco-Roman world, and many Jewish exorcists traveled around offering their services. They often used invocations of powerful names and formulas, which explains the attempt to use Jesus' name as a magical formula.

**The burning of magic books** (verse 19) reflects Ephesus' reputation as a center for magic and occult practices. The expression "Ephesian letters" (Ἐφέσια γράμματα) was in antiquity a well-known term for magical formulas and incantations. The value of 50,000 silver coins (μυριάδας πέντε ἀργυρίου) represented a substantial sum, showing the extent of magical practice among new believers.

**The riot in the theater** (verses 29–41) was triggered by the silversmith Demetrius, who feared financial loss due to Paul's preaching. The crowd shouted "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" (Μεγάλη ἡ Ἄρτεμις Ἐφεσίων) for about two hours before the city clerk calmed them down.

## Key Terms

**"The Way"** (verse 9: τὴν ὁδὸν, verse 23: τῆς ὁδοῦ) was an early designation for the Christian movement, used several times in Acts. The term emphasizes Christianity as a way of life and a path, not merely a belief system.

**Neokoros** (verse 35, Greek: νεωκόρον) means "temple warden" and was an honorary title that Ephesus proudly bore as guardian of the Temple of Artemis. The city used this title on coins and inscriptions, and it brought considerable prestige and economic benefits through pilgrimage tourism.

**"That which fell from heaven"** (verse 35, Greek: τοῦ διοπετοῦς) likely refers to a meteorite or a cult image regarded as of divine origin, not made by human hands. This was a common belief associated with especially sacred cult objects in antiquity.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Ephesus have uncovered extensive remains of the city from Roman times. The large theater mentioned in the chapter is well preserved and shows the impressive size that matches Luke's description. Inscriptions found in Ephesus confirm the existence of silversmith guilds and mention the worship of Artemis. One inscription refers to "silversmiths" as a group, confirming Luke's portrayal of an organized craftsmen's guild.

The Temple of Artemis (Artemision) has been located, although only its foundation remains. Archaeologists have found many small terracotta and metal figures of Artemis as well as miniature temples resembling those produced in Demetrius' workshop. Ephesian coins from the period bear images of the temple and the goddess, often with the title "neokoros" used by the city clerk in verse 35.
