# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 73

## People

Psalm 73 is attributed to **Asaph** (מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף), a Levite and music director during King David’s reign. The superscription links the psalm to the Asaph tradition, either as its author or as part of the repertoire performed by his descendants.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **לְבָרֵי לֵבָב** (*levare levav*, "for those pure in heart") in verse 1 does not describe external ritual purity but rather an inner integrity and undivided devotion to God. This stands in contrast to the self-satisfied wicked who are described throughout the psalm.

The word **מִקְדְּשֵׁי־אֵל** (*miqdeshé-El*, "the holy places of God") in verse 17 marks the turning point of the psalm. The plural form (or intensive/majestic plural) may refer to the sacred spaces within the temple complex or function as an abstract/intensive expression for holiness itself. It was in this context—encountering God through worship—that the psalmist gained perspective on the fate of the wicked.

The phrase **צוּר־לְבָבִי וְחֶלְקִי** (*tsur-levavi vechelqi*, "my heart’s rock and my portion") in verse 26 combines two important images: "rock" as a symbol of God’s unchanging stability, and **חֵלֶק** (*cheleq*, "portion/inherited share"), a term drawn from the land distribution in Israel. The Levites received no territorial inheritance like the other tribes; instead, the Lord Himself was their inheritance (Numbers 18:20). The psalmist confesses that God is his ultimate portion and security.

## Events and Customs

The structure of the psalm reflects a **wisdom crisis**. Traditional wisdom teaching held that righteousness leads to blessing and wickedness to punishment. When the psalmist observes that the wicked prosper (verses 3–12), he encounters the same problem of divine justice faced by Job.

The turning point comes when the psalmist enters the sanctuary (verse 17). This indicates that temple service involved not only rituals but also spiritual insight and instruction. In encountering the holy—through worship, sacrifice, and community—the Israelite gained perspective on the great questions concerning God’s justice and the ultimate fate of the wicked.
