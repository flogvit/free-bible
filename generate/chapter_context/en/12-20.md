# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 20

## People

**Hezekiah** (Hebrew: חִזְקִיָּהוּ, "Yahweh is my strength") is presented in this chapter as terminally ill but granted fifteen additional years of life through prayer and God's intervention. The chapter portrays him both in vulnerable prayer turned toward the wall (vv. 2–3) and in questionable diplomatic conduct when he shows all his treasures to Babylonian envoys (v. 13).

**Isaiah, son of Amoz** (יְשַׁעְיָהוּ בֶן־אָמוֹץ) appears as a prophet with a dual role: first as the messenger of the death sentence (v. 1), then as the bearer of healing and the promise of fifteen additional years (vv. 5–6). He prescribes a fig poultice (v. 7), calls upon God for the sign involving the shadow (v. 11), and later confronts Hezekiah regarding his careless display of treasures to the Babylonians, delivering a prophecy about the coming exile (vv. 14–18).

**Berodach-Baladan** (Hebrew: בְּרֹאדַךְ בַּלְאֲדָן, also known as Merodach-Baladan in Isaiah 39:1) was the son of Baladan and king of Babylon. His envoys came with letters and gifts to Hezekiah (v. 12), officially because he had heard about the king's illness, but likely also to explore possibilities for an anti-Assyrian alliance.

**Manasseh** is briefly mentioned in v. 21 as Hezekiah's son and successor on the throne.

## Places

**Hezekiah's pool and conduit** (הַבְּרֵכָה וְהַתְּעָלָה) are mentioned in verse 20 as part of Hezekiah's achievements. This refers to the water supply project that brought water into the city, known as the Siloam Tunnel.

**The house of the Lord** (בֵּית יְהוָה) is mentioned as the place where Hezekiah was to go up on the third day after his healing (vv. 5, 8), marking the restoration of his health.

## Events and Customs

**The fig poultice** (דְּבֶלֶת תְּאֵנִים) applied to Hezekiah's boil (שְׁחִין) represents ancient medical practice in the Middle East. Dried figs were used as an anti-inflammatory remedy. The combination of a prophetic word and a natural remedy shows that divine healing and medical treatment were not viewed as opposites.

**Diplomatic gifts and envoys** from Babylon to Hezekiah reflect the great-power politics of the time. Hezekiah's display of all his treasures (v. 13) was likely intended as a demonstration of resources for a potential uprising against Assyria. Isaiah interprets this as a lack of trust in God and prophesies that everything will be carried off to Babylon (v. 17).

## Key Terms

**The fifteen additional years** (חֲמֵשׁ עֶשְׂרֵה שָׁנָה) that God promised Hezekiah (v. 6) are a central theme in the chapter. Along with this promise comes assurance of protection from the king of Assyria and defense of Jerusalem "for my own sake and for the sake of my servant David" (לְמַעֲנִי וּלְמַעַן דָּוִד עַבְדִּי).

**The steps of Ahaz** (מַעֲלוֹת אָחָז) in verse 11 likely refer to a sundial or stair-like structure that used the movement of shadows to measure time. Hezekiah was given the choice whether the shadow should move forward or backward ten steps, and he chose the more difficult option (backward, v. 10). This supernatural sign confirmed God's promise of healing.

**The treasury** (בֵּית נְכֹתֹה, v. 13) is a rare Hebrew expression. Hezekiah showed the Babylonians everything he owned—silver, gold, balsam, fine oil, the armory, and all his storehouses. Nothing was withheld.

**Eunuchs** (סָרִיסִים) in verse 18 are often translated as "court officials" or "eunuchs." The term could refer both to castrated servants and to high-ranking court officials in general. The prophecy that Hezekiah's descendants would become eunuchs in the palace of Babylon was fulfilled with the exile in 586 BC.

**Hezekiah's response** (v. 19) to the judgment—"Good is the word of the Lord"—has been interpreted differently. Some see it as pious acceptance of God's will, while others view it as relieved selfishness that the judgment would not fall during his own lifetime.
