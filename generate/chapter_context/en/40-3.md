# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 3

## People

**John the Baptist** (Ἰωάννης ὁ βαπτιστής) appears in this chapter as a prophetic figure who consciously imitates Elijah. His clothing of camel hair and leather belt (v. 4) is a direct parallel to the description of Elijah in 2 Kings 1:8. The Jewish historian Josephus mentions John in *Jewish Antiquities* (18.116–119) and confirms his activity as a baptizer and popular preacher, as well as that he was executed by Herod Antipas. Josephus presents him primarily as an ethical teacher, while the Gospels highlight his role as the forerunner of the Messiah.

**The Pharisees and Sadducees** (v. 7) represented two different directions within Judaism. The Pharisees were a popular movement that emphasized careful observance of the Torah and oral tradition, while the Sadducees were an aristocratic group connected to the temple who recognized only the five books of Moses as authoritative. That these two often conflicting groups came together to John underscores the broad interest his movement aroused.

## Places

**The wilderness of Judea** (ἡ ἔρημος τῆς Ἰουδαίας, v. 1) refers to the dry, hilly area west of the Dead Sea and the Jordan Valley. This landscape had deep symbolic significance in Jewish consciousness: it was here that Israel wandered for forty years, and the prophets associated the wilderness with both judgment and renewal. The Qumran community, which left behind the Dead Sea Scrolls, had established itself precisely in this area and cited the same Isaiah text (40:3) as justification for their withdrawal into the wilderness.

**The Jordan River** (v. 5–6, 13) formed the natural eastern border of the promised land. The Israelites crossed the Jordan under Joshua to enter Canaan, and several prophets performed miracles at or in the river (2 Kings 2:8; 5:10–14). John's choice of this place for his baptismal activity therefore carried strong overtones of new beginnings and entry into God's kingdom.

## Events and Customs

**Baptism**, as practiced by John, differed from other ritual washings of the time. Judaism knew *mikveh* (ritual cleansing baths) which were repeated regularly, and proselyte baptism for Gentiles who converted. John's baptism, however, was a one-time event linked to repentance, and remarkably it was directed toward Jews—not Gentiles. This implied that even Abraham's descendants needed repentance (v. 9), which was radical. Baptism took place by immersion in flowing water, and those being baptized confessed their sins (v. 6).

**Confession of sins** (ἐξομολογούμενοι τὰς ἁμαρτίας, v. 6) was an established practice in Judaism, especially linked to the Day of Atonement. That people publicly confessed their sins in connection with baptism represented a humbling act that signaled genuine repentance.

## Key Terms

**Μετανοεῖτε** ("repent", v. 2) literally means "change your mind" and involves a fundamental reorientation of one's entire life. The Hebrew equivalent *shuv* ("turn back") was central to the prophets' preaching. The term encompasses both remorse for the past and a new direction in life.

**Ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν** ("the kingdom of heaven", v. 2) is an expression unique to Matthew, corresponding to "the kingdom of God" in the other Gospel writers. The paraphrase "heavens" reflects Jewish reverence for God's name. In that time, the term carried strong expectations of God's intervention in history and the establishment of his rule on earth.

**Πληρῶσαι πᾶσαν δικαιοσύνην** ("to fulfill all righteousness", v. 15) is Jesus' reason for being baptized. "Righteousness" (δικαιοσύνη) in Matthew includes faithful observance of God's will. Jesus' baptism marks his solidarity with sinful Israel and his consecration to his messianic mission.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Dead Sea Scrolls, especially the *Community Rule* (1QS 8:13–14), show that Isaiah 40:3 was used by the Qumran community as justification for their withdrawal into the wilderness to "prepare the way of the Lord". This testifies that John's use of this text (v. 3) was part of a broader Jewish expectation that the wilderness would be the place for a new beginning. At the same time, Qumran interpreted "the way" as the study of the Torah, while John linked it to repentance and baptism in anticipation of a coming figure.
