# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 20

## People

In this chapter, Moses serves as the intermediary between God and the people. When the Israelites become afraid of God’s direct speech (v. 19), they ask Moses to speak to them instead. Moses alone enters the dark cloud (*arafel*, עֲרָפֶל) where God is present (v. 21) and receives further instructions about worship there.

## Places

Mount Sinai (Horeb) is the central setting for this chapter. In biblical tradition, the mountain represents the meeting point between heaven and earth—the place where God reveals Himself directly to His people. The dark cloud (*arafel*, עֲרָפֶל) surrounding God on the mountain (v. 21) is a classic theophany motif in the Old Testament.

## Events and Customs

The Ten Commandments (Hebrew: *aseret ha-devarim*, עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים, "the ten words") are presented in this chapter. The division of the commandments into those concerning our relationship with God (vv. 3–11) and those concerning our relationships with others (vv. 12–17) follows a structure known from covenant literature: identification of the Lord, a historical prologue about His acts of kindness, and subsequent requirements.

The prohibition against images (vv. 4–5) was radical in a world where divine statues were central to all religious practice. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, gods were worshipped through statues that were "opened" through rituals so that the deity could take up residence within them. The Israelites’ ban on images marked a fundamental break with this practice and emphasized that YHWH could not be manipulated or controlled through representations.

The Sabbath commandment (vv. 8–11) stands out from surrounding cultures. While the Babylonians had days of restriction (*šapattu*) tied to lunar cycles, the Israelite Sabbath was a weekly day of rest rooted in creation. Remarkably, the Sabbath command explicitly includes servants, foreigners, and livestock—a social dimension without parallel in antiquity.

The regulations concerning altars at the end of the chapter (vv. 24–26) reflect a simple worship practice. The prohibition against using hewn stones or steps contrasts with the grand temple complexes of Egypt and Canaan, emphasizing that true worship does not require human craftsmanship.

The apodictic (unconditional) form of the commandments—"You shall not"—without specifying penalties suggests they functioned as foundational principles rather than detailed legislation.

## Key Terms

The word *qanna* (קַנָּא), translated "jealous" or "passionate" (v. 5), describes God’s exclusive claim to His people’s loyalty. This is not jealousy in the human sense, but a marriage-like faithfulness—a metaphor that the prophets later developed extensively.

The expression *lo tissa* (לֹא תִשָּׂא, v. 7), "you shall not bear/lift up," refers to the misuse of God’s name. This included more than cursing—it encompassed false oaths, magical use of the name, and any casual treatment of the holy name YHWH.

The verb *chamad* (חָמַד), "covet" (v. 17), which appears twice in the verse, describes not merely feelings but an active desire that leads to action. In a Near Eastern context, this could include legal manipulation to acquire another person’s property.
