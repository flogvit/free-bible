# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 69

## Key Terms

The psalm opens with the superscription "on shoshannim" (עַל־שׁוֹשַׁנִּים), which means "upon/after lilies." This likely refers to a well-known melody or musical instrument to be used in its performance. The term also appears in Psalms 45 and 80.

The water imagery that dominates the first part of the psalm – "the waters have come up to my soul" (v. 2), "bottomless mire" (בִּיוֵן מְצוּלָה, v. 3), "the depths of the waters," and "the flood" (שִׁבֹּלֶת) – reflects a widespread concept in the ancient Near East. Chaotic waters symbolized the powers of death and life-threatening danger. In Israelite thought, deep water often represented Sheol, the realm of the dead.

The expression "zeal for your house has consumed me" (קִנְאַת בֵּיתְךָ אֲכָלָתְנִי, v. 10) refers to an intense, burning devotion to God's temple and honor. This verse is quoted in John 2:17 concerning Jesus in the temple.

## Events and Customs

The psalm mentions several practices associated with mourning and repentance. The psalmist fasts and weeps (v. 11) and puts on sackcloth (שָׂק, v. 12). Sackcloth was a coarse, dark fabric made of goat hair worn directly against the skin as a sign of deep sorrow, remorse, or humility before God. This was the standard mourning ritual in ancient Israel and surrounding cultures.

The verse stating that "those who sit at the gate" speak about the psalmist (v. 13) refers to the city gate as the central place for public life, legal proceedings, and social interaction. The fact that both respected elders at the gate and drunkards sing mocking songs about him shows total social rejection.

## Places

The conclusion of the psalm (vv. 36–37) mentions Zion and "the cities of Judah" being rebuilt. This has led many scholars to date the psalm to the exile or early post-exilic period, when Jerusalem lay in ruins and the people longed for restoration. Others believe these verses may be a later liturgical addition to an older Davidic psalm.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Verse 22 – "They gave me poison (רֹאשׁ) for food, and for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink" – is quoted in the gospel accounts of Jesus' suffering (Matt 27:34, 48; John 19:29). Along with verse 5 ("those who hate me without cause") and verse 26 (quoted in Acts 1:20 concerning Judas), this made Psalm 69 one of the most quoted psalms in the New Testament as a prophetic text about the Messiah's suffering.
