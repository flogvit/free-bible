# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 4

## People

**Rechab and Baanah** were sons of Rimmon from Beeroth and served as "raiders" (שָׂרֵי גְדוּדִים) under Ish-Bosheth. The title suggests they led smaller military units or raiding parties, which gave them access to the king's residence. Being Benjaminites like Saul himself makes their betrayal especially serious—they betrayed the royal house of their own tribe.

The introduction of **Mephibosheth** (v. 4) appears to be a parenthetical note explaining why he was not a viable candidate for the throne. His lameness from the age of five, caused while fleeing after hearing news of Saul and Jonathan's death at Jezreel, made him unfit to rule according to the views of that time. His name is written as "Merib-Baal" in 1 Chronicles 8:34 and 9:40.

## Places

**Beeroth** was one of the four Gibeonite cities (Josh 9:17) located in the territory of the tribe of Benjamin, north of Jerusalem. The text mentions that the Beerothites fled to Gittaim and became "foreigners" (גָּרִים) there. The reason for their flight is not mentioned in this chapter but may be connected to events described in 2 Sam 21:1-2. Gittaim was likely located in the Shephelah region west of Benjamin.

The **Arabah road** (דֶּרֶךְ הָעֲרָבָה) used by Rechab and Baanah during their nighttime escape was the route through the Jordan Valley that connected north and south. This road allowed them to travel quickly from the area where Ish-Bosheth resided to Hebron while avoiding populated areas.

## Events and Customs

**Midday rest** (מִשְׁכַּב הַצָּהֳרָיִם) was a common practice in the hot climate of the Middle East. Kings and leaders typically rested during the hottest hours, which gave the murderers the opportunity they needed. They entered the house as if they were there to fetch wheat (לֹקְחֵי חִטִּים), thus exploiting normal household routines to gain access.

**Mutilation and public display** of corpses was a known punitive practice in the ancient Middle East. By cutting off the hands and feet of the murderers and hanging them by the pool in Hebron, David demonstrated both just punishment and public condemnation of their actions. This paralleled the treatment of the Amalekite in 2 Samuel 1 and communicated to all Israel that David did not tolerate such methods.

## Key Terms

The expression **"a righteous man"** (אִישׁ־צַדִּיק) that David uses regarding Ish-Bosheth (v. 11) is remarkable. Although Ish-Bosheth was his rival, David acknowledges his innocent status in this context—he was killed in his own house, on his own bed. This underscores David's consistent position that anointed leaders should not be killed by assassination.

The verb **"destroy"** (בִעַרְתִּי) used by David carries connotations of ritual cleansing—removing impurity from the land. Bloodguilt required retribution so that the land would not become defiled (Num 35:33-34).
