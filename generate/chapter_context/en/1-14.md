# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 14

## People

**The four eastern kings** formed a coalition led by Chedorlaomer, king of Elam (southwestern Iran). Amraphel of Shinar (Babylonia), Arioch of Ellasar, and Tidal of Goiim made up the rest of the alliance. These names have a Mesopotamian sound, and several scholars have attempted to identify them with known historical figures, but no certain identification has been made. The royal names follow naming patterns known from the second millennium B.C., which supports the ancient background of the narrative.

**Melchizedek** (Hebrew: מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק, "king of righteousness") is king of Salem and priest of El Elyon ("God Most High"). Salem is traditionally identified with Jerusalem. The fact that one person combined kingship and the priesthood was common in Canaanite culture, where kings often served as cultic leaders. Melchizedek's sudden appearance and disappearance in the text have given rise to much theological reflection, especially in Psalm 110 and Hebrews.

**Abram** is here called "the Hebrew" (הָעִבְרִי) for the first time, a term that likely marks him as a foreigner or immigrant from the Canaanites' perspective. His allies—Mamre, Eshcol, and Aner—were Amorites who had entered into a covenant alliance (בַּעֲלֵי בְרִית) with him, testifying to international alliances in the patriarchal period.

## Places

**Emek ha-Siddim** ("the Valley of Siddim") is located in the area near the Salt Sea (Dead Sea). The text mentions that the valley was full of "bitumen pits" (בֶּאֱרֹת חֵמָר), which corresponds to the natural bitumen deposits found in the Dead Sea region. The area may have been located in what is now the submerged southern part of the Dead Sea.

**Dan** in verse 14 is the northernmost point to which Abram pursued his enemies. This place name is likely a editorial update, since the city first received this name in Judges 18:29—the original name of the place was Laish.

**Hobah** (north of Damascus) and **the King's Valley** (Emek Shaveh, near Jerusalem) mark the geographical scope of the military operation, from far north to the central highlands.

## Events and Customs

**Tithing to Melchizedek**: Abram's gift of a tithe (מַעֲשֵׂר) to Melchizedek represents an acknowledgment of the priest's spiritual authority and blessing. The practice of tithing was widespread in the ancient Near East, not only as a religious duty but also as tribute to superiors. That Abram gave the tithe voluntarily, without being a vassal, shows his respect for Melchizedek's divine representation.

**Bread and wine**: Melchizedek's offering of bread and wine may have been a victory feast or a covenant meal. In the ancient Near East, shared meals often marked peaceful intentions and covenant relationships between parties.

**Abram's refusal of war spoils**: In the ancient Near East, the victor had the right to all war spoils. That Abram refused to accept anything from the king of Sodom—not "a thread or a sandal strap"—was a demonstrative act marking that his wealth came from the Lord alone, not from pagan kings.

## Key Terms

**El Elyon** (אֵל עֶלְיוֹן, "God Most High") was a Canaanite divine title used by Melchizedek. Abram identifies this god with YHWH (Yahweh) in verse 22, showing a theological connection between the patriarchs' God and the supreme deity also recognized outside Israel's tradition. The title "creator of heaven and earth" (קֹנֵה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ) is also found in Phoenician texts about the god El.

**Chanichim** (חֲנִכָיו, "his trained men"): This rare word, which appears only here in the Bible, describes the 318 men born in Abram's household. The word suggests trained, initiated servants who functioned as a private military force—a practice known from other ancient texts.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Ebla tablets (ca. 2400–2300 B.C.) from Syria have been claimed to mention several of the cities in the Pentapolis of the Plain (Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim, Zoar), but this interpretation is disputed among scholars. Regardless of this debate, archaeological findings confirm that the area around the Dead Sea was inhabited during the Early Bronze Age.

The Mari texts (ca. 1800–1750 B.C.) document similar military coalitions and campaigns in which four or five kings allied against other small states, just as described in this chapter. The balance of power between Mesopotamia and the western regions (including Canaan) depicted in the chapter reflects known geopolitical patterns from that period.
