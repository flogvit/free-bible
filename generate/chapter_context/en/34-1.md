# Historical and Cultural Context for Nahum 1

## People

**Nahum of Elkosh** (v. 1) is the prophet behind this book. The name "Nahum" (נַחוּם) means "comfort" or "the one who comforts"—a significant name given that the message about the fall of Nineveh would have been a comfort to oppressed Judah. The location of Elkosh is disputed; various traditions place it in Galilee, near Nineveh, or in Judah. The most likely scenario is a place in Judah, since the prophecy is directed at a Judean audience.

**"The one who plots evil against the Lord"** (v. 11) probably refers to an Assyrian king. The expression "counselor of Belial" (יֹעֵץ בְּלִיָּעַל) describes someone who plans evil and rebellion against God.

## Places

**Nineveh** (v. 1) was the capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, located by the Tigris River near modern-day Mosul in Iraq. In Nahum's time, Nineveh was one of the world's largest and most powerful cities, with massive walls stretching over 12 kilometers and a population perhaps reaching 100,000–150,000.

**Bashan, Carmel, and Lebanon** (v. 4) are mentioned as examples of fertile regions that wither before the Lord's power. Bashan (east of Galilee) was known for its rich pastures and strong oxen. Carmel was a forested mountain ridge by the Mediterranean Sea, famous for its fertility. Lebanon was renowned for its mighty cedar trees. These three areas represented the best of nature's abundance in the Middle East.

## Key Terms

**"Massá"** (מַשָּׂא, v. 1) is often translated as "oracle," "burden," or "word of judgment." The word is commonly used for prophetic utterances containing judgment and signals that what follows is a heavy, serious message from God.

**"Jealous God"** (אֵל קַנּוֹא, v. 2) is an important theological concept. The Hebrew word "qannō" does not describe jealousy in a negative sense, but rather God's intense, passionate commitment to His people and His honor. The same word is used in connection with the covenant relationship (Exodus 20:5) and expresses that God tolerates no rivals or unfaithfulness.

**"Avenger"** (נֹקֵם, v. 2) is repeated three times in verse 2, emphasizing its intensity. In an Old Testament context, divine vengeance is not about uncontrolled anger but about righteous retribution and the restoration of justice—especially on behalf of the oppressed.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

Verse 8 uses the image of a "flood sweeping away" (וּבְשֶׁטֶף עֹבֵר) to describe God's total judgment. This aligns with ancient sources reporting that the Tigris River flooded during the final siege of Nineveh in 612 BC, destroying parts of the city wall and giving Babylonians and Medes an opening.
