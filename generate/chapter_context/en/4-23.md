# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 23

## People

**Balaam** (בִּלְעָם) appears in this chapter as the messenger of God's word to Balak. Despite his background as a professional diviner, he must speak the words that YHWH puts into his mouth (vv. 5, 12, 16). His role shows that God has sovereignty even over non-Israelite prophets.

**Balak son of Zippor** (בָּלָק בֶּן־צִפֹּר) – the king of Moab who in this chapter repeatedly tries to get Balaam to curse Israel by moving to new locations (vv. 13, 27), but each time must hear blessings instead of curses.

**The officials of Moab** (שָׂרֵי מוֹאָב) (vv. 6, 17) are present as witnesses at the sacrifices and oracles, which underscores the political and public nature of the ritual.

## Places

**The field of Zophim** (שְׂדֵה צֹפִים) on top of Pisgah (v. 14) was a high point in the Abarim mountain range east of the Jordan Valley. Pisgah is likely identical to or near Mount Nebo, with a view over the northern Dead Sea and the Jordan plain. The place was chosen because it provided a view over parts of Israel's camp.

**The top of Peor** (רֹאשׁ הַפְּעוֹר) (v. 28) was a high point overlooking Jeshimon (הַיְשִׁימֹן, "the wilderness"). Peor was likely connected with the cult of Baal-Peor.

## Events and Customs

**The sacrifice rituals** described in the chapter follow a pattern common in ancient Near Eastern divination practices. Balaam instructs Balak to build seven altars and offer seven bulls and seven rams – the number seven had sacred significance throughout the ancient Middle East as a number of completeness. The fact that the king personally participated in the sacrifices emphasizes the importance of the ritual. In Mesopotamian tradition, such offerings prepared the gods to give favorable oracles, but here the text shows that YHWH cannot be manipulated.

**The three oracle locations** – The chapter describes how Balak moves Balaam to three different places in hopes that a change in position will produce a different result. This reflects ancient Near Eastern belief that geographical location could influence the outcome of divination.

**The mashal form** (מָשָׁל, "proverb" or "oracle speech") in which Balaam's prophecies are presented (vv. 7, 18) was a well-known literary genre in the ancient Orient. These poetic oracles have parallels to Mesopotamian prophetic texts, but their content – blessing instead of cursing – turns the expected situation upside down.

## Key Terms

**Shaphi** (שֶׁפִי) in verse 3 is a rare Hebrew word traditionally translated as "bare height" or "solitary place". Balaam went to such a place to meet God, which reflects the idea that revelations could more easily be received on remote, high places.

**Nachash and qesem** (נַחַשׁ וְקֶסֶם) in verse 23 refer to specific divination practices: *nachash* was snake-omen interpretation or omen reading, while *qesem* was a broader term for divination art, possibly including arrow-casting. The verse declares that Israel is not dependent on such pagan practices because God speaks directly to them.

**Teruah** (תְּרוּעָה) in verse 21, "royal shout" or "war cry", is a cultic and military term describing the powerful shout that accompanied God's presence among his people, often associated with the sound of the ram's horn (shofar).

**Lavi and ari** (לָבִיא וַאֲרִי) in verse 24 are two Hebrew words for lion, where *lavi* often refers to the lioness and *ari* to the male lion. The lion metaphor for Israel emphasizes the people's strength and invincibility.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Deir Alla inscription**, discovered in 1967 at Tell Deir Alla in the Jordan Valley (near Sukkot), is the most direct archaeological parallel to the Balaam narrative. Dated to approximately 840–760 BCE, this text contains a prophecy attributed to "Balaam son of Beor, seer of the gods". The inscription describes Balaam as a seer who receives nightly visions and delivers divine messages. Although the text is several centuries younger than the setting of the Balaam narrative, it confirms that traditions about Balaam as a well-known seer were widespread in the Transjordan region.
