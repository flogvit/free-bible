# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 25

## Summary
The chapter describes Paul's trial under the new procurator Festus, his appeal to the emperor, and the introduction of King Agrippa II, who is to hear the case.

## People

**Porcius Festus** was the new procurator in Judea who succeeded Felix. In this chapter, he appears as an administrator seeking to balance Jewish interests with Roman legal practice. He travels to Jerusalem only three days after his arrival (v.1), rejects the Jews' attempt to have Paul transferred there (vv.4-5), but later offers Paul the opportunity to be tried in Jerusalem to please the Jews (v.9). His uncertainty regarding religious matters becomes clear when he consults Agrippa (vv.20, 26).

**King Agrippa II** did not hold royal authority over Judea, but the Romans gave him responsibility for appointing high priests and managing the temple in Jerusalem. This made him an important authority on Jewish religious questions, which explains why Festus sought his counsel regarding Paul's case (v.26). He came to Caesarea together with his sister Berenice to greet the new procurator (v.13) and expressed a desire to hear Paul himself (v.22).

**Berenice** was Agrippa's sister and accompanied him on his visit to Festus (v.13). She was present at the ceremonial presentation of Paul, where they arrived "with great pomp" (μετὰ πολλῆς φαντασίας, v.23).

**The chief priests and Jewish leaders** (οἱ ἀρχιερεῖς καὶ οἱ πρῶτοι τῶν Ἰουδαίων, v.2) appear as Paul's persistent accusers. They tried to get Festus to transfer Paul to Jerusalem, with plans for an ambush along the way (v.3).

## Places

**Caesarea** was the center of the legal proceedings. The procurator's tribunal (βῆμα/bēma, vv.6, 10, 17) was the court where Festus sat in judgment over Paul. **The auditorium** (ἀκροατήριον/akroatērion, v.23) was a formal reception hall suitable for ceremonial occasions such as Agrippa's state visit.

**Jerusalem** is mentioned as the place Festus visited immediately after his arrival (vv.1, 15), and to which the Jews wanted Paul transferred—with plans for an ambush along the way (v.3).

## Events and Customs

**Appellatio ad Caesarem** (appeal to the emperor) was a legal right for Roman citizens. For Paul, this meant an opportunity to have his case heard directly by the emperor or his representatives in Rome. The expression "Καίσαρα ἐπικαλοῦμαι" (Kaisara epikaloumai – "I appeal to Caesar," v.11) was the formal wording that set this process in motion. Once the appeal had been stated and accepted by the governor, it was legally binding—Festus no longer had authority to judge the case (v.12).

**A courtesy visit from client kings** was common practice within the Roman system. Agrippa's visit to "greet" (ἀσπασάμενοι/aspasamenoi, v.13) the new procurator was a diplomatic necessity that confirmed the bonds of loyalty between a client king and Rome's representative.

**Roman legal practice** is highlighted in verse 16, where Festus explains that it is not Roman custom to hand anyone over before the accused has met their accusers face-to-face and been given an opportunity to defend themselves.

## Key Terms

**Σεβαστός** (Sebastos) – The Greek word used in verses 21 and 25 is the official Greek translation of the Latin title "Augustus," the emperor's honorific title. Festus' use of the phrase "τοῦ Σεβαστοῦ διάγνωσιν" (the decision of Caesar) shows the formal, legal character of Paul's appeal.

**Δεισιδαιμονία** (deisidaimonia) in verse 19 is a notable word choice. The word can mean both "religion" (neutral) and "superstition" (negative). Festus' use of this term when referring to Jewish belief to Agrippa reveals a typical Roman attitude toward foreign religions as "their own religious matters."

**Κύριος** (kyrios – "lord") used for the emperor in verse 26 reflects language usage in eastern provinces under Nero. Festus' concern about what he should write to "the lord" (τῷ κυρίῳ) underscores the formal nature of sending a prisoner to the imperial court.

**Φαντασία** (fantasia) in verse 23 describes "great pomp"—a term that emphasizes the ceremonial extravagance of Agrippa's and Berenice's arrival at the auditorium.
