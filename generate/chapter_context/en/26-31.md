# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 31

## People

The chapter is directed against **Pharaoh, king of Egypt, and his multitude** (verse 2). The dating to "the eleventh year, in the third month, on the first day" (verse 1) places the prophecy in June 587 B.C. (alternatively 586 B.C., depending on the chronological system), in the months before Jerusalem’s fall. At this time, Pharaoh Hophra (Apries) was on the throne in Egypt, and Judah had become dependent on Egyptian military aid against Babylon. It is remarkable that the prophecy uses **Assyria** as a warning example for Egypt—the once-mighty empire that had fallen to Babylon (Nineveh was conquered in 612 B.C., and the last Assyrian forces were defeated at Harran in 609 B.C.).

## Places

**Lebanon** and its famous cedar trees play a central role in the chapter’s imagery (verses 3, 15–16). The cedars of Lebanon were known throughout the ancient Near East as the noblest of all trees—they were used for temple construction and palaces, and symbolized greatness and majesty. Geographically, the prophecy also refers to **תְּהוֹם** (*tehom* – the deep/underground waters), which in the ancient worldview was the source of rivers and springs that nourished such mighty trees (verse 4).

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **אֵיל גּוֹיִם** (*eil gojim* – "the mighty one of the nations") in verse 11 refers to Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon, who is the divinely appointed agent of judgment. The term **שְׁאוֹל** (*sheol*) is used repeatedly (verses 15–17) and denotes the realm of the dead or the abode of the departed. The expression **עֲרֵלִים** (*arelim* – "the uncircumcised") in verse 18 describes the dishonored dead—to lie among the uncircumcised was a shameful fate in the ancient Near East, especially for Egyptians who practiced circumcision.

## Events and Customs

The **Eden motif** and "God’s garden" (verses 8–9, 16, 18) link Egypt’s pride to original sin. The comparison with the trees of Eden points to cosmic greatness, but also to cosmic judgment. The idea that great nations "go down" to the realm of the dead together with their allies and vassals (verse 17) reflects ancient Near Eastern beliefs that a ruler’s fate in the underworld is shared by his subjects and allies—entire political structures share the same destiny in death.
