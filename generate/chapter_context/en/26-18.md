## Events and Customs

Ezekiel 18 directly confronts a proverb that was widespread among the Jewish exiles: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge" (verse 2). This proverb expressed a fatalistic attitude in which the people believed they were suffering unjustly for their ancestors’ sins rather than for their own. The same proverb also appears in Jeremiah 31:29, showing that this was a common way of thinking in the exile community. The proverb may reflect a misunderstood interpretation of texts such as Exodus 20:5, where God says He "visits the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation". Ezekiel rejects this fatalistic use and emphasizes individual responsibility.

The chapter contains a detailed list of actions that define a righteous person (verses 5–9). These include the prohibition against eating sacrificial meals on the high places (idolatry), sexual purity, fair treatment of debtors, and the ban on usury. The practice of "eating on the mountains" refers to cultic meals at Canaanite high places, which remained a persistent problem in Israel’s history. The list reflects both ritual and social requirements from the Mosaic Law, especially from the Holiness Code in Leviticus.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **נֶפֶשׁ** (nefesh), translated "soul" or "the one", appears repeatedly throughout the chapter (especially verses 4 and 20). In this context, it does not mean "soul" in the Greek philosophical sense, but rather "person" or "living being". The statement "The soul who sins shall die" therefore concerns the whole person and their responsibility, not an immortal soul separate from the body.

The terms **נֶשֶׁךְ** (neshekh, "interest/usury") and **תַּרְבִּית** (tarbit, "profit/addition") in verses 8 and 13 refer to practices forbidden by Israelite law (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:36–37). Neshekh literally means "bite" and suggests the exploitative nature of interest. In an agrarian economy without a modern banking system, lending with interest was seen as taking advantage of people in distress, not as legitimate business.

The expression **לֵב חָדָשׁ וְרוּחַ חֲדָשָׁה** ("a new heart and a new spirit") in verse 31 is central to the chapter’s message about repentance. "Heart" (lev) in Hebrew thought is the seat of will and understanding, not merely emotions. Here Ezekiel calls for a fundamental change in a person’s inner orientation, a theme he develops further in chapter 36:26, where God Himself promises to give His people this new heart.
