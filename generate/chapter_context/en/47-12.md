# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Corinthians 12

## People

**Titus and "the brother" (v. 18)** are mentioned as Paul's envoys to Corinth. Titus was one of Paul's most trusted coworkers, a Gentile believer who played a key role in restoring the relationship between Paul and the church in Corinth. The unnamed "brother" sent with Titus is likely the same person mentioned in 8:18, possibly connected with the collection for the poor in Jerusalem.

**"Super-apostles" (v. 11, Greek: *hyperlian apostolōn*)** is an ironic term Paul uses to refer to rival teachers in Corinth. These were probably traveling preachers who boasted of their spiritual experiences and ecstatic encounters, while questioning Paul's apostolic authority.

## Events and Customs

**Revelations and raptures (vv. 1–4)** were highly valued in both Jewish and Greco-Roman culture. In Jewish tradition, there was a rich apocalyptic literature describing heavenly journeys, including ideas about multiple heavens (usually three or seven). Paul's mention of "the third heaven" and "paradise" connects with this tradition. In the Greco-Roman context, ecstatic experiences were seen as signs of divine favor, something Paul's opponents likely highlighted about themselves.

**Financial independence (vv. 13–18)** touches on a sensitive cultural issue. In the Greco-Roman patronage system, wealthy supporters expected honor and influence in return for financial support. Paul's refusal of financial aid from the Corinthians was seen by some as an insult—a sign that he did not consider them worthy patrons, or that he lacked authority as a true apostle. His opponents even suggested that Paul was "cunning" and took money through other means (v. 16).

## Key Terms

**"A thorn in the flesh" (v. 7, Greek: *skolops tē sarki*)** is an expression that has caused much discussion. The word *skolops* means a sharp stake, splinter, or thorn. In the Septuagint, similar expressions refer to enemies who harass Israel (Numbers 33:55; Ezekiel 28:24). What Paul's "thorn" specifically was—physical illness, opponents, temptations—remains uncertain, but the context suggests it was something persistent and humiliating that kept him from becoming proud.

**"An angel of Satan" (v. 7, Greek: *angelos Satana*)** reflects the Jewish understanding of Satan as one who is permitted by God to test believers, as we see in the book of Job. This shows that Paul understood his suffering within a theological framework where even evil serves God's purposes.

**"Signs, wonders and mighty works" (v. 12, Greek: *sēmeiois te kai terasin kai dynamesin*)** was a standard triad used to describe apostolic ministry in the early church. These expressions were applied to Moses' signs in the Old Testament and were expected as proof of genuine apostolic authority.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

Papyrus texts from Hellenistic Egypt and inscriptions from this period document that it was common for philosophers and religious teachers to highlight their mystical experiences and visions as evidence of authority. This confirms that Paul operated in a culture where such experiences were social capital. His strategy of "boasting about his weaknesses" (vv. 5, 9–10) represented a radical reversal of these cultural values and must have seemed paradoxical—even provocative—to listeners accustomed to evaluating leaders based on their demonstrated strength and success.
