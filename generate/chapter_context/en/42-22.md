## People

**Judas Iscariot** is mentioned here as one of the twelve who betrayed Jesus. The nickname "Iscariot" (Ἰσκαριώτης) has been interpreted to mean "man from Kerioth" (a town in Judea), which would make him the only non-Galilean among the disciples. Luke describes that "Satan entered Judas" (v. 3) at this point in the narrative, before the betrayal was arranged, giving the act a cosmic dimension.

**Temple officers** (στρατηγοί, vv. 4, 52) were leaders of the temple guard, a Jewish police force that maintained order in the temple area. The commander-in-chief of this force (στρατηγὸς τοῦ ἱεροῦ) was the second-highest religious official after the high priest. These men were responsible for Jesus' arrest.

**The high priest** (v. 54) at this time was Caiaphas (AD 18–36), although his father-in-law Annas still held considerable influence. Luke does not mention the high priest's name in this chapter but refers to his house where the first interrogation took place.

## Places

**The upper room** (ἀνάγαιον μέγα ἐστρωμένον, v. 12) describes a large, furnished room on the second floor. Such rooms were often used for festive meals and could accommodate a group of thirteen people reclining at table. Traditionally, this place has been identified with an area on Mount Zion in Jerusalem, where the "Coenaculum" (the Last Supper room) has been marked since Byzantine times, although the current building dates from the Crusader period.

**The Mount of Olives** (τὸ Ὄρος τῶν Ἐλαιῶν, v. 39) lies east of Jerusalem, separated from the city by the Kidron Valley. Luke notes that Jesus went there "as was his custom" (κατὰ τὸ ἔθος), indicating this was a regular place of prayer during his stay in Jerusalem. Luke does not use the name "Gethsemane" (which appears in Matthew and Mark) but refers to the place as "τόπος" (v. 40).

**The high priest's house** (v. 54) was likely located in the Upper City of Jerusalem, the affluent area west of the temple. Archaeological excavations in the Jewish Quarter have uncovered priestly residences from this period with large courtyards (αὐλή, v. 55) where Peter's denial took place.

## Events and Customs

**The Passover meal** (τὸ πάσχα, cf. ἡ ἑορτὴ τῶν ἀζύμων, vv. 1, 7) was a solemn celebration commemorating the exodus from Egypt. The meal included the slaughter of the Passover lamb in the temple on Nisan 14, followed by a ritual meal with the lamb, unleavened bread, bitter herbs, and wine. The structure of the meal followed a fixed liturgy (Haggadah) involving four cups of wine, blessings, and recitation of the Hallel psalms. Jesus' words over the bread and the cup (vv. 19–20) reinterpret these traditional elements.

**The dining custom** is described with the verb "reclined" (ἀνέπεσεν, v. 14). Participants lay on cushions around a low table, supported by their left elbow with their heads toward the table and feet extended outward. This explains how the betrayer could have his "hand on the table" together with Jesus (v. 21) and how intimate conversation was possible during the meal.

**The title "benefactor"** (εὐεργέτης, v. 25) was an honorific title adopted by Hellenistic kings and Roman emperors. The Ptolemies and Seleucids used this title, which was given to those who funded public buildings or festivals. Jesus contrasts this power-based honor with the way of service.

## Key Terms

**"The new covenant"** (ἡ καινὴ διαθήκη, v. 20) refers directly to Jeremiah 31:31–34, where the prophet foretold a new covenant that would differ from the Sinai covenant. The Greek word διαθήκη can mean both "covenant" and "testament," and both meanings may be intended in the context of Jesus' impending death. Note that vv. 19b-20 present textual critical challenges and are missing in some early manuscripts.

**"Sifting"** (σινιάσαι, v. 31) uses the image of sifting wheat, where grain is shaken in a sieve to separate chaff from kernels. This was a well-known agricultural process in antiquity and serves as a metaphor for testing and trial of faith.

**The Sanhedrin** (τὸ συνέδριον, v. 66) was the highest Jewish governing body. Traditionally it is considered to consist of 71 members including the high priest, other priests, scribes, and elders. It had limited legal authority under Roman rule, especially regarding capital punishment, which required approval from the Roman governor.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem's Upper City have uncovered affluent priestly residences from the first century with characteristic courtyards and stone staircases. These findings illuminate the setting of Peter's denial, where the courtyard with the fire (v. 55) and access for servants correspond to the architectural design of such houses. A particularly relevant find is the so-called "Burnt House" from AD 70, which shows typical features of upper-class dwellings.

Inscriptions from Hellenistic and Roman times confirm the widespread use of the title "benefactor" (εὐεργέτης) that Jesus refers to in v. 25. An inscription from Pergamon honors a certain Diodoros Pasparos as "benefactor" for his gifts to the city, illustrating the social dynamics Jesus criticizes.
