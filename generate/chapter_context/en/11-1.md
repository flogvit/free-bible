# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 1

## People

**Adonijah** was David’s fourth son, born to Haggith in Hebron (2 Sam 3:4). After Amnon and Absalom had died, and Kileab (also called Daniel in 1 Chr 3:1) seemingly disappeared from history, Adonijah became the oldest surviving son and considered himself the natural heir to the throne. His actions—using chariots, horsemen, and fifty runners ahead of him (v. 5)—mirrored exactly Absalom’s earlier rebellion (2 Sam 15:1), a pattern the text assumes readers will recognize.

**Abishag of Shunem** came from a village in the Jezreel Valley within the territory of the tribe of Issachar, in northern Israel. Her role as "sokenet" (סֹכֶנֶת)—a caretaker or attendant—was an accepted function at royal courts in the ancient Near East. The fact that David did not "know" her sexually (v. 4) highlights his physical weakness. She became part of the king’s household without becoming his wife through sexual union, a detail that becomes crucial in chapter 2 when Adonijah requests her.

**The prophet Nathan** had long experience as David’s advisor and here acts as a strategic planner to secure Solomon’s succession. His role in this chapter shows how prophets functioned both as spiritual guides and political actors within the Israelite court.

## Places

**En-Rogel** ("the Well of the Fuller") was located at the junction of the Hinnom Valley and the Kidron Valley, just south of Jerusalem. The site was an important water source outside the city walls and held strategic significance as a gathering place. "The Stone of Bohan" or "the Sliding Stone" (אֶבֶן הַזֹּחֶלֶת) near En-Rogel was likely a well-known cultic stone or landmark where Adonijah held his coronation feast—far enough from the city to avoid attention, yet close enough to maintain symbolic connection with Jerusalem.

**The Gihon Spring** lay on the eastern side of the City of David, in the Kidron Valley. This was Jerusalem’s main water source and a place with strong symbolic associations with kingship. Choosing Gihon over En-Rogel marked a deliberate contrast to Adonijah’s gathering and placed Solomon’s anointing closer to the city itself and David’s palace.

## Events and Customs

**Heir Apparent and Coregency**: In the ancient Near East, primogeniture (the right of the firstborn) was common but not absolutely binding for kings. David’s promise to Bathsheba regarding Solomon represented the king’s right to designate his successor. Coregency—where a new king is installed while the old one still lives—was widespread in Egypt and known from Mesopotamia, ensuring a peaceful transfer of power.

**The Anointing Ritual**: The practice of anointing kings with oil from the holy tent (v. 39) connected the new king with Israel’s cultic traditions. The horn of oil taken from "the tent" (הָאֹהֶל) likely refers to the temporary sanctuary tent that David had set up for the ark of the covenant in Jerusalem. That both a priest (Zadok) and a prophet (Nathan) participated in the anointing gave Solomon’s kingship double legitimacy.

**The Shout "Long Live the King!"** (יְחִי הַמֶּלֶךְ): This acclamation was the public recognition of the new king, known from several cultures in the ancient Near East. The people’s participation gave the kingship popular legitimacy in addition to its ritual foundation.

## Key Terms

**"Grasping the horns of the altar"** (v. 50): The horns of the altar were raised corners on the bronze altar for burnt offerings, and grasping them was a recognized way to seek asylum. This custom is linked to the right of asylum at holy places in ancient Israel, which did not offer protection against intentional murder. Adonijah’s action implicitly acknowledged Solomon’s kingship and was a plea for mercy.

**"Nagid"** (נָגִיד, v. 35): David uses this word meaning "prince" or "leader" to refer to Solomon—the same term used for Saul and David themselves when they were chosen. The term emphasizes that the king is appointed by God as shepherd over the people, not merely a political ruler.
