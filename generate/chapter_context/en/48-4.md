# Historical and Cultural Context for Galatians 4

## People

The chapter introduces several key figures from Genesis in Paul’s allegorical argument: **Abraham** (Ἀβραάμ, v. 22) is mentioned as the father of two sons. **Hagar** (Ἁγάρ, vv. 24–25) was an Egyptian slave woman who became the mother of Ishmael. **Sarah** is referred to as "the free woman" (ἡ ἐλευθέρα, vv. 22–23, 30). **Isaac** (Ἰσαάκ, v. 28) is named as a model for believers, while Ishmael is mentioned indirectly as "the son of the slave woman" (vv. 22–23, 30). In Jewish tradition during Paul’s time, Hagar was often associated with Arab peoples. Interestingly, Paul turns the common Jewish use of this story on its head—while Jews normally identified themselves as Sarah’s children and Gentiles as Hagar’s, Paul argues that those who cling to the law actually belong to Hagar’s line.

## Places

**Mount Sinai** (ὄρος Σινᾶ) is mentioned as the place where the Mosaic Law was given, and Paul locates it "in Arabia" (ἐν τῇ Ἀραβίᾳ, v. 25). This geographical point links Sinai to the Hagar tradition, since Arabia was associated with Hagar’s descendants, the Ishmaelites. **Jerusalem** appears in two forms: the "present Jerusalem" (τῇ νῦν Ἰερουσαλήμ, v. 25), which represents slavery under the law, and "the Jerusalem above" (ἡ ἄνω Ἰερουσαλήμ, v. 26), which represents freedom. This contrast between the earthly and heavenly Jerusalem was a familiar theme in Jewish apocalyptic thought.

## Events and Customs

Paul criticizes the Galatians for being careful to observe "days, months, seasons, and years" (ἡμέρας... καὶ μῆνας καὶ καιροὺς καὶ ἐνιαυτούς, v. 10). This refers to the Jewish religious calendar: Sabbath days, new moon festivals, annual feasts (Passover, Pentecost, Feast of Tabernacles), and sabbatical years. For Gentile Christians considering adopting Jewish practices, this meant a complete restructuring of their daily rhythm. Observance of sacred times was one of the most visible markers of Jewish identity in antiquity.

Paul reminds the Galatians that he first preached the gospel to them "because of an illness" (δι᾽ ἀσθένειαν τῆς σαρκός, v. 13). Despite this physical condition, which could have been a "trial" for them, they did not reject him but welcomed him "as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus" (v. 14). This refers to Paul’s first missionary visit to Galatia.

The image of **guardian and steward** (ἐπίτροπος, οἰκονόμος) in verses 1–2 reflects Greco-Roman inheritance practices. A minor heir, even though legally owned everything, remained under guardians until the date set by his father. This fixed date (προθεσμία) was legally binding and could not be changed after the father’s death.

## Key Terms

**Στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου** ("the elemental spirits of the world" or "basic principles of the world", vv. 3, 9) is a key term with several possible meanings in antiquity: (1) the four elements (earth, air, fire, water), (2) heavenly bodies and astrological forces, (3) basic religious principles, or (4) spiritual beings connected to natural phenomena. Paul’s point is that both Jews under the law and Gentiles under idolatry were enslaved to such powers before Christ.

**Υἱοθεσία** ("adoption as sons", v. 5) was a well-established institution in Roman law. An adopted son received all the rights of a biological son, including inheritance. This was especially powerful for the Galatians, who lived under Roman law and were familiar with this practice.

**Αββα ὁ πατήρ** (v. 6) combines the Aramaic *Abba* with the Greek *ho patēr*—both meaning "Father". This bilingual cry likely reflects early Christian prayer practice originating from Jesus himself (cf. Mark 14:36), showing continuity between Aramaic-speaking and Greek-speaking Christians.

Paul quotes **Isaiah 54:1** in v. 27 about the barren woman who will rejoice. Originally a prophecy concerning Israel’s restoration after exile, Paul applies it to believers who belong to "the free Jerusalem".
