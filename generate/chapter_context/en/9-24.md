# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 24

## Places

**En-Gedi** (עֵין גֶּדִי, "the spring of the kid") is an oasis on the western side of the Dead Sea, located in a region below sea level. The area is characterized by steep cliffs, deep ravines, and numerous caves formed within limestone formations. The oasis has permanent freshwater springs that make it a fertile place in the midst of the Judean wilderness. The geographical conditions—with countless caves and inaccessible terrain—made the area ideal as a refuge for fugitives and rebels throughout history. The name "the crags where the wild goats dwell" (צוּרֵי הַיְּעֵלִים, v. 3) refers to the Nubian ibex that still lives in the region. Archaeological investigations have revealed human activity in the caves at En-Gedi from several periods, including their use as hiding places.

## Events and Customs

The expression "to cover one’s feet" (לְהָסֵךְ אֶת־רַגְלָיו, v. 4) is a euphemism for relieving oneself. Such euphemisms were common in ancient Israel to refer to bodily functions with dignity. The act of cutting off the corner of Saul’s robe carries deep symbolic meaning in this culture. The robe (מְעִיל) was not merely a garment but represented the wearer’s status, authority, and identity. Cutting off the king’s robe could be interpreted as a symbolic attack on his royal dignity—something that explains David’s guilty conscience (vv. 6–7). In 1 Samuel 15:27–28, Samuel tears Saul’s robe, and this is understood as a sign that the kingdom will be torn from him.

## Key Terms

**"The Lord’s anointed"** (מְשִׁיחַ יְהוָה) is a central concept in this chapter, used three times (v. 7 [twice], v. 11). Anointing with oil marked divine selection and installation into office. Although Saul had rejected God’s will and David himself had been anointed as his successor, David still recognized Saul’s status as the Lord’s anointed. To "lay a hand on" the anointed was considered a crime against God Himself, who had chosen and consecrated that person.

**"The old proverb"** (מְשַׁל הַקַּדְמֹנִי, v. 14)—"From the wicked comes wickedness"—shows that Israel had an established wisdom tradition of proverbs cited to illuminate ethical situations. David uses this to argue that his actions prove his innocence: since he does not do evil, he cannot be evil.

## People

David’s men are described as a group that interprets the situation theologically—they see Saul’s vulnerability as God fulfilling a promise (v. 5). This shows how warrior bands in this period expected divine guidance and looked for signs of God’s will in the course of events. David’s authority as leader is demonstrated when he "restrained them with words" (וַיְשַׁסַּע... בַּדְּבָרִים) and prevented them from acting according to their desire.
