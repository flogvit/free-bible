# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Peter 3

## People

The chapter explicitly mentions "our beloved brother Paul" (v. 15), which is remarkable because it is the only place in the New Testament where another New Testament author directly refers to Paul's letters as authoritative writings. The writer speaks of Paul with respect and acknowledges his wisdom as God-given. The reference that Paul "has written to you" suggests that the recipients were familiar with Pauline letters, possibly Romans or one of the letters to churches in Asia Minor. The fact that Paul's letters were already circulating and being read alongside "the other Scriptures" (v. 16) indicates an early recognition of them as authoritative texts on par with the Old Testament.

## Events and Customs

The chapter addresses the issue of "scoffers" (ἐμπαῖκται, *empaiktai*) who mockingly ask, "Where is the promise of his coming?" (v. 4). This reflects a real crisis in the early church toward the end of the first century, when the first generation of Christians began to die without Christ having returned. The expression "the fathers fell asleep" (v. 4) likely refers to the first generation of believers, perhaps including the apostles. This "delay of the parousia" (Christ's return) created theological uncertainty and gave rise to skeptics who claimed that the world continued unchanged "from the beginning of creation."

## Key Terms

The word **παρουσία** (*parousia*, vv. 4, 12) was a technical term in antiquity for the official arrival of a king or emperor into a city. The term carried expectations of preparation, purification, and celebration. For Christians, it became the central word for Christ's expected return. **Στοιχεῖα** (*stoicheia*, vv. 10, 12), translated as "elements" or "basic principles," referred in Greek philosophy to the four fundamental elements (earth, water, air, fire) that made up the universe, or to heavenly bodies. The writer's imagery that these would be "dissolved by fire" communicated to Greek readers a total cosmic upheaval. The expression **"the day of the Lord"** (ἡμέρα κυρίου, v. 10) has roots in prophetic literature and denotes God's final intervention in history.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The idea of the world's destruction by fire (*ekpyrosis*) was well known in Stoic philosophy at that time. The Stoics taught that the world was periodically destroyed by fire and reborn in cycles. The writer uses similar language (vv. 7, 10, 12), but with significant differences: here it is a one-time event leading to "new heavens and a new earth" (v. 13), not an endless repetition. This use of language shows how New Testament authors could adopt contemporary philosophical concepts and reshape them within a biblical framework. The reference to the flood as a type (vv. 5–6) also connects to Jewish apocalyptic tradition, where the flood served as typology for the coming judgment.
