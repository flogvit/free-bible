# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 66

## Events and Customs

The psalm contains clear references to the Exodus events in verse 6, where it is stated that God "turned the sea into dry land" and that the people "walked through the river on foot." This points directly to the crossing of the Red Sea (Exodus 14) and likely also the Jordan River (Joshua 3). These acts of deliverance served as foundational testimonies of God's power in Israelite collective memory and were regularly remembered in temple worship.

Verses 13–15 describe in detail the fulfillment of vows (*nedarim*) through burnt offerings. The sacrificial animals mentioned—fatlings (*mechim*), rams (*eilim*), cattle (*baqar*), and male goats (*attudim*)—along with incense (*qetoret*), represent a comprehensive and costly thank offering. Such vows were typically made in times of distress, and their fulfillment at the temple was a public confession of God's faithfulness.

## Key Terms

The expression *tzaraf'tanu kitzrof-kesef* ("you refined us as silver is refined") in verse 10 uses metallurgical imagery that was well known in the ancient Near East. Silver refining required intense heat to remove impurities, and the metaphor describes God's testing actions toward His people—suffering with a purifying purpose.

The word *metsuda* ("fortress" or "net/trap") in verse 11 can be translated either way. In the context of distress, it likely describes a situation of captivity, possibly alluding to experiences of exile or military oppression.

The term *revaya* in verse 12 means "abundance" or "prosperity" and describes the goal of deliverance—from distress to blessing. This pattern of suffering-deliverance-thanksgiving structures the entire psalm.
