## Places

The chapter mentions several geographic areas known for their fertility and beauty. **Lebanon** (הַלְּבָנוֹן) was famous for its cedar trees and lush forests. **Carmel** (הַכַּרְמֶל) was a fertile mountain region along the Mediterranean coast, known for its vineyards and rich vegetation. **Sharon** (הַשָּׁרוֹן) was the coastal plain between Jaffa and Carmel, one of the most fertile regions in the land. When the prophet says that the wilderness will receive "the glory of Lebanon" and "the splendor of Carmel and Sharon", he uses these places as symbols of the highest form of natural beauty and abundance.

**Zion** (צִיּוֹן) is mentioned in verse 10 as the destination for the redeemed. This was the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, and the name is often used poetically to refer to the entire city as God’s dwelling place and the spiritual center of His people.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **חֲבַצֶּלֶת** (chavatselet) in verse 1 is a flower traditionally translated as "lily" or "crocus". It grows in dry areas and blooms quickly after rain, making it a fitting image of sudden transformation.

The expression **דֶּרֶךְ הַקֹּדֶשׁ** ("the holy way") in verse 8 refers to a processional road for those returning to Jerusalem. In ancient Near Eastern culture, such sacred roads were known from Babylonian and Assyrian cities, where they were used in religious processions. Here the concept is democratized—the way is for all the redeemed, not only for kings and priests.

The word **גְּאוּלִים** ("the redeemed") in verse 9 comes from the root גאל, which describes a kinsman’s duty to buy back family members from slavery or debt. When this is applied to God, He is portrayed as Israel’s nearest relative who redeems His people.

## Events and Customs

The description of healing for the blind, deaf, lame, and mute (verses 5–6) stands in contrast to how disabilities were often associated with ritual impurity in ancient times. The prophecy shows that the time of salvation involves full participation for all and a complete restoration of creation.

The image of water springing up in the wilderness (verses 6–7) had special resonance in the dry landscape of Judah. Words like **שָׁרָב** (heat mirage/air shimmer) and **צִמָּאוֹן** (thirst/dry land) describe the harsh reality of the desert, while **אֲגַם** (pool) and **מַבּוּעֵי מָיִם** (springs of water) represent life-giving abundance. The plants **קָנֶה** (reed) and **גֹּמֶא** (papyrus) grow only where there is plenty of water, and their presence would have been an unmistakable sign of lasting change.
