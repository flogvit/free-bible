# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 4

## People

**Abraham (Ἀβραάμ)** stands completely central in this chapter as Paul's main example. In first-century Jewish tradition, Abraham was not only the patriarch but the very ideal of righteousness. Jewish sources such as the Book of Jubilees and 1 Maccabees highlight Abraham's obedience to the law and his willingness to sacrifice Isaac as the basis for his righteous status. Paul challenges this interpretation by pointing to the chronological order in Genesis: Abraham was counted as righteous (Gen 15:6) before he was circumcised (Gen 17), an argument that would have been surprising to many Jewish readers. In verse 19, it is mentioned that Abraham was "about a hundred years old" (ἑκατονταετής που), which underscores the miraculous nature of God's promise.

**Sarah (Σάρρα)** is mentioned in verse 19 in connection with her womb being "dead" (νέκρωσις τῆς μήτρας). Together with Abraham's advanced age, this emphasizes the human impossibility of the promise of descendants, and thus the magnitude of Abraham's faith.

**David (Δαυίδ)** is introduced as a supporting witness from Psalm 32. By quoting David, Israel's most honored king, Paul strengthens his argument with another authority from Scripture. In Jewish tradition, David was also linked to messianic expectation, which made his testimony particularly relevant.

**Jesus (Ἰησοῦς)** is mentioned at the conclusion of the chapter (verses 24–25) as "our Lord" (τὸν κύριον ἡμῶν). He is described as the one who "was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification". This connects Abraham's story of faith to Christian saving faith and shows that the same justification by faith applies to all believers.

## Events and Customs

**Circumcision (περιτομή)** was the central identity marker for Jewish men and dated back to the covenant with Abraham in Genesis 17. In Paul's time, circumcision was a hot-button issue in the early church, especially the question of whether Gentile Christians had to be circumcised. Paul's point that Abraham was justified *before* circumcision was theologically explosive—it challenged the common understanding of circumcision as the entryway into the covenant relationship with God.

**Accounting terminology** characterizes the chapter. The verb λογίζομαι (to credit, count) appears repeatedly throughout the entire chapter. This word was drawn from the commercial and accounting language of the Greco-Roman world, where it meant to enter something into an account, to credit, or to debit. Paul uses this familiar economic metaphor to explain how God "credits" righteousness to the one who believes.

## Key Terms

**Δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosyne – righteousness)** is a key term with roots in the Hebrew *tsedaqah*. In a Jewish context, this described God's faithfulness to the covenant and humanity's right relationship with God and fellow humans. Paul argues that this righteousness is credited through faith, not achieved through works.

**Προπάτωρ (propator – forefather)** in verse 1 (in the accusative form προπάτορα) is a rare word that emphasizes Abraham's unique position. The expression "κατὰ σάρκα" (according to the flesh) points to physical kinship, but Paul wants to show that spiritual kinship through faith is what matters.

**Σφραγίς (sfragis – seal)** in verse 11 describes circumcision as a "seal" of the righteousness Abraham already possessed. In antiquity, seals were used to confirm and authenticate documents—circumcision thus confirmed something that was already established; it did not create it.

**Ἐπαγγελία (epangelia – promise)** occurs several times and is contrasted with νόμος (the law). Paul emphasizes that God's promise to Abraham came before the law was given, showing that the promise has priority and does not depend on law-keeping.
