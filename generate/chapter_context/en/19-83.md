# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 83

## People

The psalm mentions a number of peoples and nations that form a coalition against Israel. **Edom** were the descendants of Esau, settled southeast of the Dead Sea. The **Ishmaelites** were nomadic tribes traditionally regarded as descendants of Ishmael. **Moab** and **Ammon** ("Lot's sons," v. 9) were neighboring tribes east of the Jordan, related to Israel through Lot. The **Hagrites** were an Arab tribal group, possibly connected with Hagar. **Gebal** may refer either to a region in northern Edom or to the Phoenician city of Byblos (modern Jbeil in Lebanon); the context with Tyre may support the latter interpretation. **Amalek** was a nomadic people in the Negev desert, traditionally Israel's archenemy. The **Philistines** controlled the coastal plain, while **Tyre** was the powerful Phoenician trading city. **Assyria** is mentioned as an ally of the coalition, which may indicate a dating to the period of Assyrian supremacy (800–600 BC).

The psalm also refers to historical figures from the past: **Sisera** was the commander of the Canaanite king Jabin of Hazor and was defeated by Deborah and Barak. **Jabin** was the king whom Sisera served. **Oreb and Zeeb** were Midianite chieftains, while **Zebah and Zalmunna** were Midianite kings—all defeated by Gideon.

## Places

The **Kishon Brook** (v. 10) is the river that flows through the Jezreel Valley, where Sisera suffered defeat when his chariots sank into the mud during a heavy flood (Judg 4–5). **En-Dor** (v. 11) lies at the foot of Mount Tabor, near the battlefield. The place is also known from the account of Saul and the medium (1 Sam 28), but in this psalm it is linked to the defeat of Sisera and the Midianites. Geographically, the coalition stretches from Assyria in the northeast to Amalek in the south, and from Tyre on the Mediterranean coast to Moab and Ammon in the east—an image of total encirclement of Israel.

## Events and Customs

The psalm reflects the Old Testament tradition of "holy war," in which God himself fights for his people against overwhelming enemies. The references to Gideon's victory over the Midianites (Judg 7–8) and Deborah's victory over Sisera (Judg 4–5) serve as paradigms of divine intervention. In both cases, Israel prevailed against seemingly impossible odds through God's direct action. To become "like refuse for the ground" (v. 11) was the ultimate disgrace—being denied a dignified burial meant in the ancient Near East that one was completely wiped out and forgotten.

## Key Terms

The expression **צְפוּנֶיךָ** (*tsefunekha*, "your hidden ones," v. 5) refers to God's specially protected possession—Israel as his treasure. The verb **נָשְׂאוּ רֹאשׁ** ("lift their head," v. 3) describes arrogant rebellion and a defiant attitude. **כַגַּלְגַּל** (*kagalgal*, v. 14) means "like a rolling wheel" or "like thistle driven by the wind"—an image of total helplessness. The term **נְאוֹת אֱלֹהִים** ("pastures of God," v. 13) refers to the promised land that the enemies seek to take possession of—God's own territory on earth.
