# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 32

## People

**Hoshea (הוֹשֵׁעַ) son of Nun** is mentioned in verse 44 by his original name, not the more familiar "Joshua" (יְהוֹשֻׁעַ). Moses had previously changed his name (Numbers 13:16) by adding an abbreviation of God's name. The use of his original name here may indicate an early tradition or emphasize continuity from Moses' leadership to his successor.

## Places

**Mount Nebo** (הַר נְבוֹ, v. 49) lies in the Abarim mountain range in present-day Jordan, approximately 817 meters above sea level. From its summit, there is a panoramic view over the Jordan Valley, Jericho, and the northern part of the Dead Sea. Archaeological investigations have identified the site with Jebel Neba, where a church was built during Byzantine times to commemorate Moses. The mountain is located in the land of Moab, east of where the Jordan River empties into the Dead Sea.

**Mount Hor** (הֹר הָהָר, v. 50) is mentioned as the place where Aaron died. The mountain lies on the border of Edom, and Aaron's death there is described in Numbers 20:22-29.

**Meribah-Kadesh** (מֵי מְרִיבַת קָדֵשׁ, v. 51) refers to the event described in Numbers 20, where Moses struck the rock instead of speaking to it. Kadesh-Barnea is identified with Ein el-Qudeirat in northeastern Sinai, an oasis with a rich water source that served as a natural gathering place for nomadic groups.

## Key Terms

**Jeshurun (יְשֻׁרוּן)** in verse 15 is a poetic term of endearment for Israel, derived from the root "yashar," meaning "upright" or "sincere." The name is used here with bitter irony—the "upright one" became fat and kicked against God. This name appears only four times in the Old Testament (also in Deuteronomy 33:5, 26 and Isaiah 44:2).

**Tsur (צוּר)** – "Rock" – is used as a divine title several times in the song (vv. 4, 15, 18, 30, 31). This image emphasizes God's stability, reliability, and eternal nature, in contrast to the unstable "no-gods" Israel turned to. The metaphor was especially meaningful in the rocky landscape of Canaan and Transjordan. In verse 37, the same word is used ironically regarding idols.

**Shedim (שֵׁדִים)** in verse 17, translated as "demons" or "evil spirits," is a loanword from Akkadian "shedu"—supernatural beings in Mesopotamian religion. The word appears only here and in Psalm 106:37 in the Old Testament, marking idolatry as demonic delusion rather than mere empty superstition.

## Events and Customs

Chapter 32 contains "The Song of Moses" (שִׁירַת מֹשֶׁה), which belongs to the genre of "rib" or covenant lawsuit—a legal complaint in which God calls heaven and earth as witnesses (v. 1) against His faithless people.

The song's description of Israel as God's "inheritance" (נַחֲלָה) and "portion" (חֵלֶק) in verse 9 reflects the ancient Near Eastern system of land distribution by casting lots. When God chooses Israel as His "inheritance," this reverses the usual concept—normally a people receive a deity as their protector, but here it is God who "inherits" a people. This underscores the intimate covenant relationship.

The reference to God setting the boundaries of the nations "according to the number of the sons of Israel" (v. 8) has sparked discussion. The Septuagint and a Qumran fragment (4QDeutj) read "sons of God" (בְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים) instead of "sons of Israel," which may reflect the idea that the nations were assigned to angelic princes, while Israel became the Lord's own portion.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Masoretic Text marks the song with a special layout (שירה), where the text is written in two columns—a practice reserved for the most significant poetic texts in the Bible. Fragments from Qumran (4QDeutj, 4QDeutq) contain portions of the Song of Moses and confirm the text's great age and liturgical significance in early Jewish worship life.
