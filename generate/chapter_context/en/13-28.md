# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 28

## People

The chapter describes a grand assembly of Israel's leadership where David delivers his farewell address. The various groups mentioned—tribal leaders, division commanders, military officers over thousands and hundreds, administrators of royal property, court officials (סָרִיסִים), and warriors—represent the administrative structure David had built up throughout his reign. The term "saris" (סָרִיס) refers to high-ranking court officials; the word can mean "court official" or "eunuch," and the practice of employing such officials was known in surrounding kingdoms. Solomon is presented as God's chosen one, appointed to sit on "the throne of the Lord" (v. 5)—an expression that emphasizes that the kingdom belongs to God and that the earthly king is His representative.

## Places

Jerusalem, where the assembly takes place, was by this time established as both the political and religious capital. David had conquered the city from the Jebusites and brought the ark of the covenant there. The chapter prepares for the transition from the temporary tent David had erected for the ark (2 Sam 6:17) to the permanent temple that Solomon would build. The temple plans handed over include the porch (אוּלָם), the main building with its various rooms, the treasuries, the upper chambers, and "the room of the mercy seat" (בֵּית הַכַּפֹּרֶת)—the Most Holy Place where the ark was to stand.

## Events and Customs

The handing over of the temple plans (תַּבְנִית) from David to Solomon has clear parallels with how Moses received the tabernacle plans on Sinai (Exod 25:9, 40). The expression "all this in writing from the hand of the Lord upon me, he made me understand" (v. 19) indicates that the plans were understood as divine revelation rather than human architecture. This pattern—where a divine "original" is transferred to earthly execution—was known in the ancient Near East, where temples were often regarded as earthly copies of heavenly models.

David's reason for not being able to build the temple himself—"you are a man of war and have shed blood" (v. 3)—reflects the belief that the sanctuary required ritual purity and peace. This contrasts with the name Solomon (שְׁלֹמֹה), which is related to the word for peace (שָׁלוֹם).

## Key Terms

The expression "a resting place for the ark of the Lord's covenant and a footstool for our God" (v. 2) uses the term הֲדֹם (hadóm, footstool)—a metaphor in which the ark and temple are seen as the place where the heavenly King sets His feet, while His throne is in heaven (cf. Isa 66:1). The phrase "the chariot, the cherubim" (הַמֶּרְכָּבָה הַכְּרֻבִים, v. 18) describes the cherubim above the mercy seat as a divine chariot or throne, an image also found in Ezekiel's visions (Ezek 1; 10).
