# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 71

## Key Terms

The psalm contains several Hebrew expressions that illuminate the situation of the aging petitioner. The word **גוֹזִי** (*gozi*) in verse 6 is rare and is often translated as "the one who took me out" or "the one who separated me from my mother's womb," pointing to God as the one who guided birth—a role otherwise assigned to the midwife. This underscores the psalmist's lifelong dependence on God.

The expression **כְּמוֹפֵת** (*kemofet*, "like a sign/wonder") in verse 7 can mean both an omen and a miracle. In this context, it describes how the psalmist's life—with all its trials—has become a public example for others, either as a warning or as testimony to God's faithfulness.

**תְּהֹמוֹת הָאָרֶץ** (*tehomot ha'arets*, "the depths of the earth") in verse 20 refers to the cosmic abyss beneath the earth, associated with the realm of the dead in ancient Near Eastern thought. The prayer to be "brought up again" from this depth expresses hope for deliverance from a state akin to death.

## Events and Customs

The psalm's references to **נֶבֶל** (*nevel*, harp) and **כִּנּוֹר** (*kinnor*, lyre) in verse 22 reflect the practices of temple music. These instruments were central to Israel's worship and were played by Levitical musicians. The promise to praise God with these instruments may indicate a formal thanksgiving service in the temple following experienced deliverance.

The psalm's strong emphasis on "proclaiming" and "telling" (verses 15, 17, 18) reflects the Israelite custom of orally transmitting the story of salvation. The older generation had a sacred responsibility to pass on testimony about God's deeds to future generations, as we also see in Deuteronomy 6:7.

## Chapter-Specific Observations

Psalm 71 has no heading in the Hebrew text, which is unusual for this part of the Book of Psalms. It shares significant linguistic material with Psalms 22, 31, 35, and 70. In particular, verses 1–3 have strong parallels to Psalm 31:2–4, and the psalm immediately follows Psalm 70, with which it shares thematic elements. This has led to discussion about whether the psalm represents a revised collection of older psalmic elements. The Greek Septuagint translation attributes the psalm to David and links it to "the sons of Jonadab and the first captives," but this attribution is not found in the Hebrew text.
