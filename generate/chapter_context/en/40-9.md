# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 9

## People

**Matthew (Levi) the tax collector** (v. 9) was employed at the tax station (τὸ τελώνιον) in Capernaum. As a tax collector, he likely worked for Tetrarch Herod Antipas and collected taxes on goods passing through the region. Tax collectors (τελῶναι) were despised by Jews for two reasons: they collaborated with the Roman occupying power, and they were known to demand more than what was legally required. That Jesus called such a person to become a disciple was socially shocking.

**The synagogue ruler** (ἄρχων, v. 18) was a respected leader in the local community responsible for synagogue services. Mark 5:22 identifies him as Jairus. The fact that such a prominent figure threw himself at Jesus' feet and begged for help shows desperation that overcame social barriers.

## Places

**"His own town"** (τὴν ἰδίαν πόλιν, v. 1) refers to Capernaum, which served as Jesus' base during his ministry in Galilee (cf. Matt 4:13). The city was located on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee and was a hub for trade and fishing. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of a synagogue and what is traditionally identified as Peter's house, which may have been the place where the healing of the paralytic took place.

## Events and Customs

**Fellowship meals with tax collectors and sinners** (vv. 10–11) were far more than simply eating together. In Jewish culture, sharing a meal expressed acceptance and equality. The Pharisees practiced strict purity regulations regarding meals and would not eat with those they considered unclean. Jesus' willingness to recline at table (συνανέκειντο – reclining was the common way to eat festive meals) with such people challenged fundamental social and religious boundaries.

**Fasting** (vv. 14–15) was an established practice of piety. The Law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement, but the Pharisees fasted voluntarily twice a week (Monday and Thursday). John's disciples also followed strict fasting customs. Jesus' defense that his disciples did not fast used the image of a wedding – guests do not fast while the bridegroom is present.

**Burial rituals** (vv. 23–24) began immediately after death. Flute players (αὐλητάς) and professional mourners were mandatory even in poor families – according to the Mishnah, even the poorest man was required to provide at least two flute players and one professional mourner for his wife's funeral. The fact that these people were already present confirms that the girl had died.

**Touching the hem of the garment** (v. 20) referred to κράσπεδον, the tassels or fringes that all Jewish men were required to have on their garments according to Numbers 15:38–39. These symbolized obedience to God's commands. The woman with the issue of blood was ritually unclean according to Leviticus 15:25–27 and normally should not touch others.

## Key Terms

**"Son of Man"** (ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, v. 6) is a title with background in Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man receives eternal dominion from God. Jesus uses this title here to claim divine authority to forgive sins – something the scribes rightly understood as a demand only God could fulfill.

**"Son of David"** (υἱὲ Δαυίδ, v. 27) is a messianic title based on God's promises to David concerning an eternal royal line (2 Sam 7). The fact that the blind men use this title shows popular messianic expectation connected with healing – Isaiah 35:5 predicted that the Messiah's time would bring sight to the blind.

**"Mercy, not sacrifice"** (Ἔλεος θέλω καὶ οὐ θυσίαν, v. 13) is a quotation from Hosea 6:6. Jesus uses the prophet's words to criticize a religiosity that prioritizes ritual purity over love for one's neighbor.
