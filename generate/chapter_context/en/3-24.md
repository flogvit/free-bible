# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 24

## People

The chapter introduces a specific individual: the son of an Israelite woman (Shelomith, daughter of Dibri from the tribe of Dan) and an Egyptian father. This mixed heritage is central to the narrative. In ancient Israel, tribal affiliation normally followed the father's line, which made this man's social and tribal status unclear since his father was Egyptian—possibly a contributing factor to the conflict mentioned in verse 10. Shelomith is the only woman named in Leviticus, and her name (from Hebrew *shalom*, "peace") stands in contrast to her son's actions.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes several important religious customs. **The lamp in the tabernacle** (vv. 1–4) was to burn continuously with pure, pressed olive oil (*shemen zayit zakh katit*, שֶׁמֶן זַיִת זָךְ כָּתִית). This oil was produced by crushing olives without grinding them, yielding the purest oil without sediment. **The showbread** (vv. 5–9) consisted of twelve loaves representing the twelve tribes of Israel. The breads were replaced every Sabbath and eaten by the priests in a holy place, as part of the covenant relationship between God and His people.

**Stoning** (vv. 14, 23) was a public method of execution in which the witnesses first laid their hands on the head of the guilty person—an act that transferred responsibility and confirmed their testimony. That the execution took place "outside the camp" was important for maintaining the ritual purity of the camp.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew verb **נָקַב** (*naqav*) in verses 11 and 16 literally means "to pierce" or "to state/specificate clearly." Combined with **וַיְקַלֵּל** (*vayqallel*, "and cursed") in verse 11, it shows that the action involved both uttering the Name and cursing. In this context, it refers to blasphemously pronouncing God's holy name (YHWH). Jewish tradition later developed a strong aversion to pronouncing this name at all.

The term **אֶזְרָח** (*ezrach*, "native-born") contrasted with **גֵּר** (*ger*, "foreigner/sojourner") is central in verses 16 and 22. The text emphasizes that the same law applies to both groups—a remarkable expression of equality before the law.

The principle **עַיִן תַּחַת עַיִן** (*ayin tachat ayin*, "eye for eye") in verse 20 is called *lex talionis* (the law of retaliation). Rabbinic tradition understood this primarily as a principle of proportional financial compensation rather than literal mutilation. Biblical law stresses that it applies equally to all, regardless of social status.
