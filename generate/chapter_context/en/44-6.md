# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 6

## People

**The Seven Servants**: All seven named men have Greek names, indicating that they likely represented the Hellenistic group that had raised the complaint. Stephen (Στέφανος, "crown/victory crown") is highlighted as the leader and prominent speaker. Nicolas from Antioch is specifically described as a *prosēlytos* (προσήλυτος) – a Gentile who had converted to Judaism before becoming a Christian. This shows that the church already included people with diverse ethnic and religious backgrounds. Philip is mentioned as one of the seven.

**Conversion of Priests**: Verse 7 notes that "a large number of priests became obedient to the faith." At that time, there were estimated to be between 8,000 and 18,000 priests in total in Israel, organized into 24 divisions that served on rotation in the temple. The fact that many of them joined the believers was remarkable, since they had much to lose socially and economically by following the Jesus movement.

## Places

**The Synagogue of the Freedmen**: The expression *synagōgē tōn Libertinōn* (συναγωγῆς τῆς λεγομένης Λιβερτίνων) refers to a synagogue for "freedmen" – descendants of Jews who had been slaves in Rome but were later set free. Many of these returned to Jerusalem. A Theodotos inscription found on the Ophel hill in Jerusalem (dated before 70 AD) describes a synagogue built for "reading of the law and teaching of the commandments," with an attached guesthouse for pilgrims. Discovered in 1913, the inscription mentions that Theodotos was the son of Vettenus (a Latin name suggesting a freedman background). This archaeological finding directly confirms the existence of diaspora synagogues in Jerusalem. The chapter also mentions members from Cyrene (Libya), Alexandria (Egypt), Cilicia, and Asia (Asia Minor), showing the diverse Jewish community in the city.

## Events and Customs

**Daily Food Distribution**: The *diakonia kathēmerinē* (διακονίᾳ τῇ καθημερινῇ, "daily service") to widows reflects an established Jewish charitable practice. In Jewish tradition, there was a *tamhui* (daily food distribution to traveling poor) and a *quppah* (weekly collection for local needy). Widows held a particularly vulnerable position in antiquity without a male provider, and caring for them was a central duty in both Jewish law and prophetic tradition.

**Laying on of Hands**: The ritual where the apostles laid hands on the seven (ἐπέθηκαν αὐτοῖς τὰς χεῖρας) had deep roots in Jewish tradition. In the Old Testament, laying on of hands was used for transferring blessing and authority, such as when Moses appointed Joshua (Numbers 27:18–23). In the synagogue, rabbis were ordained through *semikah* (laying on of hands). For the early church, this marked appointment to an recognized ministry.

## Key Terms

**Hellenists and Hebrews**: The distinction between *Hellēnistōn* (Ἑλληνιστῶν) and *Hebraious* (Ἑβραίους) does not refer to ethnicity but to language and culture. The Hellenists were Greek-speaking Jews, often from the diaspora, who used the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament). The Hebrews were Aramaic/Hebrew-speaking Jews from Palestine. This linguistic and cultural difference created natural groupings even within the believing community.

**Diakonia**: The word *diakonia* (διακονία) is used in two ways in this chapter – for "serving at tables" (verse 2) and for "the ministry of the word" (verse 4). Its basic meaning is "service" or "ministry," and the chapter shows how the church began to differentiate between various forms of service without ranking them hierarchically.
