# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 22

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains a collection of civil and religious laws regulating daily life in ancient Israel. The laws concerning theft and restitution reflect how livestock such as oxen, donkeys, and sheep constituted the family's most important property. The double restitution required for stolen animals found alive (v. 3) was milder than other ancient laws, which could demand much higher compensation.

The provisions regarding the custody of property (vv. 6–14) show that deposit arrangements were common in antiquity, where neighbors kept valuables for one another in the absence of banks. The instruction to bring the case "before God" (אֶל־הָאֱלֹהִים, *el-ha'elohim*, vv. 7–8) likely refers to a legal process at the sanctuary, where an oath was sworn.

The bride-price (מֹהַר, *mohar*) mentioned in verse 16 was compensation paid to the bride's family and was standard practice throughout the ancient Near East. This was not a "purchase" of the bride but served to secure her financial standing.

## Key Terms

The word **גֵּר** (*ger*, "foreigner/sojourner") in verse 20 designates a person who lived permanently in Israel without belonging to one of the tribes. These individuals had no inheritance rights to land and were therefore economically vulnerable. The command to protect them is grounded theologically in Israel's own experience as foreigners in Egypt.

**נֶשֶׁךְ** (*neshekh*, "interest") in verse 24 literally means "bite" and describes how interest "bites away" at the borrower's wealth. The prohibition against interest applied to loans given to poor Israelites and was intended to prevent debt slavery from arising.

The expression **מְכַשֵּׁפָה** (*mekhashefa*, "sorceress") in verse 17 refers to someone who practiced harmful magic. In the ancient Near East, such practices were viewed as attempts to manipulate divine powers and undermine social order.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from Bronze Age Canaan confirm the economic importance of livestock described in the chapter. Seal impressions and ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from Israelite cities show that deposit arrangements and loan contracts were widespread. An ostracon from Mesad Hashavyahu (ca. 630 B.C.) records a worker complaining about the seizure of his cloak—a direct parallel to the law concerning the pledge of cloaks in verses 25–26. This demonstrates that these laws were practiced in daily life in ancient Israel.
