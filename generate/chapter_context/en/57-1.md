## People

The chapter mentions several individuals who provide insight into early Christian networks. **Philemon** (Φιλήμων, "the loving") was the recipient of the letter and owner of the slave Onesimus. He hosted a house church (verse 2). **Apphia** (Ἀπφία, verse 2) is referred to as "sister" (τῇ ἀδελφῇ) and was likely Philemon's wife. **Archippus** (Ἄρχιππος, "master of horses") is called "our fellow soldier" (τῷ συστρατιώτῃ ἡμῶν, verse 2).

**Onesimus** (Ὀνήσιμος, "useful") was a slave belonging to Philemon. Paul calls him "my child, whom I became father to while in chains" (verse 10). In verse 11, Paul makes a play on words: the one who was "useless" (ἄχρηστος) is now "useful" (εὔχρηστος). The greetings in verses 23–24 mention **Epaphras** as "my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus" (συναιχμάλωτός μου), along with **Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke**—all described as Paul's coworkers (οἱ συνεργοί μου).

## Events and Customs

**Onesimus' situation**: The text does not explicitly state that he ran away. Paul urges Philemon to receive him back (verses 12, 17) and offers to cover any debt or damage (verses 18–19). Paul's appeal to welcome him "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (verse 16) represented a radical reinterpretation of the master-slave relationship.

The chapter is addressed to **the house church** (ἡ κατʼ οἶκόν σου ἐκκλησία, verse 2) in addition to Philemon personally, making the appeal public within the community.

## Key Terms

The expression **δέσμιος Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ** ("prisoner of Christ Jesus," verses 1 and 9) is theologically charged. Paul does not describe himself as a Roman prisoner but as Christ's prisoner—his imprisonment serves the gospel. The word **πρεσβύτης** (verse 9) means "old man," but some manuscripts and scholars read πρεσβευτής ("ambassador"). Both interpretations make sense in context: Paul appeals either to his advanced age as a basis for respect, or to his apostolic authority as Christ's representative.

**Σπλάγχνα** ("innards," verses 7, 12, 20) refers to the internal organs as the seat of deep emotions—equivalent to our "heart." When Paul calls Onesimus his "innards" (τὰ ἐμὰ σπλάγχνα, verse 12), he expresses deep personal attachment. The verb **ἐλλόγα** (verse 18, "charge it to my account") is a technical accounting term showing that Paul gives a formal financial guarantee.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The wordplay in verse 11 between ἄχρηστος ("useless") and εὔχρηστος ("useful") may also play on the similarity with Χριστός (Christos). In verse 20, Paul uses the verb ὀναίμην ("let me gain benefit"), which is related to the name Ὀνήσιμος, in a direct appeal.

Paul's personal guarantee in verses 18–19 ("ἐγὼ Παῦλος ἔγραψα τῇ ἐμῇ χειρί" – "I, Paul, write this with my own hand") marks that he personally takes responsibility for any debt or damage Onesimus may have caused.
