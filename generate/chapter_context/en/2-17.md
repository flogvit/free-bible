# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 17

## People

**Joshua (יְהוֹשֻׁעַ, Yehoshua)** is introduced for the first time in the Bible in this chapter. The name means "The Lord saves" or "The Lord is salvation." He is presented as Moses' military leader and trusted servant. His appearance as a commander suggests that he had already established himself as a trusted leader among the people.

**Hur (חוּר)** is mentioned here for the first time alongside Aaron. According to Jewish tradition (Sotah 11b), Hur was the son of Caleb and Miriam (Moses' sister), which would make him Moses' nephew by marriage. This is not confirmed in the Bible itself. He played an important role in supporting Moses during the battle and is also mentioned in Exodus 24:14.

## Places

**Rephidim (רְפִידִים)** was a campsite on the way from Egypt to Sinai. The name may mean "places of rest" or "supporting places." Its exact location is uncertain, but it is traditionally placed in the Wadi Feiran area on the Sinai Peninsula, which is the largest oasis in the region. Archaeological surveys have uncovered traces of ancient settlements in this area.

**Horeb (חֹרֵב)** is used here to refer to the mountain range also called Sinai. The rock where God stood (v. 6) is connected with this holy place. The water from the rock at Horeb became an important symbol in later biblical and Jewish tradition for God's care in the wilderness (cf. Ps 78:15-16; 105:41; Isa 48:21).

## Events and Customs

**The attack by the Amalekites** represents the first military conflict Israel faced after leaving Egypt. The Amalekites were a nomadic people who controlled trade routes in the Negev desert and the Sinai region. They likely attacked Israel because the Israelites' journey threatened their territory and water resources. In the ancient Near East, control over water sources was often a cause of conflict between nomadic groups.

**Moses' raised hands** during the battle have parallels in ancient Near Eastern practice where uplifted hands symbolized prayer and invocation of divine help. In Egyptian and Mesopotamian iconography, priests and kings are often depicted with raised hands in prayer. The fact that the hands had to be held up continuously emphasizes that victory came from God, not from human strength alone.

## Key Terms

**Massah and Meribah (מַסָּה וּמְרִיבָה)** are place names with theological significance. "Massah" means "testing" or "temptation" (from the verb נסה, "to test"), and "Meribah" means "quarrel" or "contention" (from the verb ריב, "to argue"). The naming serves as a memorial of Israel's doubt and complaint against God. These names became important theological reference points in later Israelite tradition (cf. Ps 95:8-9; Deut 6:16).

**YHWH Nissi (יְהוָה נִסִּי)** – "The Lord is my banner" – is the altar Moses built after the victory. The word "nes" (נֵס) means "banner" or "standard" and was used as a rallying point in war. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, the banner was a symbol of the deity who protected the army. By giving the altar this name, Moses declared that Israel's victory belonged to the Lord.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological surveys in the Sinai region have documented several rocks and mountain formations with natural water sources that emerge when groundwater passes through porous rock layers. Although it is not possible to identify the specific rock from the story, geological studies show that the phenomenon of water coming from rocks occurs naturally in the area. This does not rule out the miraculous element but places the account in a credible geographical setting.

Egyptian texts from the New Kingdom (ca. 1550–1070 BCE) mention "Shasu" nomads in the Sinai region who occasionally came into conflict with both Egyptians and other peoples. Some scholars have suggested a possible connection between these groups and the Amalekites, although this remains speculative.
