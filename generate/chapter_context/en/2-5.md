# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 5

## People

**Pharaoh** appears in this chapter as the powerful ruler who rejects Moses' demands. His question, "Who is the LORD?" (v. 2), reflects the Egyptian worldview in which Pharaoh himself was regarded as divine—an incarnation of the god Horus and son of the sun god Ra. For an Egyptian king, it would have been unthinkable to obey an unknown foreign deity.

**Overseers and Scribes** (Hebrew: *nogsim* and *shoterim*) represent a hierarchical system. The *nogsim* were Egyptian drivers who had overall supervision, while the *shoterim* were Israelite foremen who served as intermediaries. This two-tier structure is confirmed by Egyptian sources describing similar organization of forced labor, in which local leaders were held responsible for work quotas.

## Events and Customs

**Brickmaking** is described in detail in this chapter. In Egypt, clay was mixed with straw (Hebrew: *teven*) to bind the material and prevent cracking during drying. The process involved trampling the clay with feet, shaping it in wooden molds, and sun-drying the bricks. Pharaoh's command to deny the Israelites straw while maintaining production quotas was a brutal punitive measure.

**Religious Festival in the Wilderness** – Moses requests that the people be allowed to travel three days into the wilderness to "hold a feast" (Hebrew: *chagag*) and "sacrifice" (Hebrew: *zabach*) to the LORD. This followed a common pattern in the ancient Near East where peoples traveled to holy places to perform sacrifices. A three-day journey would take them beyond Egyptian territory and control.

## Key Terms

**"God of the Hebrews"** (*Elohei ha-Ivrim*, v. 3) is a designation used when Israelites spoke to foreigners. The term "Hebrew" (*Ivri*) may be connected with *Habiru/Apiru*, a social class mentioned in Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts from the second millennium B.C.—often referring to stateless, wandering peoples or forced laborers.

**"Lazy/Sluggish"** (Hebrew: *nirpim*, vv. 8, 17) – Pharaoh's accusation that the Israelites are "lazy" uses a word meaning to be slack or weakened. This was common ruler rhetoric used to justify harsher working conditions. In Egyptian thought, labor for the state was a sacred duty, and resistance was viewed as rebellion against cosmic order (*maat*).

**"Quota/Fixed Amount"** (Hebrew: *matkonet*, v. 8) refers to the daily production requirement. Egyptian administrative texts document careful record-keeping of work performance and materials.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Tomb of Vizier Rekhmire** (ca. 1450 B.C.) in Thebes contains wall paintings showing foreign workers—including Syro-Palestinian peoples—producing bricks. The scene depicts the entire process: mixing clay, shaping bricks, and transporting them. Inscriptions at the site show drivers supervising workers with staffs, using language reminiscent of Exodus 5.

**Papyrus Anastasi III and IV** (from the 13th century B.C.) contain administrative reports mentioning brick production quotas and lack of straw as a problem. One text complains: "There is no one bringing straw, and there are no bricks." These documents confirm the historical realism of the chapter's description of Egyptian labor management and the specific challenges associated with brickmaking.
