# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 21

## People

**Manasseh** reigned for 55 years (c. 697–642 B.C.), the longest reign of any king in Judah. His name means "the one who causes to forget" and is the same as Joseph’s son. Assyrian sources confirm his existence: Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal both mention "Manasseh of Judah" (Akkadian: *Me-na-si-i*) as a vassal king who paid tribute and contributed materials to building projects. His mother Hephzibah ("my delight is in her") was likely from Jerusalem. Jewish tradition claims that the prophet Isaiah was martyred during Manasseh’s reign.

**Amon** reigned only two years (642–640 B.C.) before he was murdered by his own servants. His name is strikingly similar to the Egyptian god Amun, which may reflect the religious syncretism of the period. His mother Meshullemeth ("the recompensing one") came from Jotbah, probably a town in southern Judah.

## Places

**Gan-Uzza** ("Uzzah’s garden") was the burial place for both Manasseh and Amon, described as a garden near the king’s house. This departure from the royal tombs in the City of David may indicate that these kings were not considered worthy of traditional royal burial, or that new burial grounds had been established. Archaeologically, this area has not been identified with certainty, but it was likely located near the palace.

**The temple courts** where Manasseh built altars for the host of heaven (v. 5) refer to the two main courtyards that were part of the temple complex since Solomon’s time. Placing foreign altars there was a direct challenge to Yahwistic exclusivity.

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** ("he made his son pass through the fire", v. 6) refers to the Canaanite practice of *molek* offerings. Archaeological finds from *tophets* in Carthage and other Phoenician colonies have uncovered urns containing burned child remains, confirming the widespread nature of this practice in the Semitic world. Such rituals were performed in the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem.

**The host of heaven** (*tseva hashamayim*) denotes astral worship—the veneration of the sun, moon, stars, and planets—which was central to Assyrian and Babylonian religion. Under Assyrian hegemony, such worship was often imposed or voluntarily adopted by vassal states. Manasseh’s introduction of this practice likely reflects political-religious adaptation to his overlords.

**Divination practices** (v. 6)—the text mentions *onen* (augury), *nichesh* (omen interpretation), *ov* (familiar spirit), and *yideonim* (spirit mediums). These practices were widespread in Mesopotamian culture and were explicitly forbidden in Israelite law (Deut 18:10–12).

## Key Terms

**Toebot** (תּוֹעֵבוֹת, "abominations") is a strongly theologically charged term used for actions fundamentally incompatible with the covenant relationship with YHWH. In this chapter, it is especially linked to Canaanite and Assyrian religious practices.

**Gillulim** (גִּלּוּלִים, "dung-gods/idols") is a derogatory term for idols, probably derived from the root meaning "excrement" (*galal*). The word is consistently used to express contempt for foreign worship.

**Am-haarets** (עַם־הָאָרֶץ, "the people of the land", v. 24) here refers to the landowning elite in Judah who intervened politically to secure Davidic succession after Amon’s murder.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian source texts provide important context for this chapter. In Esarhaddon’s vassal treaty list (c. 676 B.C.), Manasseh is mentioned as one of twenty-two kings who contributed to building projects in Nineveh. Ashurbanipal’s annals refer to him as a tribute payer. These sources confirm that Judah was a loyal vassal state under Manasseh, which explains the introduction of Assyrian cultic forms. The archaeological material from Judah during this period also shows an increased occurrence of astral symbols and figurines associated with fertility worship.
