# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 28

## People

**Publius (Ποπλίῳ)** is referred to as "the chief man of the island" (τῷ πρώτῳ τῆς νήσου), a title confirmed by inscriptions found on Malta. Both Greek and Latin inscriptions from the island use exactly this title ("πρῶτος Μελιταίων" and "primus Melitensium"), demonstrating Luke's precision in his use of official titles. Publius likely served as the Roman representative or headman of the island under the province of Sicily.

**The Jewish leaders in Rome** represented a significant community. The Jewish community in Rome had existed since the second century B.C.E. and was substantial at Paul's time. Emperor Claudius had expelled the Jews from Rome around 49 C.E. (cf. Acts 18:2), but they returned after his death in 54 C.E. The fact that these leaders claimed to have received no official letters concerning Paul may indicate that the Jewish leadership in Jerusalem chose not to pursue the matter further after Paul's appeal to Caesar.

## Places

**Malta (Μελίτη)** lies approximately 100 kilometers south of Sicily and was under Roman rule at this time as part of the province of Sicily. The island had a population with Phoenician-Punic background, which explains the term "barbarians" (βάρβαροι) – a concept that was not derogatory but simply denoted people who did not speak Greek or Latin as their native language. Archaeological findings confirm the presence of both Punic and Greco-Roman culture on the island during this period.

**Syracuse (Συρακούσας)** on Sicily's east coast was one of antiquity's most famous cities and had been an important cultural center. **Rhegium (Ῥήγιον)**, today's Reggio Calabria, lay at the Italian "toe" by the Strait of Messina. **Puteoli (Ποτιόλους)**, today's Pozzuoli near the Bay of Naples, was Italy's most important port for grain imports from Egypt and trade connections with the East. It was a natural destination point for ships from Alexandria.

**Appius Forum (Ἀππίου Φόρου)** and **Tres Tabernae (Τριῶν Ταβερνῶν)** were stations along the Via Appia, Rome's main road southward. Appius Forum lay approximately 69 kilometers (43 Roman miles) from Rome, while Tres Tabernae ("Three Taverns") was about 53 kilometers (33 Roman miles) from the city. The fact that Christians from Rome met Paul at both places shows that the church in Rome was already well-established and organized.

## Events and Customs

**The snake incident and the local population's reaction** illustrate typical Mediterranean folk religion. The Greek text uses ἔχιδνα (poisonous viper/snake), and although there are no poisonous snakes on Malta today, climate changes or habitat shifts over two thousand years could explain this. The idea that "Dike" (δίκη) – the goddess of justice – would not let a murderer escape even after a shipwreck was widespread in Greco-Roman thought. The rapid reevaluation from viewing Paul as a murderer to calling him a god reflects the same fleeting folk theology seen in Lystra (Acts 14:11–18).

**Custodia libera** was the mildest form of Roman custody. That Paul was allowed to live "by himself" (καθʼ ἑαυτὸν) with only one guard indicates that the authorities did not consider him dangerous. The soldier was likely chained to Paul with a light chain (cf. ἅλυσιν in verse 20). This arrangement allowed Paul to receive visitors and continue his preaching, which was unusually favorable for a prisoner awaiting imperial hearing.

**Renting his own lodging (ἰδίῳ μισθώματι)** for two years required substantial funds. This was likely financed by support from the churches, perhaps especially from Philippi, which had previously sent gifts to Paul (Phil 4:15–18).

## Key Terms

**"Barbarians" (βάρβαροι)** is a Greek term that originally was onomatopoeic and imitated unintelligible speech ("bar-bar"). In this context, it does not denote uncivilized peoples but simply non-Greek speakers. The Maltese probably spoke a Punic dialect. Luke ironically emphasizes that these "barbarians" showed exemplary "humanity" (φιλανθρωπίαν).

**"The Dioscuri" (Διοσκούροις)** were the twin brothers Castor and Pollux from Greek mythology, sons of Zeus. They were particularly popular as protectors of sailors, and ships bearing their figureheads were common in Mediterranean trade. That an Alexandrian grain ship bore this mark was completely normal for the period.

**"The hope of Israel" (τῆς ἐλπίδος τοῦ Ἰσραήλ)** in verse 20 refers to the messianic hope, especially the resurrection from the dead – which Paul connects directly to Jesus' resurrection. This was the core of Paul's preaching, which he claimed was in continuity with, not in opposition to, Pharisaic Judaism.

**The Isaiah quotation (Isa 6:9–10)** in verses 26–27 is a central scripture passage that Paul uses to explain Israel's lack of response to the gospel. This quotation about hearing without understanding and seeing without perceiving is also used by Jesus in the Gospels (Matt 13:14–15; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10; John 12:40) and represents a recurring theme of hardened resistance to God's message.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Inscriptions from Malta confirming the title "the chief man" (πρῶτος) are among the most important archaeological confirmations of Luke's historical accuracy. A Greek inscription mentions "Prudens, knight of the Romans, the chief of the Maltese" (πρῶτος Μελιταίων), and a Latin inscription refers to "Lucius Castricius, knight of the Romans, the chief of all the Maltese" (primus Melitensium omnium).

Excavations in Rome have uncovered remains of the Jewish quarter in the Trastevere area and several synagogues, confirming the significant Jewish presence described by Luke. The catacombs in Rome also contain early Christian inscriptions testifying to the established church Paul encountered.
