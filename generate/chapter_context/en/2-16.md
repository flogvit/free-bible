# Historical and Cultural Context for Exodus 16

## Places

**The Wilderness of Sin** (מִדְבַּר־סִין, midbar-Sin) is the geographical setting for this chapter, located between Elim and Mount Sinai. This is not the same as the Wilderness of Sinai but a distinct area along the route through the Sinai Peninsula. The Hebrew name סִין (Sin) has no connection to the English word "sin"; it is simply a place name. The region is characterized by dry, rocky desert with limited water resources and vegetation—explaining the people’s desperation for food. Its exact location remains debated among scholars.

## Events and Customs

**Sabbath observance** is introduced here in practical form for the first time before the giving of the Law at Sinai. The people were to gather a double portion on the sixth day and rest on the seventh. This represents the first practical institutionalization of the Sabbath as a weekly day of rest. The concept of a fixed weekly day of rest was unique in the ancient world.

**Quails as food provision** (שְׂלָו, selav, v. 13) reflects a known phenomenon on the Sinai Peninsula, where migratory birds—especially quails—pass through the area in spring and autumn on their way between Europe and Africa. Often exhausted after crossing the Mediterranean Sea, these birds can be caught relatively easily. This natural occurrence became a means of God’s provision.

## Key Terms

**Manna** (מָן, man) is a central term in this chapter. Its name is explained in verse 15 from the question "What is it?" (מָן הוּא, man hu), where מָן may be an archaic form of the interrogative word "what". The description—white, like coriander seed, with a taste like honey cakes (צַפִּיחִת בִּדְבָשׁ)—has led scholars to compare it with natural phenomena such as secretions from tamarisk trees caused by scale insects. The best-known parallel is the secretion from tamarisk trees (Tamarix mannifera) that forms when scale insects pierce the bark. This substance falls at night, is white and sweet, and melts in the sun—features matching the biblical description. Bedouins in the area still call it "mann" and collect it as a sweetener. Nevertheless, this natural "manna" is produced only in small quantities during summer, which cannot account for the quantity, regularity, or Sabbath pattern of manna described in the text.

**Omer** (עֹמֶר) was a volume measure equivalent to about 2.2 liters (one-tenth of an ephah, as verse 36 specifies). This was the daily ration per person. The unit was well known in the ancient Near East, and standardization of measures was important in agricultural societies.

**Jar** (צִנְצֶנֶת, tsintsenet, v. 33) was to preserve manna as a testimony for future generations. This is a rare Hebrew word that appears only here in the Old Testament. The Septuagint translates it with στάμνος (stamnos), a type of jar. Hebrews 9:4 refers to it as a "golden jar" (χρυσῆ στάμνος).
