# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 14

## People

**Amaziah** (אֲמַצְיָהוּ, "Yahweh has strengthened") reigned in Judah from approximately 796–767 B.C. His mother Jehoaddan (יְהוֹעַדָּן) of Jerusalem indicates that he came from an established family in the capital. Amaziah's fate—being murdered after a conspiracy and fleeing to Lachish—follows a pattern of violent royal depositions during this period.

**Joash of Israel** (also called Jehoash) reigned from approximately 798–782 B.C. His victory over Amaziah marks a turning point where the Northern Kingdom temporarily dominated Judah. The parable he tells about the thistle and the cedar (v. 9) is a classic wisdom form in the ancient Near East, used to humble an arrogant opponent.

**Jeroboam II** reigned in Israel from approximately 782–753 B.C. and was one of the most powerful kings in the history of the Northern Kingdom. During his forty-one-year reign, Israel reached its greatest territorial extent since the time of David, characterized by great wealth and territorial expansion.

**Jonah son of Amittai** (v. 25) is mentioned here as a historical prophet from Gath-hepher in Galilee, who prophesied about the restoration of Israel's territory. This is the same prophet about whom the book of Jonah tells.

**Azariah** (עֲזַרְיָה), Amaziah's son, was made king by the people when he was sixteen years old (v. 21), after his father had been murdered in Lachish.

## Places

**The Valley of Salt** (גֵּיא־מֶלַח) where Amaziah defeated the Edomites is likely located south of the Dead Sea in the Arabah valley. The area was strategically important for controlling trade routes toward the Gulf of Aqaba.

**Sela** (הַסֶּלַע, "the rock"), which Amaziah captured and renamed Jokteel (יָקְתְאֵל), is traditionally identified with Petra in modern-day Jordan, or possibly with Umm el-Biyara, a fortress near Petra. The name literally means "rock" and describes the dramatic geography.

**Beth-shemesh** (בֵּית שֶׁמֶשׁ, "house of the sun"), where the battle between Judah and Israel took place, was located in the Shephelah region about 24 kilometers west of Jerusalem. The text specifies that this is "Beth-shemesh which belongs to Judah" (v. 11), to distinguish it from other places with the same name. Archaeological excavations have revealed a significant Iron Age city with fortifications.

**The Ephraim Gate** and **the Corner Gate** in Jerusalem's wall marked the northern city wall. The four hundred cubits (about 180 meters) that Joash tore down left the city vulnerable from the north—the direction from which enemies typically attacked.

**Eloth** on the Red Sea (Gulf of Aqaba) was important for trade with Arabia and East Africa. Azariah's rebuilding of the city (v. 22) shows Judah's desire to control this lucrative trade route.

## Events and Customs

**The principle of individual responsibility** (v. 6), where Amaziah does not execute the murderers' children, is remarkable. He quotes directly from Deuteronomy 24:16, showing that the Mosaic Law was regarded as binding authority. In contemporary cultures, collective punishment of entire families was common practice.

**"Let us look each other in the face"** (נִתְרָאֶה פָנִים, v. 8) is an idiomatic expression meaning to meet in battle. Amaziah's challenge to Joash after his victory over Edom suggests arrogant self-confidence.

**Hostages** (בְּנֵי הַתַּעֲרֻבוֹת, "sons of the pledge," v. 14) were a common practice in the ancient Near East to secure obedience from a subordinate party. Joash took sons of prominent Judeans as a guarantee of Judah's submission.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Jeroboam II's reign is partially confirmed through archaeological discoveries. A seal from Megiddo bears the inscription "Belonging to Shema, servant of Jeroboam," which likely refers to Jeroboam II. Furthermore, excavations in Samaria have uncovered ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from this period that testify to a well-organized administration and significant trade in wine and oil.

Assyrian sources from Adad-nirari III (approximately 810–783 B.C.) mention tribute payments from an Israelite king who can be identified with Joash, confirming that Israel was under Assyrian pressure during this period. This explains why Jeroboam II could expand: Assyria was temporarily weakened by internal strife.
