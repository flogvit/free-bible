# Historical and Cultural Context for Numbers 28

## Events and Customs

The chapter presents a systematic sacrificial calendar that structures Israel's worship around fixed times. The daily offering (*tamid*) of two lambs—one in the morning and one "between the two evenings" (בֵּין הָעַרְבָּיִם, *ben ha'arbayim*)—established a rhythm that became central to temple worship. This practice of morning and evening offerings was well known in surrounding cultures; Egyptian temples had similar daily rituals for their gods, and Mesopotamian temples also followed fixed sacrificial rhythms. The difference in Israel was the exclusive worship of one God and the understanding of sacrifice as covenant fellowship.

The new moon celebration (*rosh chodesh*) mentioned in verses 11–15 was important throughout the ancient Near East, where the lunar calendar governed religious and civil life. In Israel, the appearance of the new moon marked the beginning of each month, and this chapter shows that the celebration required significantly greater sacrifices than the Sabbath. Parallels exist in Babylonian celebrations at the new moon and full moon.

## Key Terms

The expression "my bread" (לַחְמִי, *lachmi*) in verse 2 is used metaphorically for the food offerings. This language was common in the ancient Near East, where sacrifices were understood as "the god's food." In Israelite theology, this imagery was preserved in worship, but with the awareness that God did not literally need nourishment (see Psalm 50:12–13).

The expression "drink offering of strong drink" (נֶסֶךְ שֵׁכָר, *nesech shechar*) in verse 7 is noteworthy. The word *shechar* usually refers to fermented drink, but here it is likely used poetically for wine. Drink offerings of wine were standard practice also in Canaanite, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian religion, where they were poured out at the foot of the altar.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological discoveries from the Arad sanctuary in the Negev (ca. 900–586 B.C.) have revealed an Israelite temple with two incense altars and traces of sacrificial activity. Ostraca (inscribed pottery shards) from the site mention deliveries of flour, oil, and wine—exactly the ingredients specified in this chapter for the grain offering and drink offering. This provides archaeological confirmation that the prescribed sacrificial components were indeed used in Israelite worship practice.
