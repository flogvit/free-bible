# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 44

## People

In this chapter, **Judah** emerges as the central spokesperson among the brothers. His long speech (verses 18–34) is the longest continuous speech in all of Genesis. This marks a significant development from chapter 37, where it was Judah who suggested selling Joseph. Now he offers himself as a slave in Benjamin’s place, demonstrating his transformation and willingness to make a self-sacrifice.

**The one in charge of Joseph’s house** (verses 1, 4) was a high-ranking servant with considerable authority. Egyptian texts confirm that such stewards held great power and could act on their master’s behalf in legal and economic matters.

## Events and Customs

The **divination cup** (גָּבִיעַ, *gavia*) mentioned in verses 2, 5, and 12 reflects a widespread practice in antiquity known as **lecanomancy**—divination using liquid in a cup. The method involved observing patterns of oil on water or reflections in the liquid. This practice is well documented in Mesopotamian and Egyptian sources. Whether Joseph actually practiced this or merely pretended to maintain his Egyptian role remains unclear in the text.

**Tearing one’s garments** (verse 13) was an established expression of grief and despair in the ancient Near East. This custom is also seen elsewhere in Genesis, such as when Jacob mourned for Joseph (37:34).

## Key Terms

The Hebrew expression **נַחֵשׁ יְנַחֵשׁ** (*nachesh yenachesh*, verses 5, 15) means "he divines" or "he practices augury." The root נחש is associated with seeking hidden knowledge through magical means. In an Egyptian context, such practices were accepted and widespread.

**עָרַב** (*arav*, verse 32) means "to stand as a guarantor" or "to guarantee." Judah’s guarantee for Benjamin represents a legally binding obligation well known in ancient Near Eastern law. The consequence of breaking such a guarantee was serious—in this case, Judah would "sin against his father all the days of his life."

The expression **שְׁאֹלָה** (*sheolah*, verses 29, 31) refers to the realm of the dead, Sheol. Jacob’s fear of going down to Sheol in grief expresses that losing Benjamin would lead to a death marked by inconsolable sorrow.
