# Historical and Cultural Context for Amos 6

## Places

Chapter 6 mentions several important cities that serve as points of comparison. **Calneh** (verse 2) was a significant city in northern Syria, possibly identical with Kullani. The city was likely conquered by Assyria in the eighth century B.C.E. **Hamath** was the capital of an Aramean kingdom north of Israel, a powerful city-state that also fell to Assyria. **Gath** was one of the five Philistine cities, severely weakened by the Aramean king Hazael around 815 B.C.E. (cf. 2 Kings 12:17) and later by Uzziah of Judah (2 Chronicles 26:6). Amos uses these cities rhetorically—they were all powerful, yet they fell nonetheless. This should warn Israel and Judah that their own fate was not secure.

**Zion** and **the mountain of Samaria** (verse 1) represent the capitals of the two kingdoms—Jerusalem and Samaria. The fact that both are mentioned together is unusual in Amos and shows that the "woe" cry (הוֹי) applies to all God's people, not just the northern kingdom. The boundary description "from Lebo-Hamath to the Arabah valley" (verse 14) marks the territory of the northern kingdom at its greatest extent, as it was under Jeroboam II (cf. 2 Kings 14:25). Ironically, this entire area is now threatened by oppression.

## Events and Customs

The chapter offers a rare glimpse into the lifestyle of the upper class in the eighth century B.C.E. The **ivory couch** (verse 4) testifies to extreme luxury. Archaeological finds from Samaria have uncovered hundreds of carved ivory plaques that decorated furniture and walls, imported from Phoenicia. **Marbled calves** were especially fat animals kept in stalls and force-fed—a delicacy reserved for the wealthiest.

**Mirzach** (מִרְזַח, *mirzeach*, verse 7) was a special banquet or festival institution known throughout the ancient Near East, including Ugaritic texts. It was a religious-social community that organized drinking parties, often associated with death cults and extravagant consumption. The fact that guests anointed themselves with "the finest oil" and drank wine from **mizraq** bowls (מִזְרָק, verse 6)—large offering vessels normally used in temple rituals—underscores both the extravagance and the blasphemous character of these feasts.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the ruin of Joseph"** (שֵׁבֶר יוֹסֵף, *shever Yosef*, verse 6) refers to the spiritual and social decay of the northern kingdom—Ephraim and Manasseh. The fact that the elite do not "grieve over" this suggests complete moral blindness. The verb **נֶחְלוּ** (*nechlu*) from the root חלה means to feel deep pain, become sick with sorrow, or feel compassion for a condition.

**Lo-debar** (לֹא דָבָר, 'nothing') and **Karnaim** (קַרְנָיִם, 'horns/power') in verse 13 are wordplays where Amos mocks Israel's military self-confidence. The text literally says they "rejoice over lo-debar" (nothing) and boast of having "taken Karnaim" (horns/power) by their own strength. At the same time, these names may have referred to actual places in Transjordan where Israel had won victories.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The extensive ivory finds from Samaria, excavated in the 1930s, confirm precisely the luxury Amos describes. Over 500 ivory plaques with Egyptian and Phoenician motifs were discovered, dated to the ninth-eighth centuries B.C.E. These decorated furniture and wall panels in the homes of the aristocracy—exactly the "ivory couches" the prophet condemns. Along with finds of imported goods and fine stone houses on the acropolis at Samaria, this provides an archaeological picture of the extreme social inequality Amos attacks.
