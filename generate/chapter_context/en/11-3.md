# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 3

## People

**Pharaoh's daughter** is mentioned as Solomon’s wife in the opening of the chapter (v. 1). This marriage represents a remarkable diplomatic alliance. In Egyptian history, it was highly unusual for Pharaoh’s daughters to be married off to foreign rulers—Egypt considered itself the dominant power. The fact that Solomon achieved such a marriage indicates either Israel’s significant status at this time or a period of Egyptian weakness (likely during the 21st dynasty). She was temporarily housed in the City of David until Solomon’s own house, the temple of the Lord, and the walls of Jerusalem were completed.

## Places

**Gibeon** (גִּבְעוֹן) was located at modern-day el-Jib, about 10 km northwest of Jerusalem. Archaeological excavations have confirmed the city’s importance during this period. The site is described as "the great high place" (הַבָּמָה הַגְּדוֹלָה), indicating that it was the most important cultic center before the temple was built. According to 2 Chronicles 1:3–6, the tent of meeting and the bronze altar made by Bezalel were located in Gibeon. This explains why Solomon chose this place for his great sacrifice of a thousand burnt offerings.

## Events and Customs

**High places (בָּמוֹת)** were local shrines, typically situated on hills or elevated ground. Before the temple was built, worship at such sites was an accepted practice in Israel, although it was later condemned. The text almost excuses this practice by explaining that "no house had yet been built for the name of the Lord" (v. 2).

**Divine dream revelation** was a well-known practice in the ancient Near East. Royal "incubation dreams"—where a ruler slept at a holy place to receive divine guidance—are documented in both Egyptian and Mesopotamian sources. Solomon’s experience at Gibeon follows this pattern, but the text emphasizes that the initiative came from God Himself.

## Key Terms

**"A listening heart" (לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ)** in verse 9 is a central concept. In Hebrew, the "heart" is the seat of intellect and will, not merely emotions. The verb שָׁמַע (shama) means both "to hear" and "to obey". Solomon is therefore asking for a heart that both understands and acts rightly—a receptive and obedient mind capable of distinguishing between good and evil in judging situations.

**"To go out and come in" (צֵאת וָבֹא)** in verse 7 is an idiom describing daily leadership and administration, especially in military and royal contexts. When Solomon calls himself a "young boy" who does not know how to "go out and come in", he expresses humility regarding the task of leading the people.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The story of the two women and the child (vv. 16–28) has parallels with narratives from several other cultures. This so-called "Solomon’s judgment" motif has become an archetypal example of wisdom in world literature. The fact that the women are described as זֹנוֹת (prostitutes) underscores that Solomon’s justice applied to all members of society, regardless of social status. The king’s role as supreme judge was central in the ancient Near East, and this episode demonstrates that God had fulfilled His promise of wisdom.
