# Historical and Cultural Context for Revelation 20

## Places

**The Abyss (ἡ ἄβυσσος, hē abyssos)** refers in Jewish cosmology to an underground prison for evil spiritual powers. In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word *tehom* (the deep) is translated as *abyssos* in the Septuagint. In intertestamental literature, especially 1 Enoch, the abyss is described as the place where fallen angels are held captive until judgment. In this chapter, the abyss serves as Satan’s prison during the thousand-year period (vv. 1–3).

**The beloved city (τὴν πόλιν τὴν ἠγαπημένην)** in verse 9 is a reference to Jerusalem. This designation has roots in prophetic tradition where Jerusalem is called God’s beloved city (Ps 78:68; 87:2). For the first Christians, this represented the gathering place of God’s people, whether the historical Jerusalem or the eschatological people of God.

**The lake of fire and sulfur (ἡ λίμνη τοῦ πυρὸς καὶ θείου)** is mentioned three times in the chapter (vv. 10, 14, 15) as the final place of punishment. In verse 14 it is explicitly identified with "the second death." The image combines elements from the Gehenna tradition (the Hinnom Valley with its burning refuse) and prophetic descriptions of judgment by fire and sulfur (Isa 30:33; Ezek 38:22).

## Events and Customs

**Those beheaded (τῶν πεπελεκισμένων)** in verse 4 refers to a specific method of execution. The Greek verb *pelekizō* means to behead with an axe. This was a Roman form of execution reserved for Roman citizens, while non-citizens were crucified. The reference indicates that the martyrs included Jews, Romans, and other nations who had refused emperor worship. Execution by beheading was regarded as more honorable than crucifixion.

**Thrones and judgment (θρόνους... καὶ κρίμα ἐδόθη αὐτοῖς)** reflect both Roman legal culture and Jewish ideas of a heavenly council. In Daniel 7:9–10, thrones are set before God’s court. The fact that believers will judge together with Christ fulfills the promise from Daniel 7:22 that judgment will be given to "the holy ones of the Most High."

**The great white throne (θρόνον μέγαν λευκὸν)** in verse 11 represents the final court of judgment, distinct from the thrones in verse 4. The color white symbolizes purity and righteousness. The image of earth and heaven fleeing from God’s presence (v. 11) underscores the absolute authority and holiness of this judgment.

## Key Terms

**Gog and Magog (τὸν Γὼγ καὶ Μαγώγ)** are drawn from Ezekiel 38–39, where Gog is a prince from the land of Magog who leads an end-time attack against Israel. In Jewish tradition, these names became symbols for all hostile nations that would attack God’s people in the last days. While Ezekiel describes Gog as a person from Magog, John treats both terms as designations for peoples from "the four corners of the earth"—that is, a universal coalition against God.

**The thousand years (χίλια ἔτη)**—repeated six times in the chapter—has its background in Jewish apocalyptic thought. In 2 Enoch and later rabbinic tradition we find the idea of a temporary messianic age before the eternal state. The number one thousand symbolizes completeness and wholeness (10³), representing a long but limited period.

**The first resurrection (ἡ ἀνάστασις ἡ πρώτη)** in verses 5–6 describes the resurrection of the martyrs who reign with Christ during the thousand years. The term suggests a two-stage resurrection: first, the faithful to life and rule, then "the rest of the dead" (v. 5) for judgment. Those who share in the first resurrection are protected from "the second death."

**The second death (ὁ δεύτερος θάνατος)** is an expression found in Jewish targums (Aramaic Bible translations), where it describes the final punishment of the wicked. In the Targum to Isaiah 65:6 and elsewhere, "the second death" refers to eternal destruction or punishment, distinct from physical death. In verse 14, the second death is explicitly identified with the lake of fire.

**The book of life (βίβλος τῆς ζωῆς)** has parallels in several biblical texts. In the Old Testament, God’s book is mentioned where the names of the living are written (Exod 32:32–33; Ps 69:29; Dan 12:1). In verse 12, other books are also opened containing people’s deeds—the basis for judgment. Not being written in the book of life results in being thrown into the lake of fire (v. 15).
