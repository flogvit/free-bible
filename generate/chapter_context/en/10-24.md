# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Samuel 24

## People

**Gad the prophet** (חֹזֵה דָוִד, "David's seer") is one of David's court prophets. He is called both נָבִיא (navi, "prophet") and חֹזֶה (choze, "seer"), indicating his role as a spiritual advisor with direct access to the king. In this chapter, he serves as God's spokesperson to David, delivering both judgment and the solution.

**Araunah the Jebusite** (אֲרַוְנָה הַיְבֻסִי) was a prominent Jebusite who still lived in Jerusalem after David's conquest. The fact that he owned a threshing floor on the ridge north of the City of David suggests he was a wealthy man. His name has been interpreted as non-Semitic, possibly Hurrian. In verse 23, the phrasing "Araunah gave to the king" (נָתַן אֲרַוְנָה הַמֶּלֶךְ לַמֶּלֶךְ) may imply that he had been a local Jebusite ruler, or it could be an honorific title.

## Places

**Araunah's threshing floor** was located on Mount Moriah, north of the City of David. This site was later purchased by David and, according to Jewish tradition (2 Chron 3:1), became the place where Solomon built the temple. Threshing floors were typically situated on open, windy heights to facilitate the cleaning of grain. The fact that the angel stopped precisely here gave the location a unique sacred significance, preparing it to become Israel's central place of worship.

**The route of the census** (verses 5–7) describes a systematic journey covering the entire kingdom: from Aroer by the Arnon Gorge in the east, through Gilead and northward to Dan and the Sidon region, then south along the coast to Beersheba in the Negev. The expression "from Dan to Beersheba" was the standard formula for all of Israel's territory, from its northernmost to its southernmost border.

## Events and Customs

**The census and its problems** was a sensitive issue in ancient Israel. While counting could be necessary for military mobilization or taxation, it was often viewed as a sign that the king trusted in military strength rather than in God. In Exodus 30:12, it is required that each person counted must give a ransom "so that no plague may come upon them." Joab's resistance (verse 3) reflects this concern—he saw the census as unnecessary and potentially dangerous.

**The choice among three punishments** (verses 13–14)—famine, war, or pestilence—represents the three classic forms of divine judgment in the ancient Near East. David's choice of pestilence over war ("to fall into human hands") shows his trust in God's mercy rather than in the mercilessness of enemies.

**The course of the plague** (verses 15–16): The plague was stopped before the three days were completed. The text says it lasted "from morning until the appointed time" (מֵהַבֹּקֶר וְעַד־עֵת מוֹעֵד), when God relented and commanded the angel to stop at Araunah's threshing floor.

## Key Terms

The expression **וַיָּסֶת** (vayyaset, "he incited") in verse 1 creates a theological tension. In the parallel passage of 1 Chronicles 21:1, it is Satan who incites David. This difference reflects the development in Israelite theology regarding the origin of evil—the older text in Samuel attributes everything to God, while the later books of Chronicles introduce an intermediary figure.

**נִסְכַּלְתִּי** (niskkalti, "I have acted foolishly") in verse 10 is a strong expression of repentance. The root ס.כ.ל refers to foolish, unwise action—David acknowledges not only sin but also a lack of wisdom in his decision.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The place David purchased is traditionally identified with the Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif) in Jerusalem, based on 2 Chronicles 3:1. The price David paid—50 shekels of silver—stands in contrast to 1 Chronicles 21:25, which mentions 600 shekels of gold. This difference may be explained by the possibility that Chronicles includes a larger area around the threshing floor itself, or it may reflect different traditions.
