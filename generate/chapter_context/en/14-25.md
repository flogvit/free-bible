# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 25

## People

**Amaziah** (אֲמַצְיָהוּ, "The Lord is strong") was the ninth king of Judah after the kingdom split and reigned approximately 796–767 B.C. His mother Jehoaddan (יְהוֹעַדָּן) was from Jerusalem, which underscores his legitimate connection to the capital. The text gives him a mixed evaluation: he did what was right, but "not with a whole heart" (v. 2)—a characteristic that explains his later apostasy into idolatry.

**Joash of Israel** (יוֹאָשׁ), son of Jehoahaz and grandson of Jehu, reigned in Samaria approximately 798–782 B.C. His fable about the thornbush and the cedar tree (vv. 18–19) shows literary sophistication and serves as a sharp political warning to the overconfident Amaziah.

## Places

**Valley of Salt** (גֵּיא הַמֶּלַח) was the battlefield against the Edomites. This is likely Wadi el-Milh south of the Dead Sea, an area with salt deposits where several historical battles took place. David had previously defeated the Edomites at this same location (2 Sam 8:13).

**Sela** (הַסֶּלַע, "the rock") was an Edomite fortress, probably identical to later Petra in present-day Jordan. The ten thousand captives were thrown down from this cliff (v. 12)—a brutal execution method known from antiquity.

**Beth-shemesh** (בֵּית שֶׁמֶשׁ, "house of the sun") was located in the Shephelah region, about 24 km west of Jerusalem. The city was a strategically important border town between Judah and Philistine territory. Archaeological excavations at the site (Tell er-Rumeileh) have uncovered settlement layers from this period.

**Lachish** (לָכִישׁ), where Amaziah fled and was killed, was Judah's second most important fortified city. Tel Lachish has yielded rich archaeological finds confirming the city's significance in the 700s B.C.

## Events and Customs

The chapter cites **Deuteronomy 24:16** regarding individual responsibility for punishment—"fathers shall not be put to death for their children" (v. 4). This principle was remarkable in a culture where collective punishment was common. Amaziah's adherence to this law shows respect for the Mosaic Law, even though he later broke the covenant through idolatry.

**Mercenary soldiers** were common practice in the ancient Near East. Amaziah paid 100 talents of silver (about 3.4 tons) for 100,000 Israelite soldiers—a substantial sum equivalent to roughly one-third of a talent per man. The man of God's warning against this alliance reflects the recurring theme in Chronicles that Judah should not seek military support from the apostate Northern Kingdom.

## Key Terms

The expression **"not with a whole heart"** (לֹא בְּלֵבָב שָׁלֵם) is theologically central in Chronicles. The Hebrew שָׁלֵם ("whole, complete") describes undivided loyalty to the Lord. Amaziah's divided loyalty led to his downfall.

**"Let us meet face to face"** (נִתְרָאֶה פָנִים, v. 17) was a formal challenge to battle in antiquity, a diplomatic way of declaring hostilities.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The plundering of Jerusalem, where 400 cubits (about 180 meters) of the city wall were torn down between the Ephraim Gate and the Corner Gate (v. 23), represents a serious humiliation. Archaeologically, remains of Jerusalem's walls from this period have been identified, although these specific gates have not been securely located. Assyrian reliefs and texts from the same era document similar practices where victors systematically destroyed parts of city walls to demonstrate the vulnerability of the defeated city.
