# Historical and Cultural Context for John 5

## Places

### The Pool of Bethesda
The chapter mentions a pool called Βηθεσδά (Bethesda) with five colonnades, located near the Sheep Gate (ἐπὶ τῇ προβατικῇ) in Jerusalem. The Sheep Gate was an entrance in the city wall on the north side of the temple area, mentioned already in Nehemiah 3:1. The name "Bethesda" likely comes from the Aramaic בֵּית חֶסְדָּא (beit ḥesda), meaning "house of mercy" or "house of grace". Other manuscripts have the variants "Betsaida" or "Betsata".

Archaeological excavations at St. Anne's Church in the Muslim Quarter of Jerusalem have uncovered two large pools with five colonnades—four around the edges and one dividing the two pools. This confirms the unusual architectural description in the text. The pool was originally built in the 700s BC as a water reservoir, but during Hellenistic-Roman times it became associated with healing. Finds of votive offerings suggest that the site functioned as a healing center, possibly linked to the Greek god Asclepius or similar local traditions.

## Events and Customs

### The Feast Mentioned
The text begins with "ἑορτὴ τῶν Ἰουδαίων" (a Jewish feast), without specifying which one. Some manuscripts include the definite article ("the Jewish feast"), which would point to Passover. Other suggestions include the Festival of Tabernacles, the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost), or Purim. Regardless of which feast it was, this explains why Jesus went up to Jerusalem, since Jewish men were required to attend the three major pilgrimage festivals.

### Sabbath Rules
The healed man is criticized for carrying his bed (κράβαττος) on the Sabbath. According to Jeremiah 17:21-22 and Nehemiah 13:19, it was forbidden to carry burdens on the Sabbath. The later Mishnah (tractate Shabbat 7:2) lists 39 main categories of prohibited work, including "carrying from one domain to another". Jesus' healing on the Sabbath and his command to carry the bed directly challenged the religious leaders' interpretation of the Sabbath law.

### Belief in the Movement of the Water
Verse 4 (which is absent in the earliest manuscripts) describes the belief that an angel stirred the water and that the first person to enter it after the stirring was healed. This belief reflects widespread ancient views about healing waters and divine intervention at certain springs and pools. Similar traditions existed at Asclepius sanctuaries in the Greco-Roman world.

## Key Terms

### "My Father is working" (ὁ πατήρ μου ἐργάζεται)
Jesus' statement in verse 17 touches on an ongoing rabbinic debate about God's relationship to the Sabbath. The rabbis acknowledged that God continues to sustain the world even on the Sabbath—he gives life and judges even on the day of rest. Jesus' claim that "my Father is working until now, and I am working" places himself in the same category as God, which his opponents immediately understood as a claim to equality with God (verse 18).

### "Son of Man" (υἱὸς ἀνθρώπου)
In verse 27, the title "Son of Man" is used without the definite article in connection with judicial authority. This alludes to Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" receives eternal dominion and kingly power. In Jewish apocalyptic tradition (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra), this figure develops into a heavenly judge. Jesus applies this to himself as the one who has been given authority to judge.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The excavations at the Pool of Bethesda, especially the work carried out by the French Biblical School (École Biblique) from 1914 onward, have been of great significance for understanding this chapter. Not only were the two pools with colonnades discovered, but also a later-built Asclepius temple from the 100s AD, indicating that the site's reputation as a healing place continued into Roman times. Votive offerings in the form of feet and other body parts testify that sick people sought healing here. These findings confirm that the topographical details in the Gospel of John are historically reliable and that the site was already known in Jesus' time as a place where people sought miraculous healing.
