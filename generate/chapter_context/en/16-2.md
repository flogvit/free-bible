# Historical and Cultural Context for Nehemiah 2

## People

**Artaxerxes I** (Persian *Artaxšaça*) was the Persian Great King who reigned from 465–424 B.C. His twentieth regnal year (v. 1) corresponds to 445/444 B.C. Artaxerxes is known from Persian inscriptions and Greek historians as a powerful yet relatively tolerant ruler toward his subjects. The fact that Nehemiah could obtain royal favor for such a project aligns with what we know of Persian religious policy, which permitted local cults and traditions.

**Sanballat the Horonite** is mentioned in verses 10 and 19. His existence is confirmed by the Elephantine papyri from Egypt (ca. 407 B.C.), where he is called "Sinuballit, governor of Samaria." The title "Horonite" may refer to Beth-Horon in Ephraim or to the god Horon. **Tobiah** is called "the Ammonite servant," indicating that he was a Persian official of Ammonite background. **Geshem the Arab** (v. 19) was likely the leader of an Arabian tribal confederation; inscriptions from Dedan in Arabia mention a "Geshem/Gasmu" from this period.

## Places

Nehemiah’s nighttime inspection (vv. 13–15) provides valuable topographical information about Jerusalem. **The Valley Gate** (שַׁעַר הַגַּיא) was probably located on the west side of the city and led out toward the Hinnom Valley. **The Dragon Spring** (עֵין הַתַּנִּין, possibly "Jackal Spring") and **the Dung Gate** (שַׁעַר הָאַשְׁפֹּת) mark the southern part of the city. **The Fountain Gate** (שַׁעַר הָעַיִן) and **the King’s Pool** (בְּרֵכַת הַמֶּלֶךְ) were situated near the Pool of Siloam in the southeast. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of city walls from this period, as well as water systems that correspond with these descriptions.

**The Royal Park** (פַּרְדֵּס, *pardes*) in verse 8 refers to a royal forest. The word is a loanword from Persian (*pairidaeza*) and is the origin of our word "paradise." Asaph was the keeper of this royal park, which was probably located in the Lebanon region or near Jerusalem.

## Events and Customs

**The Cupbearer’s Office** was one of the most trusted positions at the Persian court. The cupbearer served the king’s wine and thus had daily, intimate access to the ruler. The fact that Nehemiah "had not looked sad" in the king’s presence before (v. 1) reflects strict court etiquette: showing sorrow or negative emotions in the king’s presence could be perceived as an insult or a threat. Nehemiah’s fear (v. 2) was well-founded—looking sad could be interpreted as dissatisfaction with the king.

**The Queen/Concubine** (שֵׁגַל, *shegal*, v. 6) is mentioned as present at this private audience. This Hebrew word is rarely used and may indicate either the chief wife or a favored concubine. Her presence suggests an informal setting and possibly that she supported Nehemiah’s cause.

## Key Terms

The expression **"God of heaven"** (אֱלֹהֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם, vv. 4, 20) was a common Jewish designation for Yahweh during the Persian period, especially in communication with non-Jews. This title also appears in Persian administrative documents from the period, including Cyrus’s edict.

**"The graves of my fathers"** (קִבְרוֹת אֲבֹתַי, vv. 3, 5) was a culturally charged argument. In Persian and general Near Eastern culture, honoring the burial places of one’s ancestors was a sacred duty. By emphasizing this aspect rather than the temple or God’s law, Nehemiah appealed to values that Persians shared and respected.
