# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalm 109

## Key Terms

The chapter contains several Hebrew key words that require cultural understanding. The word **שָׂטָן** (*satan*, v. 6) here means "accuser" or "adversary"—not the devil in a later theological sense, but a legal opponent who stands at the right hand of the accused in court. The expression **יַעֲמֹד עַל־יְמִינֽוֹ** ("stand at his right hand") refers to the legal practice where the accuser stood in this position. The same root is used in verse 4 (**יִשְׂטְנ֗וּנִי**, "they accuse me"), which underscores the legal framework.

The term **פְּקֻדָּה** (*pequddah*, v. 8) is often translated as "office" or "service" and is cited in Acts 1:20 regarding Judas's position. In an Old Testament context, it refers to a trusted role or position of responsibility.

## Events and Customs

The extensive curses (vv. 6–19) reflect the culture of Old Testament legal proceedings. When the psalmist prays that the enemy's prayer should "become sin" (v. 7), this reflects the belief that a guilty person's prayer is not only ineffective but actually worsens their situation. The curses mentioning a widow, fatherless children, and creditors (vv. 9–11) strike at the most vulnerable points in an Israelite family's existence—without a male head of household, the family lacked legal protection.

The images of the curse as clothing and a belt (vv. 18–19) use metaphors from daily life. A belt (**מֵזַח**) was an essential garment that held the robe in place during work—the curse should thus become as inseparable from the enemy as his most necessary clothes.
