# Chapter Context for Job 5

## Key Concepts

### "The Fool" (אֱוִיל, *evil*) and "the Simple One" (פֹתֶה, *poteh*)
In verses 2–3, Eliphaz uses two Hebrew terms for unreasonable people. *Evil* does not refer to someone intellectually weak, but rather one who morally and spiritually rejects wisdom—someone who lives as if God does not exist. *Poteh* describes a person who is easily led astray. In wisdom literature, these were established categories standing in contrast to "the wise" (*hakham*).

### "Bene-reshef" (בְנֵי־רֶשֶׁף) – "Sons of the Flame"
In verse 7, the expression "bene reshef", often translated as "sparks", is used. *Reshef* was the name of a Canaanite plague deity, and in Hebrew the word also referred to fire, pestilence, or arrows. The phrase "sons of Reshef" could mean sparks flying upward, birds, or metaphorically calamities that inevitably strike people. The context with the verb "fly high" (יַגְבִּיהוּ עוּף) supports the interpretation of something rising upward. This duality reflects Job’s cultural setting where Canaanite and Israelite imagery overlapped.

### "At the Gate" (בַשַּׁעַר, *basha'ar*)
Verse 4 mentions that the fool’s children are "crushed at the gate". The city gate was the place for legal proceedings and judicial decisions in ancient Israel. The elders of the city sat there to judge disputes. To be "crushed at the gate" meant losing lawsuits, losing one’s property through legal processes, and having no one to defend one’s cause.

## Events and Customs

### Covenant with the Stones of the Field (verse 23)
The idea of a "covenant with the stones of the field" reflects the concerns of an agricultural society. Stony ground was the farmer’s enemy—stones destroyed plows and hindered crops. The image of a "covenant" with the stones expresses a condition where even natural obstacles to prosperity have been removed. Parallel texts from Mesopotamia show similar ideas that divine blessing could make nature cooperative.

### The Number Sequence "Six... Seven" (verse 19)
The formula "in six troubles... in seven" is a well-known literary device in Semitic wisdom literature (cf. Proverbs 6:16; 30:15). This "x, x+1" formula indicates completeness—not exactly six or seven, but "no matter how many". The same technique appears in Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra.
