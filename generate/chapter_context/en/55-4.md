# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Timothy 4

## People

This chapter mentions a remarkable number of individuals, offering insight into Paul's network toward the end of his life. **Demas** (v. 10) is also mentioned in Colossians 4:14 and Philemon 24 as Paul's fellow worker, but here he has deserted the apostle. **Crescens** is known only from this verse. **Titus** was one of Paul's most trusted coworkers, known from Galatians and the letter bearing his name. **Luke**, the physician and Gospel writer, remained faithful to Paul. **Mark** (John Mark) had previously caused a split between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37-39), but here he is described as "useful."

**Tychicus** (v. 12) was a coworker from the province of Asia who is also mentioned in Acts 20:4, Ephesians 6:21, and Colossians 4:7. **Carpus** (v. 13) is otherwise unknown. **Alexander the coppersmith** (v. 14) may possibly be the same person mentioned in 1 Timothy 1:20. **Priscilla and Aquila** (v. 19) were the well-known couple who hosted a house church and worked with Paul in Corinth and Ephesus. **Onesiphorus** is also mentioned in 2 Timothy 1:16-18. **Erastus** (v. 20) may be identical to "the city treasurer" (οἰκονόμος τῆς πόλεως) mentioned in Romans 16:23 – an inscription found in Corinth mentions an Erastus who funded street work. **Linus** (v. 21) is identified in early Christian tradition as Peter's successor and the first bishop of Rome after the apostles.

## Places

The chapter paints a geographical picture of the spread of the early church. **Thessalonica** (v. 10), the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, was an important hub along the Via Egnatia. **Galatia** may refer either to the ethnic region in central Asia Minor or to the larger Roman province. **Dalmatia** (v. 10), present-day Croatia (and parts of Bosnia-Herzegovina and Montenegro), was part of the province of Illyricum along the Adriatic coast – this shows that the gospel had spread to the Balkans.

**Troas** (v. 13) was an important port city in northwest Asia Minor, near ancient Troy, and served as a link between Asia and Europe. **Corinth** (v. 20) was the capital of the province of Achaia and a significant commercial center. **Miletus** (v. 20), an old port city south of Ephesus, was the place where Paul had previously delivered his farewell address to the elders of the Ephesian church (Acts 20:17-38).

## Events and Customs

The expression "I am already being poured out as a drink offering" (σπένδομαι, v. 6) draws imagery from Greco-Roman and Jewish sacrificial practices, where wine was poured over a sacrifice to conclude the ritual. Paul uses this as a metaphor for his impending death – his life completes an offering already given in service.

The reference to "my first defense" (ἀπολογία, v. 16) reflects the Roman legal system. A *prima actio* (first hearing) was a preliminary court proceeding where charges were presented and the accused could defend themselves. That "no one stood with" Paul may mean he lacked an *advocatus* (defense attorney) or character witnesses, which were crucial in Roman trials. The phrase "delivered from the lion's mouth" (v. 17) is likely metaphorical for a death sentence or may allude to Emperor Nero, who was occasionally compared to a lion in literature.

## Key Terms

The word **φαιλόνης** (*failonēs*, v. 13), here translated "cloak," was a heavy, circular outer garment with a hole for the head, used as protection against cold and rain – practical for Paul in a cold prison. **Βιβλία** (*biblia*, "books") likely refers to papyrus scrolls, possibly Old Testament texts. **Μεμβράναι** (*membranai*, "parchments") is a Latin loanword denoting animal skins used as writing material – more durable and costly than papyrus. These may have been personal notes, copies of Paul's own letters, or early Christian writings.

The expression **στέφανος τῆς δικαιοσύνης** ("the crown of righteousness," v. 8) draws imagery from the Greek athletic games. The victory wreath (*stephanos*) was a crown of leaves – not gold – awarded to winners at Olympia, Isthmia, and other games. For Paul, it represents the eschatological reward from "the righteous Judge."

## Chapter-Specific Findings

An inscription found at the theater in Corinth from the middle of the first century mentions an "Erastus" who funded the paving of a square in exchange for his office as *aedile* (city official). Although identification with Paul's coworker is not certain, the find offers a fascinating glimpse that Christians could hold high social status and administrative positions in Roman cities at this time.
