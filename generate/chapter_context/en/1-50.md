# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 50

## People

**Jacob (Israel)** concludes his role as patriarch in this chapter, and his burial marks the transition to a new era. His wish to be buried in Canaan (verses 5, 13) reflects the deep significance of the Promised Land and the family tomb. **Joseph** emerges as the dominant figure who unites the roles of Egyptian nobleman and Hebrew son. His age at death, 110 years (verses 22, 26), was considered the ideal lifespan in Egyptian culture—a sign of divine blessing and a full life. **Machir**, Manasseh's son, is specifically mentioned (verse 23). That his children were "born on Joseph's knees" (עַל־בִּרְכֵּי יוֹסֵף) indicates a formal adoption or recognition, and Machir's lineage later becomes significant in the conquest of Canaan.

## Places

**The Cave of Machpelah** near Mamre (verse 13) was the family tomb that Abraham had purchased from the Hittite Ephron (Genesis 23). This place, near present-day Hebron, was the only piece of land the patriarchs owned in Canaan and represented a tangible guarantee of the promise of the land. **Goren ha-Atad** (the threshing floor of thorns, verses 10–11) is located "beyond the Jordan" and was renamed **Abel-Mizraim** ("Mourning of Egypt"). The precise location is debated, but the text suggests that the funeral procession route went eastward around the Dead Sea.

## Events and Customs

**Embalming** (חֲנֻטִים/*khanuttim*, verses 2–3) was a typical Egyptian practice normally not performed by Israelites. That Joseph had Egyptian physicians embalm Jacob shows his integration into Egyptian culture. The process took 40 days (verse 3), which aligns reasonably well with Egyptian sources, although these vary in their descriptions of the duration of embalming. **The mourning period** of 70 days (verse 3) for Jacob was remarkably long and demonstrates the great honor shown to him in Egypt. In contrast, the brothers' **seven-day mourning period** at Goren ha-Atad (verse 10) reflects Semitic tradition, which later became standard Jewish practice (*shiva*).

The **funeral procession** (verses 7–9), with chariots, horsemen, and high-ranking Egyptian officials, was a state funeral of the highest rank, underscoring Joseph's position and Jacob's honor in Egypt.

## Key Terms

The verb **פָּקַד** (*paqad*, verses 24–25), translated "visit" or "care for," is a key word expressing God's faithful intervention to fulfill His promises. Joseph uses the intensified form *paqod yifqod* (פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד, "God will surely visit you"), which becomes a programmatic phrase repeated when Moses is later called (Exodus 3:16). The expression **"Am I in God's place?"** (הֲתַחַת אֱלֹהִים אָנִי, verse 19) shows Joseph's humility and his refusal to take on the role of judge over his brothers.

The word **אָרוֹן** (*aron*, verse 26), usually translated "coffin," is the same word later used for the ark of the covenant. Joseph's bones in a coffin in Egypt become a symbol of the expectation of deliverance.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian texts and archaeological findings confirm the practice of embalming performed by physicians (*sunu*), as well as mourning periods of varying lengths based on social status. The size and composition of the funeral procession, with chariots and officials, correspond to known Egyptian burial customs for high-ranking individuals. The interest in returning remains to one's homeland is also documented in Egyptian sources, where foreigners serving in Egypt expressed a desire to be buried in their native land.
