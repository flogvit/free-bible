# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 23

## Events and Customs

Leviticus 23 provides a comprehensive calendar of Israel’s annual festivals (מוֹעֲדִים, *moʿadim*). The chapter describes the Sabbath (v. 3) and seven annual feasts: Passover (פֶּסַח, *pesach*), the Feast of Unleavened Bread (חַג הַמַּצּוֹת, *chag hamatzot*), the Firstfruits offering (עֹמֶר, *omer*), the Feast of Weeks (שָׁבֻעוֹת, *shavuot*), the Feast of Trumpets (יוֹם תְּרוּעָה, *yom teruʿah*), the Day of Atonement (יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים, *yom hakippurim*), and the Feast of Tabernacles (חַג הַסֻּכּוֹת, *chag hasukkot*). These festivals structured religious life around the agricultural year and Israel’s history of salvation.

In surrounding cultures in Mesopotamia and Egypt, there were also religious calendars tied to the agricultural cycle. The Babylonians had the Akitu festival at New Year, and the Egyptians held harvest festivals. What distinguishes the Israelite calendar is its strong connection to historical events—especially the Exodus from Egypt—rather than merely natural cycles. The Feast of Tabernacles (vv. 42–43) is explicitly explained as a reminder of the wilderness wanderings, giving the festival an educational and historical dimension that was unique in the ancient world.

## Key Terms

The expression **מִקְרָא־קֹדֶשׁ** (*miqra qodesh*, "a holy convocation") is used repeatedly throughout the chapter and designates days when the people were to gather for worship and refrain from work. The word מוֹעֵד (*moʿed*) means "appointed time" or "festival" and emphasizes that these feasts were divinely ordained—not chosen by humans.

The expression **וְעִנִּיתֶם אֶת־נַפְשׁוֹתֵיכֶם** (*veʿinnitem et-nafshoteikhem*, "you shall afflict/humble yourselves", vv. 27, 32) is linked to the Day of Atonement and was understood as fasting and self-denial. The expression **שַׁבַּת שַׁבָּתוֹן** (*shabbat shabbaton*, "a complete Sabbath/rest") refers to the most holy days. The term **עֲצֶרֶת** (*ʿatseret*, "solemn assembly", v. 36) designates the concluding day of a festival and involves an assembly where the people are "held back" from ordinary work.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Gezer Calendar, a limestone tablet dating to approximately 925–900 BCE found in 1908 at Gezer (Tell el-Jazari), offers insight into the Israelite agricultural cycle. It lists the months of the year according to farming activities such as sowing, flax harvest, barley harvest, and grape harvest. This confirms the close connection between the festival calendar in Leviticus 23 and the actual agricultural year in Israel—Passover coincided with the barley harvest in spring, and the Feast of Tabernacles with the grape harvest in autumn.

Archaeological discoveries from the Judean Desert, including texts from Qumran (the Dead Sea Scrolls), show that Jewish groups during the Second Temple period engaged in intense debates over exactly when the festivals should be observed, particularly regarding the interpretation of "the day after the Sabbath" (vv. 11, 15) for counting the omer. This demonstrates that this chapter was central to Jewish religious practice and identity throughout the centuries.
