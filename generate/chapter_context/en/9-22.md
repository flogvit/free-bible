# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 22

## People

**Doeg the Edomite** (דֹּאֵג הָאֲדֹמִי) was introduced in the previous chapter as Saul’s "chief shepherd" (21:8), but plays a central role in this chapter. He came from Edom, Israel’s neighboring people to the southeast. The fact that an Edomite held a trusted position at Saul’s court shows that foreigners could serve Israelite kings, yet his willingness to kill the LORD’s priests stands in sharp contrast to the refusal of the Israelite servants. His Edomite background may explain why he did not share the Israelites’ reverence for the priesthood.

**Abiathar** (אֶבְיָתָר), son of Ahimelech, is the sole survivor of the massacre at Nob. His flight to David marks the beginning of an important relationship in which Abiathar becomes David’s priest.

**The prophet Gad** (גָּד הַנָּבִיא) is mentioned here for the first time as David’s prophet. He instructs David to leave the stronghold and return to the land of Judah (v. 5).

## Places

**The Cave of Adullam** (מְעָרַת עֲדֻלָּם) is located in the lowlands of Judah (the Shephelah), about 20 km southwest of Jerusalem. The area is known for its many limestone caves that provide natural protection. Archaeological investigations have confirmed the existence of large cave systems in this region capable of sheltering significant groups of people. The site was strategically positioned between the mountains and the coastal plain.

**Mizpah in Moab** (מִצְפֵּה מוֹאָב) was a place in Moab where David placed his parents for safety. David’s connection to Moab through his great-grandmother Ruth (Ruth 4:17) explains why he could expect goodwill from the king of Moab.

**Forest of Hereth** (יַעַר חָרֶת, "Hereth Forest") is the place where David went after the prophet Gad’s instruction to leave the stronghold and return to Judah (v. 5). Its exact location is uncertain but it was in the region of Judah.

**Gibeah** (הַגִּבְעָה) is mentioned as the place where Saul sat under the tamarisk tree with his spear in hand, surrounded by his servants (v. 6). This was Saul’s hometown and administrative center.

**Nob** (נֹב), the priestly city, was likely located north of Jerusalem, possibly on Mount Scopus or nearby. The complete destruction of the city, including women, children, and livestock, recalls herem warfare (the ban), but here directed against Israel’s own priests—a grotesque perversion of holy war.

## Events and Customs

**The formation of David’s warrior band** follows a pattern known in the ancient Near East where men in distress, burdened by debt, or discontent gathered around charismatic leaders. The Hebrew expression "מַר־נֶפֶשׁ" (bitter in soul) describes people in deep despair. About 400 men gathered around David (v. 2).

**The servants’ refusal** to kill the priests demonstrates the deep reverence Israelites had for those who wore the "linen ephod" (אֵפוֹד בָּד). The ephod was a sacred priestly garment, and killing 85 priests who wore it was an unheard-of crime. Saul’s use of a foreigner to carry out this wicked act underscores how he had alienated himself from his own people and their religious values.

## Key Terms

The expression **"בְּנֵי יְמִינִי"** (sons of Benjamin) in verse 7 is used by Saul to appeal to tribal loyalty. He implied that David, from the tribe of Judah, would not reward Benjamites as Saul could. This reveals the underlying tribal tension in the early Israelite monarchy.

**"לִפְגֹּעַ בְּכֹהֲנֵי יְהוָֽה"** (to lay hands on the LORD’s priests) uses the verb פָּגַע, which can mean both "meet" and "attack." The servants’ reluctance to "strike" the priests shows that even royal orders could not override the sacred protection surrounding the LORD’s servants.
