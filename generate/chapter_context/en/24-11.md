# Historical and Cultural Context for Jeremiah 11

## People

**The Men of Anathoth** (אַנְשֵׁי עֲנָתוֹת, vv. 21–23) were Jeremiah’s fellow countrymen and likely relatives who conspired against him. They threatened to kill him if he continued prophesying in the Lord’s name. Their opposition was especially painful because they belonged to his own hometown and priestly lineage. God’s judgment upon them included that their young men would die by the sword and their children by famine.

## Places

**Anathoth** (עֲנָתוֹת, vv. 21–23) was Jeremiah’s hometown, a priestly city in the tribe of Benjamin located about 5 km northeast of Jerusalem. The site is identified with present-day Anata. It held significance as one of the cities assigned to the Levites (Josh 21:18) and was the home of the priest Abiathar, who was banished there by Solomon (1 Kgs 2:26).

## Events and Customs

Chapter 11 focuses on "this covenant" (הַבְּרִית הַזֹּאת), referring to the Sinai covenant described in Deuteronomy. The expression "iron furnace" (כּוּר הַבַּרְזֶל, v. 4) was an established metaphor for Egypt as a place of testing and purification, drawn directly from Deut 4:20. Iron smelting furnaces were known in the ancient Near East as extremely hot ovens used for metalworking, and the image underscores both the intensity of suffering and the transformative purpose of the Exodus from Egypt.

The covenant formula "you shall be my people, and I will be your God" (v. 4) appears here as a reminder of the covenant relationship that Judah has broken. The curse pronounced on anyone who breaks the covenant (v. 3) mirrors the curses in Deut 27–28, which were a standard part of covenant documents in the ancient Near East.

## Key Terms

The word **"conspiracy"** (קֶשֶׁר, qesher, v. 9) is commonly used in the Old Testament to describe political conspiracies and coups. That God applies this term to Judah’s sin suggests that the people’s apostasy was not merely individual weakness but a coordinated, deliberate rebellion against the covenant relationship.

The expression **"let us destroy the tree with its food"** (נַשְׁחִיתָה עֵץ בְּלַחְמוֹ, v. 19) is debated. The Hebrew בְּלַחְמוֹ literally means "with its bread/food" but is often interpreted as "in its vitality" or "with its fruit". The image seems to indicate that the enemies intended to kill the prophet while he was still in his prime, so as to wipe out both him and his legacy.

**"Shame"** (בֹּשֶׁת, boshet, v. 13) is a derogatory title used for Baal in the Old Testament. Hebrew scribes often replaced "Baal" with "boshet" (shame) to express contempt for the idol. The number of altars "for shame" corresponded to the number of streets in Jerusalem, indicating how widespread idolatry had become.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered numerous cultic objects and altars from the period before the Babylonian exile, confirming the widespread religious syncretism that Jeremiah condemned. In particular, discoveries of Baal figurines and incense altars in private houses throughout Judah support the biblical description that idolatry occurred "in every street" and "in every city".
