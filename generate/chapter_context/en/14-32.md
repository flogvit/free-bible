# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Chronicles 32

## People

**Sennacherib** (סַנְחֵרִיב) was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire (705–681 B.C.). The campaign against Judah in 701 B.C. was part of a larger effort to suppress revolts throughout the Levant. The chapter notes that he was killed by his own sons in the temple of his god (v. 21), an event Assyrian sources confirm occurred in 681 B.C.

**Isaiah son of Amoz** (יְשַׁעְיָהוּ בֶן־אָמוֹץ) is mentioned as a prophet who prayed together with Hezekiah (v. 20). This is one of the few places in Chronicles where Isaiah is named, whereas the parallel account in 2 Kings gives the prophet a much more prominent role with detailed prophecies.

## Places

**Lachish** (לָכִישׁ) is mentioned as the place where Sennacherib stayed while sending his threats to Jerusalem (v. 9). Lachish was the second most important city in Judah and a key fortress protecting the road to Egypt. Archaeological excavations at Tell ed-Duweir have uncovered massive destruction from this period. Sennacherib himself commissioned extensive reliefs depicting the siege of Lachish that decorated his palace in Nineveh—these are now displayed in the British Museum and show in detail how the Assyrians attacked the city.

**The Gihon Spring** (גִּיחוֹן) is mentioned in connection with Hezekiah’s water project (v. 30). The spring lies in the Kidron Valley east of the City of David and was Jerusalem’s most important water source. Hezekiah "stopped up the outlet" from this spring and directed the water westward to the Pool of Siloam inside the city.

## Events and Customs

**Psychological warfare**: The Assyrians systematically used propaganda as part of their military strategy. The chapter describes how Sennacherib’s envoys shouted in Judean (יְהוּדִית) to the people on the walls (v. 18) to create fear and division. By comparing Israel’s God with the idols of conquered nations (vv. 13–15, 19), they attempted to undermine resistance—a tactic well documented in Assyrian royal inscriptions.

**Blocking water sources** (vv. 3–4) was a recognized defensive strategy in antiquity. By stopping up springs and streams outside the city, Hezekiah denied the enemy army access to water, which was critical for withstanding a prolonged siege in the dry climate.

## Key Terms

**"The arm of flesh"** (זְרוֹעַ בָּשָׂר, v. 8) is an expression Hezekiah uses regarding Assyrian military power, contrasting it with God’s help. The phrase denotes human strength that is limited and perishable, in contrast to God’s power.

**Millo** (הַמִּלּוֹא, v. 5) refers to a specific fortification structure in Jerusalem, likely a terrace or fill construction that was part of the city’s defenses since the time of David.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**Hezekiah’s Tunnel** is one of the most impressive archaeological discoveries from biblical times. The tunnel, which is 533 meters long and carved through solid rock, carries water from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam. An inscription found in the tunnel in 1880 (now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museums) describes how workers carved from opposite ends and met in the middle. The tunnel directly confirms what is described in verse 30.

**Sennacherib’s Prism** (the Taylor Prism and similar copies) contains Sennacherib’s own account of the campaign in 701 B.C. There he boasts of having besieged 46 of Hezekiah’s fortified cities and imprisoned Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem. Remarkably, he never claims to have captured Jerusalem itself—a tacit confirmation of the biblical account that the city did not fall.

**LMLK Seals** (למלך, "belonging to the king") are stamps found on hundreds of jars from Hezekiah’s time. These likely represent a centralized food supply system prepared in anticipation of an Assyrian invasion and confirm the chapter’s description of Hezekiah’s extensive preparations.
