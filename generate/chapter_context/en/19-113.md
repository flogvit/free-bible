# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 113

## Events and Customs

Psalm 113 is the first of the six "Hallel psalms" (113–118), which were sung during the major Jewish festivals: Passover, Pentecost, the Feast of Tabernacles, and Hanukkah. At the Passover meal, Psalms 113–114 were traditionally sung before the meal, and Psalms 115–118 afterward. This means that Jesus and his disciples likely sang this psalm at their last supper (cf. Matt 26:30). The expression "servants of the Lord" (עַבְדֵי יְהוָה) in verse 1 refers to the assembly of worshippers, possibly specifically the Levites who served in the temple.

## Key Terms

The psalm opens and closes with "Hallelujah" (הַלְלוּ יָהּ), an imperative calling for praise of Yahweh. The short form "Yah" (יָהּ) is a poetic abbreviation of the divine name YHWH. The expression "from the rising of the sun to its setting" (verse 3) is a merism—a literary technique that uses two extremes to express wholeness, meaning "everywhere on earth."

The word "ash heap" (אַשְׁפֹּת) in verse 7 refers to the garbage dumps outside cities where the poorest searched for food and warmth. The fact that God lifts someone "from the dust" and "from the ash heap" illustrates the radical social elevation that only God can accomplish. This imagery has clear parallels with Hannah's song of praise in 1 Samuel 2:8, suggesting literary dependence or a shared tradition.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

Psalm 113:9, concerning the barren woman who becomes "a joyful mother," has interesting parallels in other Old Testament texts, especially Hannah's song (1 Sam 2:1–10). In ancient Israel, barrenness was a source of great social shame and personal sorrow, while fertility was seen as a blessing from God. Therefore, God giving children to the barren woman was regarded as a powerful sign of divine intervention and care for the marginalized—a recurring theme in the psalm alongside the exaltation of the poor.
