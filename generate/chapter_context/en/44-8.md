# Historical and Cultural Context for Acts 8

## People

**Philip** (Φίλιππος) was one of the seven men chosen to serve at the tables in chapter 6, and must not be confused with the apostle Philip. He is later referred to as "Philip the evangelist" (Acts 21:8) and was the first to bring the gospel beyond Jewish circles on a large scale. His ministry in Samaria and toward the Ethiopian official represent important milestones in the spread of the gospel.

**Simon (the Magician)** was a well-known figure in Samaria who practiced magic (μαγεύων) and was credited with divine power by the people. Early church historians such as Irenaeus and Justin Martyr (himself from Samaria) refer to him as the founder of Gnostic heresy. The term "simony" – the buying and selling of spiritual offices – originates from his attempt to buy the power of the Holy Spirit. Historically, he represents a type of religious entrepreneur common in the Hellenistic world.

**The Ethiopian official** (εὐνοῦχος δυνάστης) was the treasurer under **Candace** (Κανδάκης), which was a dynastic title for queen mothers in the Nubian kingdom of Meroë (modern-day Sudan). The term "eunuch" could mean a castrated court official or simply a high-ranking officer. If he was actually castrated, according to Deuteronomy 23:1 he would have been excluded from full participation in temple worship, making his conversion theologically significant.

## Places

**Samaria** was the region between Judea and Galilee, inhabited by Samaritans – a people of mixed Israelite and pagan origin following Assyrian deportations and population transfers in the 700s BC. Jews and Samaritans had an hostile relationship that had lasted for centuries. The Samaritans had their own sanctuary on Mount Gerizim and accepted only the five books of Moses as sacred scripture. Archaeological excavations at Sebaste (the Hellenistic city in Samaria) have revealed significant structures from the Hellenistic and Roman periods.

**The road from Jerusalem to Gaza** ran southwest toward the Mediterranean coast. Gaza was one of the ancient Philistine cities and served as an important trade hub on the route to Egypt. The addition "this is desert" (αὕτη ἐστὶν ἔρημος) may refer either to the fact that the road passed through wilderness, or to old Gaza which was destroyed in 96 BC and rebuilt closer to the coast.

**Ashdod** (Ἄζωτος) was the Greek name for the ancient Philistine city of Ashdod, one of the five Philistine cities. **Caesarea** (Καισάρεια) was the great port city built by Herod the Great, and later became Philip's home city (Acts 21:8). Archaeological excavations have revealed Herod's impressive harbor facilities and theater.

## Events and Customs

**Burial and mourning rituals** (verse 2): The expression "made great lamentation" (κοπετὸν μέγαν) refers to traditional Jewish mourning practices that included loud weeping, beating the breast, and wailing. That "devout men" (ἄνδρες εὐλαβεῖς) performed this for Stephen even though he had been executed as a blasphemer was a courageous act demonstrating that they recognized him as righteous.

**Magic in the Hellenistic world**: Simon's practice (μαγεύων) was part of a widespread phenomenon in antiquity. Magicians often combined elements from various religious traditions and claimed access to divine powers. Papyrus finds from Egypt (the so-called "Greek magical papyri") document extensive magical practices during this period, including spells, amulets, and rituals.

**Jewish pilgrimage**: The fact that the Ethiopian official had gone to Jerusalem to worship (προσκυνήσων) and owned a scroll of Isaiah indicates that he was either a Jewish proselyte or "God-fearing" (a Gentile who worshipped the God of Israel without full conversion). Scrolls were expensive, and his possession of the prophecy of Isaiah demonstrates both his wealth and his religious seriousness.

## Key Terms

**"The power of God, which is called Great"** (ἡ Δύναμις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ καλουμένη Μεγάλη) was the title given to Simon by the people. "Power" (δύναμις) was a common circumlocution for divine beings in Jewish and Samaritan thought. The Samaritans expected a Taheb (a Moses-like restorer), and Simon may have exploited such expectations.

**Laying on of hands** (ἐπίθεσις τῶν χειρῶν) was an established Jewish practice for blessing, ordination, and the transfer of authority. In this context it is connected with the imparting of the Holy Spirit, demonstrating the unique authority of the apostles in the early church.

**"Bitter gall and a chain of unrighteousness"** (χολὴν πικρίας καὶ σύνδεσμον ἀδικίας) in verse 23 is an allusion to Deuteronomy 29:18 (Septuagint), which warns against idolatry. Peter uses this Old Testament language to describe Simon's spiritual condition.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Kingdom of Meroë and Candace**: Archaeological excavations in Sudan have uncovered pyramids and temples from the Meroitic kingdom, which flourished from approximately 300 BC to 300 AD. Inscriptions confirm that "Candace" was a royal title used by several queens and queen mothers. This kingdom had extensive trade connections with the Mediterranean world and known contacts with the Jewish world.

**Early Ethiopian Christianity**: The tradition of the Ethiopian church claims that the official, often called Simeon Bachos, brought the gospel to Ethiopia and founded the Christian church there. Although this cannot be confirmed historically, it testifies to the chapter's significance for Ethiopian Christian identity. The Ethiopian church is one of the world's oldest Christian traditions.
