# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Solomon 2

## Places

**The Plain of Sharon** (הַשָּׁרוֹן, v. 1) was a fertile coastal plain stretching along the Mediterranean Sea from Jaffa to Mount Carmel. In biblical times, this area was known for its lush vegetation and abundant flowers. Isaiah 35:2 mentions "the glory of Sharon" as a symbol of beauty and abundance. Archaeological studies show that in ancient times the region had plenty of wildflowers in spring, making the reference to "the flower of Sharon" (traditionally called "the rose of Sharon", possibly a crocus or narcissus) natural.

**The Mountains of Beter** (הָרֵי בָתֶר, v. 17) are disputed. Some scholars identify this with Beter (Bittir), a village southwest of Jerusalem that later became known as the last stronghold of the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 CE). Others interpret "bater" as "cleft mountains" – that is, mountains with deep valleys, which fits the imagery of the gazelle.

## Key Terms

**Chavatzelet** (חֲבַצֶּלֶת, v. 1) is uncertainly identified. The Septuagint translates it as "flower" (ἄνθος), while modern botanists suggest crocus, narcissus, or tulip. This word appears only here and in Isaiah 35:1 in the Hebrew Bible.

**The Winehouse** (בֵּית הַיַּיִן, v. 4) likely refers to a banquet hall where wine was served. In the ancient Near East, such gatherings were important social occasions, often associated with celebration and love. The phrase "his banner over me is love" (וְדִגְלוֹ עָלַי אַהֲבָה) may allude to military banners that marked belonging and protection.

**Raisin Cakes** (אֲשִׁישׁוֹת, v. 5) were pressed, dried grapes shaped into cakes. They were a delicacy in ancient Israel and used both as food and in cultic contexts (cf. Hosea 3:1). Combined with apples, they were considered strengthening food.

## Events and Customs

**An Oath by the Gazelles and Does** (v. 7) is an unusual oath formula repeated three times in Song of Solomon. In Israelite culture, oaths were sacred and binding. Swearing by wild animals rather than by God is unique in biblical literature and may reflect the distinctive sphere of love poetry – separate from the strictly religious. Gazelles and does were associated with grace and love throughout the ancient Near East.

**Signs of Spring** (vv. 11–13) are described with precision, reflecting the Israelite agricultural year: winter rains end in March–April, flowers bloom, the turtledove arrives as a migratory bird, fig trees ripen their early fruit (פַגֶּיהָ), and grapevines blossom. This was the time for weddings and celebration in agrarian communities.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

The use of dove imagery (v. 14) has been illuminated by archaeological discoveries of rock-cut tombs and natural caves in Judea's mountainous terrain. Doves nested in such rock crevices, making the description "my dove in the clefts of the rock" a naturalistic observation. The dove was also a symbol of love and faithfulness throughout Near Eastern culture, and dove figurines have been found in many archaeological contexts from the Bronze Age.
