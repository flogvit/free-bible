## Musical Direction

The expression "ajjélet hasjachar" (אַיֶּלֶת הַשַּׁחַר) in the heading literally means "the hind of the dawn" or "the deer of the morning." This is likely the name of a well-known melody to which the psalm was sung. In the ancient Near East, it was common to give melodies poetic names, often based on the opening words of a popular song. The image of the dawn may also point to the psalm's movement from deep distress to deliverance—from the darkness of night to the light of day.

## Events and Customs

The description of the enemies as "strong bulls of Bashan" (v. 13) draws its imagery from the powerful oxen raised in the fertile region of Bashan east of the Sea of Galilee. These animals were known for their strength and aggression and were used throughout the ancient Near East as symbols of mighty, threatening foes.

The dividing of garments and the casting of lots (v. 19) reflects a widespread practice in antiquity in which the possessions of a condemned person were distributed among executioners or onlookers. Casting lots was a common method for deciding how to divide valuable items, and this custom is documented in several cultures throughout the region.

The conclusion with promises of a communal meal and praise (vv. 23–27) points to the thank offering practice in Israelite worship. After deliverance from distress, the saved person was to bring a thank offering, and the meat was shared in a festive meal with family, friends, and the poor as an expression of gratitude and community.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word "tola'at" (תּוֹלַעַת, "worm," v. 7) describes the deepest form of degradation and contempt for humanity. The word is also used for the crimson color extracted from a certain type of insect, creating a double association with both lowliness and blood.

The expression in verse 17 concerning hands and feet is textually disputed. The Masoretic Text has "ka'ari" (כָּאֲרִי, "like a lion"), while other textual traditions (the Septuagint, some Dead Sea Scrolls) support the reading "ka'aru" ("they pierced"). This variation has been central to discussions about the messianic interpretation of the psalm.

"Jechidati" (יְחִידָתִי, "my only one," v. 21) is a rare poetic expression for the soul or life, literally "my only [one]." The word emphasizes that each person has only one soul, one life, which must be protected.
