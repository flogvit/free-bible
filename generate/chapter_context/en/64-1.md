## People

The Third Letter of John mentions four people by name, which is unusually many for such a short letter. **Gaius** (Γαΐῳ) was the recipient of the letter. The name was very common in antiquity, so it is uncertain whether he can be identified with other persons of the same name in the New Testament. Gaius was evidently a wealthy Christian who had the ability to offer hospitality to traveling preachers.

**Diotrephes** (Διοτρέφης) is a rare Greek name meaning "nurtured by Zeus," suggesting a pagan background. He appears as a local leader who "loves to be first" (φιλοπρωτεύων)—a unique word in the New Testament. His behavior illustrates tensions regarding authority and hospitality in the early congregations. **Demetrius** (Δημητρίῳ) receives strong commendation from the author (verse 12) and was possibly the bearer of the letter. The name was common and associated with the goddess Demeter. It is unlikely that he is identical to the silversmith in Ephesus (Acts 19:24).

## Events and Customs

The letter provides important insight into **the role of traveling preachers** in the early church. Verses 5–8 describe the practice of "hospitality" (φιλοξενία) toward Christian missionaries who traveled between congregations. These travelers did not accept support from "Gentiles" (ἐθνικῶν), that is, non-believers, to avoid making the gospel appear bought or compromised. This made them dependent on the hospitality of local Christians. The expression "send them on their way in a manner worthy of God" (προπέμψας ἀξίως τοῦ θεοῦ) meant equipping them with food, money, and companions for the next stage of their journey.

**Exclusion from the congregation** (verse 10) was a serious action in early Christianity. The fact that Diotrephes "casts out of the congregation" (ἐκ τῆς ἐκκλησίας ἐκβάλλει) those who wanted to receive the traveling brothers shows that church discipline was already established practice, but here it was misused for personal purposes.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the elder"** (ὁ πρεσβύτερος) in verse 1 is identical with the opening of Second John. The term can mean both "the older" in age and a leadership office in the congregation. That the author uses it as his only identification suggests that the recipient knew who he was.

**"Walk in the truth"** (ἐν ἀληθείᾳ περιπατεῖς, verses 3–4) combines Hebrew thought about "walking" as a metaphor for conduct with the Johannine focus on truth as faithfulness to Christ and his teaching. The verb "walk" (περιπατέω) is used repeatedly in the Johannine writings concerning Christian life.

The greeting **"face to face"** (στόμα πρὸς στόμα, verse 14) is a Semitic expression known from Numbers 12:8, where it describes God's direct communication with Moses. Here it expresses the desire for personal conversation rather than written communication.
