# Historical and Cultural Context for Psalms 55

## People

The psalm is attributed to David and describes an intense personal betrayal by a close friend and advisor. Although the text does not name this person, Jewish and Christian tradition has often linked the psalm to Ahithophel's betrayal during Absalom's rebellion (2 Samuel 15–17). Ahithophel was David's trusted counselor whose advice "was as if one inquired of God himself" (2 Sam 16:23), yet he joined Absalom's revolt. The description of someone who was "my equal, my friend and close companion" (v. 14) and who "walked with me to the house of God" (v. 15) fits well with such a high-ranking advisor who shared both political and religious fellowship with the king.

## Places

The psalm mentions "the city" (vv. 10–12) where violence, strife, wickedness, and deceit prevail on its walls and in its streets. This likely refers to Jerusalem, David's capital. The reference to city walls with guards patrolling day and night reflects the typical fortified Israelite city. The psalmist's wish to flee to "the wilderness" (v. 8) reflects the proximity of the Judean wilderness to Jerusalem—a traditional refuge for the persecuted, as David himself fled there during times of persecution (1 Sam 23–24).

## Key Terms

The word **יְהָבְךָ** (*yehavkha*, "your burden") in verse 23 is a rare Hebrew word that appears only here in the Old Testament. It is related to an Aramaic word and means "that which is given you to bear"—that is, life's burdens and cares. This verse is quoted in 1 Peter 5:7.

The expression **סוֹד** (*sod*, "confidential talk/secret") in verse 15 describes the deep, private communication between close friends. In Israelite culture, such intimacy was sacred, and breaking it was a serious violation of trust.

## Events and Customs

The description of going "in the crowd to the house of God" (v. 15) refers to the Israelite custom of communal worship at the temple or tabernacle. **בְּרָגֶשׁ** (*beragesh*) means "in the crowd" or "with movement/noise" and describes the gathering of worshippers. The fact that the psalmist shared this holy experience with his betrayer makes the betrayal all the more painful—it broke both human friendship and sacred fellowship.

The curse that enemies should "go down alive to Sheol" (v. 16) has parallels with Korah's rebellion, where the earth opened up and swallowed the rebels (Num 16:30–33). This was the ultimate punishment for treason against God's chosen ones.
