# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 27

## Places

The chapter mentions two mountains that played a central role in the ceremony: **Ebal** and **Gerizim**. These mountains are located in the central highlands of Samaria, with the ancient city of Shechem in the valley between them. Ebal lies north of the valley (about 940 meters above sea level) and Gerizim south of it (about 881 meters). The location was strategically important—Shechem lay at a major crossroads and was already connected to the patriarchs (Gen 12:6; 33:18-20). Archaeological excavations on Ebal have uncovered a large stone structure from the early Iron Age (about 1200 BCE) that some scholars have identified as an altar, although this remains debated.

## Events and Customs

**The Stone Monument with Inscription**: The practice of erecting large stones, coating them with plaster (שִׂיד, *sid*), and writing laws on them was known in the ancient Near East. Egyptian and Mesopotamian cultures erected similar monuments. The plaster provided a white, smooth surface that made the writing visible and readable from a distance. The expression "clearly and plainly" (בַּאֵר הֵיטֵב, *ba'er hetev*) suggests that the text was to be easily understood by all.

**The Altar of Uncut Stones**: The prohibition against using iron tools on the stones of the altar (v. 5-6) reflects an important distinction between the holy and the profane. Iron tools were associated with war and human craftsmanship, while uncut stones represented nature as God created it. Similar prohibitions are found in Exod 20:25.

**The Blessing and Cursing Ceremony**: The litany of twelve curses (v. 15-26), with the people's "amen" response after each curse, has parallels in Hittite treaty texts and Egyptian cursing rituals. This form is called a "dodecalogue" (twelvefold), possibly corresponding to the twelve tribes. The Levites proclaiming the curses while the people responded created a public, binding obligation.

## Key Terms

**אָרוּר (*arur*)** – "cursed": This word begins each of the twelve declarations. It denotes a state of being excluded from God's blessing and the community's protection. The curse is activated automatically when the prohibition is broken, without the need for human legal proceedings.

**אָמֵן (*amen*)**: The people's response means "so let it be" or "this is true and binding". By saying amen, each Israelite personally took responsibility for the covenant obligations and their consequences.

**הַסְכֵּת (*hasket*)** – "be silent and listen" (v. 9): This rare Hebrew verb occurs only twice in the Old Testament—here and in Deut 32:1. It implies a call to reverent, attentive silence before an important declaration.

## Archaeological Finds

The so-called **Ebal structure**, excavated by archaeologist Adam Zertal between 1982 and 1989, has attracted considerable interest. The structure is a rectangular stone building with ramps and ash-filled layers containing animal bones (mainly from young oxen, sheep, and goats). Zertal identified this as Joshua's altar mentioned in Josh 8:30-35. Other scholars are more cautious and suggest alternative interpretations. Regardless of the identification, the find confirms cultic activity on the mountain during the relevant period.
