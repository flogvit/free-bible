## People

The chapter describes the birth of six of Jacob’s sons and his daughter Dinah, born to four different women. **Bilhah** was Rachel’s maidservant whom she brought as a dowry when she married Jacob. **Zilpah** was similarly Leah’s maidservant. Both women had a legal status that allowed the children they bore to be counted as their mistresses’ own children. The names of the sons reflect the mothers’ feelings and circumstances: Dan ("he has judged"), Naphtali ("my struggle"), Gad ("good fortune"), Asher ("happy"), Issachar ("reward"), Zebulun (from זָבַל "to honor/dwell with"), and Joseph ("may he add"). The daughter **Dinah** is also briefly mentioned as Leah’s daughter (v. 21). **Reuben**, Leah’s firstborn son (born in ch. 29), finds mandrakes during the wheat harvest and brings them to his mother Leah (v. 14).

## Events and Customs

The practice of a barren wife giving her maidservant to her husband so that she may bear children through her (vv. 3–4, 9) was an established custom in the ancient Near East. The expression "bear upon my knees" (v. 3, Hebrew: עַל־בִּרְכַּי) refers to an adoption rite in which the wife took the child on her lap at birth as a symbol that she accepted it as her own. This practice is documented in contemporary Nuzi texts.

The negotiations between Rachel and Leah over **dudaim** (love apples, vv. 14–16) reflect popular belief in certain plants’ fertility-enhancing properties. Dudaim are usually identified as mandrake root (*Mandragora officinarum*), a plant with a forked root that grows in the Mediterranean region. In antiquity it was regarded as an aphrodisiac and fertility aid because of the humanlike shape of its root.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **נָחַשׁ** (nachash) in verse 27, translated "I have observed" or "I have divined," indicates that Laban used some form of omen interpretation or occult practice to determine that the Lord’s blessing was with Jacob. This was a common Mesopotamian practice that contrasted with the patriarchs’ faith in direct divine revelation.

The terms **נָקֹד** (nakod, "speckled"), **טָלוּא** (talua, "spotted") and **עָקֹד** (akod, "ring-streaked/streaked around the legs") describe the various color variations in the small livestock that formed the basis of Jacob’s wage agreement with Laban. In a region where white and solid-colored light animals were the norm and considered more valuable, Jacob’s demand for the speckled and dark animals appeared to be an unfavorable deal.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Jacob’s method of placing striped rods in front of the animals during mating (vv. 37–42) reflects a widespread ancient folk belief that visual impressions at conception could influence the appearance of offspring. This theory, called "maternal impression," was commonly accepted in many cultures until modern times. The trees mentioned—**לִבְנֶה** (livneh, poplar or styrax tree), **לוּז** (luz, almond tree) and **עֶרְמוֹן** (armon, plane tree)—are all native to the Haran area and had white wood beneath their bark, providing the visual contrast that Jacob exploited. The text itself does not attribute Jacob’s success to the rod method; in Genesis 31:7–12, Jacob explains that it was God who intervened and gave him wealth despite Laban’s attempts to deceive him.
