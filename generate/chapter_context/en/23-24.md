# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 24

## Places

Isaiah 24 mentions several geographical references that carry symbolic and theological significance. The expression "the isles of the sea" (verse 15) refers to the coastal lands and islands of the Mediterranean, a common term for distant nations and the ends of the earth. "The end of the earth" (verse 16) reinforces this universal perspective. The chapter culminates with "Mount Zion and Jerusalem" (verse 23), contrasting the extent of destruction with the place where the Lord will reveal His glory. This contrast between universal judgment and the exaltation of Jerusalem is central to the chapter's eschatological vision.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the cessation of festivities and wine production (verses 7–11), reflecting the central role that wine and harvest festivals played in Israelite culture. The grape harvest was a time of great joy, accompanied by music from tambourines and harps. The stopping of these customs signals total societal collapse. The description of social classes in verse 2 (priest and people, slave and master, buyer and seller) shows the leveling aspect of judgment—all social distinctions are abolished.

## Key Terms

The Hebrew word **בּוֹקֵק** (*boqeq*, "empties/destroys") in verse 1 is a rare and powerful verb describing complete emptying. The expression **קִרְיַת־תֹּהוּ** (*qirjat-tohu*, "city of emptiness") in verse 10 uses the same word (*tohu*) as in Genesis 1:2 regarding the primordial state before creation, suggesting that judgment reverses the work of creation. **בְּרִית עוֹלָם** (*berit olam*, "everlasting covenant") in verse 5 may refer to the covenant with Noah (Gen 9) or universal moral principles that bind all humanity. The expression **צְבָא הַמָּרוֹם** (*tseva hamarom*, "host of the heights") in verse 21 denotes heavenly beings or powers who will also be called to account.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The image of the windows on high being opened (verse 18, אֲרֻבּוֹת מִמָּרוֹם) and the earth shaking has parallels both to the flood narrative in Genesis 7:11 and to Mesopotamian cosmic catastrophe texts. This reflects an Ancient Near Eastern worldview where chaotic forces threatened the created order from above and below. The notion that the moon "is put to shame" (חָפְרָה) and the sun "is ashamed" (בוֹשָׁה) before the Lord's glory (verse 23) reflects a polemic against astral cults widespread in Assyria and Babylon, where the sun and moon were worshipped as deities.
