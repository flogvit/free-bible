# Historical and Cultural Context for 2 Kings 23

## People

**Josiah** appears in this chapter as the great reforming king who carried out the most comprehensive religious cleansing in Judah's history. The reforms described here took place in his eighteenth year of reign (v. 23), during a period when Assyria's weakening gave Judah greater political room to maneuver.

**Hilkiah** was the high priest and led the work of removing idolatrous objects from the temple (v. 4). **Pharaoh Neco II** of Egypt is the Egyptian ruler who killed Josiah at Megiddo (v. 29). He was heading north "toward the king of Assyria by the Euphrates River" (עַל־מֶלֶךְ אַשּׁוּר עַל־נְהַר־פְּרָת) – likely to support the weakened Assyrian power against Babylon. **Jehoahaz** and **Jehoiakim** were sons of Josiah who succeeded him; Neco deposed Jehoahaz after only three months and installed Jehoiakim as a vassal king (v. 30-34).

## Places

The **Kidron Valley** (נַחַל קִדְרוֹן), which runs between Jerusalem and the Mount of Olives, served as a dumping ground for the idolatrous objects that were destroyed (v. 4, 6, 12). This place had traditionally been used to remove unclean items from the city.

**Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom** (גֵּי בֶן־הִנֹּם, v. 10) was located south of Jerusalem and was the site of Molech worship involving child sacrifice. The name "Gehenna" (Greek for Valley of Hinnom) was later used as a designation for hell.

**Bethel** was the main sanctuary of the northern kingdom, established by Jeroboam I (v. 15-17). That Josiah extended his reform here shows that he took advantage of Assyria's weakening to assert control over former Israelite territory. **Megiddo** (v. 29-30) was a strategically important fortified city on a major route between Egypt and Mesopotamia. Here Josiah met his death when he attempted to stop the Egyptian army.

## Events and Customs

The **Kemarim** (הַכְּמָרִים, v. 5) were idolatrous priests, a term used only negatively in the Old Testament. These had been appointed by earlier kings to serve at the high places. Worship of "the host of heaven" (צְבָא הַשָּׁמָיִם) included the sun, moon, and constellations (מַזָּלוֹת), an astral cult with strong ties to Mesopotamian religion.

**Cultic servants** are mentioned in verse 7, where קְדֵשִׁים (literally: "holy/consecrated ones") had rooms within the temple itself. Women wove there "houses" (בָּתִּים) for Asherah – likely tent-like structures, garments, or draperies for the cult object.

The **horses and chariots dedicated to the sun** (v. 11) reflect a sun cult that was widespread in the Middle East. Assyrian kings mention similar practices, and sun worship was central to Egyptian religion. The fact that these stood at the entrance of the Lord's house shows how deeply syncretism had penetrated.

## Key Terms

The **Book of the Covenant** (סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית, v. 2, 21), which was read aloud and formed the basis for the reform, is identified by many scholars with an early form of Deuteronomy or parts of it. The expression in verse 3 describes that "all the people stood in the covenant" (וַיַּעֲמֹד כָּל־הָעָם בַּבְּרִית) – a formal renewal of the covenant with active participation by the people.

**Molech** (לַמֹּלֶךְ, v. 10) was a Canaanite deity associated with child sacrifice. The expression "to make pass through the fire" (לְהַעֲבִיר ... בָּאֵשׁ) is the technical term for this practice. Phoenician and Punic inscriptions confirm that such practices existed in the region.

**Ob and Yiddeoni** (אֹבוֹת וְיִדְּעֹנִים, v. 24) refer to spirit mediums and familiar spirits – practices strictly forbidden in Israelite law but evidently widespread among the people.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations in Jerusalem have uncovered numerous fertility figurines from the period before Josiah's reform, confirming the text's description of widespread idolatry. In Arad, remains of an Israelite sanctuary that was systematically closed have been found – possibly as part of Josiah's centralization reform.

Babylonian chronicles confirm the political backdrop for this chapter: Assyria's fall, Babylon's rise, and Egypt's attempt to intervene in the struggle for power. The chronicles date the battle at Harran to 609 BC, the same year as Josiah's death at Megiddo, confirming the historical context for the events in verses 29-30.
