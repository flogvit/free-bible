# Historical and Cultural Context for Joel 1

## People

Joel is introduced as the "son of Petuel" (בֶּן־פְּתוּאֵל), but neither the prophet nor his father is known from other biblical texts. Petuel is a rare name that may mean "God's persuasion" or "God's openness."

## Key Terms

The chapter mentions four different Hebrew words for locusts: גָּזָם (gazam), אַרְבֶּה (arbeh), יֶלֶק (jelek) and חָסִיל (chasil) in verse 4. Scholars debate whether this represents four different species, four developmental stages of the same species, or a poetic accumulation to emphasize the extent of the destruction. The term אַרְבֶּה is the most common Hebrew word for locust and is also used regarding the plagues in Egypt (Exodus 10). In verse 6, the locusts are described metaphorically as a "nation" (גּוֹי) with lion's teeth, which underscores the overwhelming character of the invasion.

The expression "the day of the Lord" (יוֹם יְהוָה) in verse 15 is a central theological concept in prophetic literature that describes God's powerful intervention in history, often with themes of judgment (cf. Amos 5:18–20).

## Events and Customs

The call to "consecrate a fast" and "call a solemn assembly" (קַדְּשׁוּ־צוֹם קִרְאוּ עֲצָרָה) in verse 14 refers to an official, ritual national day of mourning. Such gatherings involved the entire people coming together at the sanctuary to fast, mourn, and cry out to God. The instruction for the priests to "spend the night in sackcloth" (verse 13) reflects an intense mourning ritual where they sought God's grace through humility. Sackcloth (שַׂק) was a coarse, dark fabric made of goat hair that symbolized grief and repentance.

The interruption of the grain offering (מִנְחָה) and drink offering (נֶסֶךְ) in verses 9 and 13 was a serious crisis for temple service. These daily offerings of flour, oil, and wine were fundamental to the continuous worship of YHWH, and their cessation signaled that the connection between God and His people was threatened.

The detailed description of agricultural devastation—with grapevines, fig trees, pomegranates, palm trees, and apple trees (verse 12)—reflects the varied agriculture of the Judean highlands and underscores the total scope of the crisis.
