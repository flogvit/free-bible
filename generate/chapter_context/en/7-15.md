# Historical and Cultural Context for Judges 15

## Events and Customs

**The Wheat Harvest** (v. 1) took place in May–June in ancient Israel and was a critical economic period. Samson visiting his wife with a young goat as a gift reflects a common custom in antiquity where men brought gifts when visiting. The type of marriage described here, where the wife remains in her father's house and the husband visits, is known from other Semitic cultures and is often called a "visiting marriage." Her father interpreted Samson's absence as rejection and therefore gave his daughter to another—a action motivated economically to preserve the family's honor and secure the daughter's future.

**The Burning of the Crops** (vv. 4–5) was an extremely serious act in an agricultural culture where one year's harvest could mean the difference between survival and famine. The text mentions both grain shocks (harvested grain), standing grain, and olive groves—meaning both that year's crop and perennial trees that took generations to establish. Such destruction was a known act of war in antiquity and would have economically devastated an entire community for several years.

## Places

**The Rock of Etam** (vv. 8, 11) was a natural fortress in the hill country between Judah and the Philistine coastal plain. The area has many caves and inaccessible rock formations that made it ideal as a refuge. A village named Etam is mentioned in other texts (1 Chron 4:3; 2 Chron 11:6), but the exact location of the rock formation is uncertain.

**Lehi** (vv. 9, 14, 17, 19) means "jaw" or "jawbone" in Hebrew. The place name "Lehi" may have existed before the event, or the name may have been given as a memorial to the incident with the jawbone. The text gives two explanations of the name: "Ramat-Lehi" (the height of the jaw) and "En-Hakkore" (the spring of the caller). Such double name explanations are common in biblical narratives and mark places' significance for later generations.

## Language and Expressions

The Hebrew word **שׁוּעָלִים** (*shu'alim*, v. 4) is traditionally translated as "foxes," but can also mean jackals. Jackals live in larger packs than foxes and would have been easier to catch in large numbers. The number 300 may indicate a large-scale operation or be a round number for "many."

The expression **"put hand on the thigh"** (שׁוֹק עַל־יָרֵךְ, v. 8) is an idiom meaning complete destruction or a crushing defeat. It may originally refer to a wrestling technique or method of slaughter.

**Maktesh** (מַכְתֵּשׁ, v. 19), translated "depression" or "mortar," may refer to a natural hollow in the rock. That God "split open" this place to provide water has parallels with wilderness journey narratives where God gives water from the rock.
