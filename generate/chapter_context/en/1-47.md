# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 47

## Places

**Goshen** (גֹּשֶׁן) – In this chapter, Goshen is confirmed as the dwelling place of Jacob’s family (verses 1, 4, 6, 27). Pharaoh offers them "the best of the land" in this region. Goshen was located in the eastern Nile Delta, ideal for livestock grazing and somewhat separate from the Egyptian heartland.

**The Land of Rameses** (אֶרֶץ רַעְמְסֵס) is mentioned in verse 11 as the area where Jacob’s family received property (אֲחֻזָּה). This name likely reflects a later place designation from the time when the text reached its final form, since the city of Rameses was built during the reign of Ramesses II (c. 1279–1213 BC). The area is identical with Goshen.

## Events and Customs

**Jacob’s Blessing of Pharaoh** (verses 7, 10) is remarkable from a cultural perspective. That a foreign nomad would bless the world’s most powerful ruler twice would normally have been unthinkable in Egyptian protocol. Hebrews 7:7 expresses the principle that "the lesser is blessed by the greater," which in this context suggests that Jacob here acts in a spiritual role surpassing Pharaoh’s earthly power.

**The Tax System of One-Fifth** (verses 24–26) established that Egyptians were to give twenty percent of their harvest to Pharaoh. Compared with other tax systems of the time, this was actually moderate—many feudal systems required up to half. Joseph’s economic policy in this chapter—collecting money, then livestock, and finally land (verses 14–20)—represents a systematic centralization of resources.

**The Special Status of the Priests** (verses 22, 26) reflects the privileged position of the Egyptian priestly class. Pharaoh provided the priests with fixed rations (חֹק), so they did not need to sell their land. This is the only group exempted from Joseph’s purchase of all the land.

## Key Terms

**"Years of My Sojourn"** (שְׁנֵי מְגוּרַי) – In verse 9, Jacob uses the root ג-ו-ר (gur, "to dwell as a foreigner"), which gives the noun מְגוּרִים (megurim), meaning "temporary residence" or "sojourn," not permanent settlement. He states his age as 130 years and describes his days as "few and evil" compared with those of his fathers.

**"Put Your Hand Under My Thigh"** (שִׂים־נָא יָדְךָ תַּחַת יְרֵכִי) in verse 29 was a solemn oath-taking custom in the patriarchal period. The gesture was connected to offspring and the future of the family line, emphasizing the seriousness of the oath. The same ritual is used by Abraham in Genesis 24:2–3.

**"Bowed at the Head of the Bed"** (וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל עַל־רֹאשׁ הַמִּטָּה) in verse 31 shows Jacob’s worship or gratitude. The Septuagint translates this differently ("at the top of his staff"), which Hebrews 11:21 follows, based on an alternative reading of the Hebrew consonants.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Egyptian administrative texts document the practice of allowing Semitic nomads to settle in the Delta region during drought periods. A well-known example is Papyrus Anastasi VI (c. 13th century BC), which describes how Shasu Bedouins from Edom were permitted to pass through border fortresses to sustain themselves and their flocks at water pools in the area of Pithom.

The chapter covers a significant period: from the family’s arrival in Egypt, through the hardest years of famine with Joseph’s economic reforms, to Jacob’s final 17 years in Egypt (verse 28). Jacob’s total lifespan is given as 147 years.
