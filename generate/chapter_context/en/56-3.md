## People

Chapter 3 mentions several coworkers in Paul’s mission team. **Artemas** (Ἀρτεμᾶς) is mentioned only here in the New Testament. The fact that he is named as a possible replacement for Titus suggests he was a trusted coworker. **Tychicus** (Τυχικόν) is known from other letters as a faithful messenger (Eph 6:21; Col 4:7; 2 Tim 4:12). **Zenas** (Ζηνᾶν) is described as "lawyer" (τὸν νομικὸν), which could mean either a Jewish scribe or a Roman jurist. **Apollos** (Ἀπολλῶν) is likely the same person mentioned in Acts 18:24–28 and 1 Cor 1–4. The fact that these two traveled together suggests an established network of Christian coworkers moving between congregations.

## Places

**Nicopolis** ("city of victory") was most likely the city in Epirus on the west coast of Greece, founded by Emperor Augustus in 31 BC to commemorate the victory at Actium. The city was strategically located along the main route between Italy and the East, making it a natural hub for missionary activity. Paul’s plan to "spend the winter" (παραχειμάσαι) there reflects that sea voyages were dangerous and uncommon during winter (November to March), so missionaries planned stays in important cities during this period.

## Events and Customs

The exhortation to submit to "authorities and powers" (ἀρχαῖς ἐξουσίαις) in verse 1 reflects life under Roman provincial administration on Crete. For Christians, who were already suspected of worshipping a crucified rebel, it was crucial to show themselves as law-abiding citizens. The term "πειθαρχεῖν" (to obey) was a technical word for obedience toward civil authorities in Greco-Roman political language.

The call to help Zenas and Apollos "on their journey" (σπουδαίως πρόπεμψον, "send off eagerly") refers to the widespread ancient custom of "propempsis" – equipping travelers with supplies, a traveling party, and possibly financial support for the next destination. This was an important part of Christian hospitality practices that made early missionary work possible.

## Key Terms

**Παλιγγενεσία** (palingenesia, "rebirth/renewal") in verse 5 was a term with broad application in antiquity. The Stoics used it to describe the cosmic renewal of the world, and the term also appeared in Jewish contexts regarding restoration. In imperial cults, it could describe a new age under a new ruler. Paul applies the term to the individual believer who experiences radical spiritual renewal through baptism.

**Φιλανθρωπία** (filanthropia, "love for humanity") in verse 4 was a central virtue in Greco-Roman ethics, especially associated with rulers and benefactors. By attributing this quality to "God our Savior", Paul presents the gospel in culturally recognizable categories while redefining the term theologically.

**Αἱρετικὸν ἄνθρωπον** (hairetikon anthropon, "a divisive person") in verse 10 does not primarily refer to doctrinal heresy in the later sense, but to someone who creates factions and division within the congregation – a serious threat to the vulnerable Cretan congregations.
