# Historical and Cultural Context for Micah 7

## Places

Chapter 7 mentions several geographic areas with significant historical resonance. **Bashan** (v. 14) was the fertile highland region east of the Sea of Galilee, known for its rich pastures and strong oxen (cf. Ps 22:13). **Gilead** was the mountainous region east of the Jordan River, traditionally belonging to the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh. Both areas had been lost to the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III in 732 B.C. (2 Kgs 15:29), so the prophet's promise that the people would pasture there again "as in days of old" was a promise of restoration of lost land.

**Carmel** (v. 14) was the fertile mountain by the Mediterranean coast, known for its lush vegetation. The reference to **Assyria** and **Egypt** (v. 12) marks the two superpowers that defined Micah's geopolitical world, and the prophecy that people will come from there to Jerusalem turns the power dynamics of the times upside down.

## Events and Customs

The agricultural images in verse 1 reflect the custom of gleaning. After the main harvest, the poor had the right to gather what was left (cf. Lev 19:9–10), but the prophet finds "no cluster of grapes to eat" – even the remnants are gone. This was a powerful image of the total moral depletion of society.

The description of family breakdown in verse 6, where a son dishonors his father and a daughter-in-law rises against her mother-in-law, broke with fundamental honor-shame structures in the patriarchal society. The relationship between in-laws was especially regulated, since the young bride lived in her husband's family household. Jesus quotes these verses in Matthew 10:35–36, which shows the text's enduring relevance.

## Key Terms

The word **חָסִיד** (*ḥasid*, v. 2) describes the "faithful" or "loyal" one – someone who lives in **ḥesed**-fellowship with both God and people. That such people had "disappeared from the land" was a radical accusation.

The expression **חֵרֶם** (*ḥerem*, v. 2) is translated as "net" or "snare", but the word also means "devoted to destruction" in the context of holy war. The wordplay suggests that people hunt each other as if they were war booty devoted to destruction.

The rhetorical question **מִי־אֵל כָּמוֹךָ** (*mi-ʼel kamoḵa*, "who is a God like you?", v. 18) is a wordplay on the prophet's own name, **מִיכָה** (*Mîḵāh*), which means precisely "Who is like Yahweh?" The chapter thus concludes by answering the question embedded in the prophet's name.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The expressions "putting a hand over the mouth" (v. 16) and "licking dust like a serpent" (v. 17) reflect well-known submission rituals from the ancient Near East. Assyrian reliefs from Nineveh show defeated kings and peoples in exactly such humiliating postures before the great Assyrian king. Micah turns this image around: One day the powerful nations will show the same submission – not to Assyria, but to the God of Israel.
