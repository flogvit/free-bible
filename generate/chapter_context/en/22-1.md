# Historical and Cultural Context for Song of Solomon 1

## Characters

Chapter 1 introduces the two main characters: the male lover (referred to as "the king" in verses 4, 12) and the female lover. The woman is described as dark-skinned due to her work in the vineyards (verse 6), indicating a lower social status—she is not a court lady who could protect herself from the sun. The "daughters of Jerusalem" are introduced as a chorus or audience to whom the woman addresses her words (verse 5).

## Places

**Cedar** (verse 5) refers to a Bedouin tribe descended from Ishmael (Genesis 25:13), known for their black goat-hair tents. The tribe lived in the Syrian-Arabian desert east of Israel. The comparison between "the tents of Cedar" and "Solomon's curtains" creates a contrast between simple nomadic life and royal splendor.

**En Gedi** (verse 14) is an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, known in ancient times for its aromatic plants, especially henna bushes (cofer). Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of perfume production from the Iron Age. The site was a fertile exception in the otherwise barren landscape surrounding the Dead Sea.

## Key Terms

**Dod** (דּוֹד) – translated as "love" or "beloved" – appears in verses 2, 4, 13, 14, and 16, referring both to the beloved person and the physical love between them.

**Nard** (נִרְדִּי, verse 12) is nardus, an expensive aromatic plant imported from the Himalayas. It was among the most costly perfumes in ancient times and here symbolizes the woman's love and devotion.

**Mor** (מֹר, verse 13) is myrrh, an aromatic resin from trees in South Arabia and East Africa. It was used in perfume, religious rituals, and embalming, and was a valuable trade commodity.

## Events and Customs

The reference to "Pharaoh's mare" (verse 9) reflects the connection between Israel and Egypt. According to 1 Kings 10:28–29, Solomon imported horses and chariots from Egypt. Egyptian horses were highly valued throughout the Middle East, and comparing someone to a magnificent mare among Pharaoh's chariot horses was an expression of supreme beauty and worth.

The use of perfumes and fragrances (verses 3, 12–14) was central to both court life and love rituals in the ancient Near East. Ointments and oils were used for personal care, and a person's "fragrance" could symbolize their reputation and character—hence the wordplay on "name" (shem) and "ointment" (shemen) in verse 3.
