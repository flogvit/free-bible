# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 3

## People

**Samuel as a "boy" (נַעַר, na'ar)**: The term *na'ar* is used of Samuel and can refer to anything from a child to a young servant. In this context, it indicates that Samuel was likely in his early youth and served as a kind of temple assistant under Eli's guidance. He performed practical tasks such as opening the doors of the sanctuary (v. 15). Samuel's role as "the one who serves the LORD" (מְשָׁרֵת, mesharet) places him in a tradition of young men who assisted at the sanctuary, similar to the service of the Levites.

**Eli as spiritual mentor**: Despite failing physical sight, Eli continues to function as a spiritual guide. His ability to recognize that it is God calling Samuel (v. 8) shows that he retained spiritual insight even in the midst of his family's moral decline. His response "The LORD is he" (v. 18) reveals a pious acceptance of judgment upon his own house.

## Places

**Heikal YHWH – The Lord's temple at Shiloh**: The term "heikal" (הֵיכַל) is used here for the sanctuary, a word that usually denotes a palace or temple. This is the tabernacle at Shiloh, which by this time had developed into a more permanent structure. That Samuel slept "where the ark of God was" (v. 3) indicates he had access to the innermost area—an unusual position that underscores his special role.

**The expression "from Dan to Beersheba"** (v. 20) describes all Israel's territory from north to south, a distance of about 240 kilometers. This standard expression for the nation's full extent shows that Samuel's reputation as a prophet was recognized throughout the land.

## Events and Customs

**God's lamp (נֵר אֱלֹהִים)**: The time reference "before God's lamp went out" (v. 3) likely refers to the seven-branched lampstand (menorah) in the sanctuary, which burned through the night and was relit each morning. This places God's call to Samuel in the early morning hours, just before dawn—a time that in ancient Near Eastern thought was often associated with divine revelations.

**Prophetic calling and revelation**: The text emphasizes that "the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision" (v. 1). The Hebrew expression "אֵין חָזוֹן נִפְרָץ" (*ein chazon nifrats*) literally means visions did not "break forth" or were not common. This marks a time of spiritual crisis in Israel, and Samuel's calling represents a renewal of prophetic communication.

## Key Terms

**"Hinneni" (הִנֵּנִי) – "Here I am"**: This answer, which Samuel gives repeatedly (vv. 4, 5, 6, 8, 16), is the classic Israelite expression of readiness and availability. The same word is used by Abraham (Gen 22:1), Moses (Exod 3:4), and Isaiah (Isa 6:8) when they respond to God's call. The term expresses complete attention and willingness to serve.

**"Ears that tingle" (תְּצִלֶּינָה... אָזְנָיו)**: The expression in verse 11 that "both ears of the one who hears it will tingle" is an idiomatic description of shock and horror at news so serious that it causes a physical reaction. The same expression is used concerning the fall of Jerusalem (2 Kgs 21:12; Jer 19:3).

**Atonement through sacrifice (v. 14)**: God's oath that the sin of Eli's house "will never be atoned for by sacrifice or grain offering" breaks with normal sacrificial theology, where sin could be covered through proper offerings. This underscores the seriousness of the sin and that some transgressions exceed what the sacrificial system could handle.
