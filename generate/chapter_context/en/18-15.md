# Historical and Cultural Context for Job 15

## Characters

Eliphaz the Temanite speaks here in his second speech to Job. As the oldest and most respected of the three friends, he now takes the lead in this second round of dialogues. In this chapter, Eliphaz appeals to traditional wisdom and the authority of the elders (verses 10, 18), reflecting the ancient Near East's deep respect for ancestral teaching. Teman, his homeland, was known as a center of wisdom in antiquity, and Eliphaz's argumentative style—invoking revelations and traditions—is characteristic of this environment.

## Key Terms

**"The first man" (רִאישׁוֹן אָדָם, verse 7)**: Eliphaz's rhetorical question about whether Job was born before Adam or created before the hills draws from an ancient Near Eastern notion that those who existed at the beginning of creation had special access to divine wisdom. A similar idea appears in Proverbs 8:25, where wisdom existed before the hills.

**"God's council" (סוֹד אֱלוֹהַ, verse 8)**: The Hebrew word *sod* means a confidential assembly or secret counsel. In ancient Israel, prophets were considered to have access to God's heavenly council (cf. Jeremiah 23:18). Eliphaz accuses Job of believing he has such privileged access.

**"East wind" (קָדִים, verse 2)**: The east wind from the desert was known for its destructive and drying power in the Middle East. Eliphaz uses this image to describe Job's words as empty and devastating.

## Events and Customs

Eliphaz's appeal to the tradition of wise fathers (verses 17–19) reflects the important role oral transmission played in the ancient Near East. The expression that the land was given "to them alone" and that "no foreigner went among them" may point to an idealized past of cultural purity before outside influences entered. This is typical of wisdom traditions that valued unbroken inheritance from ancestors as more reliable than new insight.

The images of the wicked person's fate in verses 20–35—terrible sounds in the ears, destruction in the midst of peace, wandering for bread, darkness waiting—form a catalog of curses that Eliphaz implicitly applies to Job. These images have parallels in ancient Mesopotamian and Egyptian wisdom texts. The notion that the wicked "covers his face with fat" (verse 27) describes arrogant wealth, while the dwellings turning into ruin mounds (verse 28) reflect archaeologically known phenomena of abandoned cities in the ancient Middle East.
