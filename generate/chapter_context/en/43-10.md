# Historical and Cultural Context for John 10

## Places

**Solomon’s Colonnade (στοᾷ τοῦ Σολομῶνος, v. 23)** was a covered colonnade along the eastern side of the temple court. It stood atop a massive retaining wall that still partially stands today. The colonnade provided shelter from winter cold and rain, which the text emphasizes by noting that it was winter (χειμὼν ἦν). Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of Herodian column bases in this area. This location served as a natural gathering place for religious teaching and debate, and is also used in Acts 3:11 and 5:12 as a meeting place for the early church.

**The region across the Jordan (v. 40)** refers to Perea, where John the Baptist had been active. Bethany beyond the Jordan (not to be confused with Bethany near Jerusalem) was likely located near Wadi el-Kharrar on the east side of the Jordan. This place marks a return to the beginning of Jesus’ public ministry, where John first testified about him (John 1:28).

## Events and Customs

**The Feast of Dedication (τὰ ἐγκαίνια, v. 22)**, known in Hebrew as Hanukkah, celebrated the rededication of the temple in 164 BC following the Maccabean victory over Antiochus IV Epiphanes. The festival lasted eight days and fell in the month of Kislev (November/December). It involved lighting lamps, songs of praise (especially the Hallel psalms), and celebration of Jewish religious freedom. Although not commanded in the Law of Moses, it had become an important observance marking God’s faithfulness. That Jesus was in the temple during this festival celebrating cleansing and rededication provides meaningful background for his statement about his own consecration (ἡγίασεν, v. 36).

**Sheepkeeping in Judea** followed a pattern where several flocks were often gathered into a common enclosure (αὐλὴν τῶν προβάτων, v. 1) at night, guarded by a gatekeeper (θυρωρός, v. 3). In the morning, each shepherd would come and call his own sheep by name—the sheep recognized the shepherd’s voice and followed him out to pasture. The hired hand (μισθωτός, vv. 12–13) was a familiar figure who was paid for his work but lacked the personal bond with the flock that the owner had. Wolves were a real threat in the Judean highlands.

## Key Terms

**"I am" (ἐγώ εἰμι)** appears repeatedly in this chapter: "I am the door" (vv. 7, 9) and "I am the good shepherd" (vv. 11, 14). This formulation resonates with God’s self-revelation in Exodus 3:14 (LXX: ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ὤν). The statement "I and the Father are one" (ἐγὼ καὶ ὁ πατὴρ ἕν ἐσμεν, v. 30) provoked an accusation of blasphemy, showing that his listeners understood it as a claim to divinity.

**"The good shepherd" (ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός)** was an image with deep resonance in the Old Testament. God himself is described as Israel’s shepherd (Psalm 23; Isaiah 40:11), and Ezekiel 34 criticizes Israel’s bad shepherds (leaders) and promises that God himself will care for his flock through his servant David. The adjective καλός means not only "good" in a moral sense, but "noble", "perfect", or "ideal"—the shepherd who fully fulfills the role of shepherd.

**To "lay down one’s life" (τὴν ψυχὴν τίθημι, vv. 11, 15, 17, 18)** is an unusual formulation that emphasizes the voluntary nature of Jesus’ death. The more common expression would have been to "give" (διδόναι) one’s life. The verb τίθημι ("lay down") suggests a deliberate act in which something is set aside, with the possibility of taking it up again (vv. 17–18).

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Inscriptions from Herodium and other places confirm the temple’s practice of public announcements and warnings, including stone tablets forbidding Gentiles from entering the inner courts under threat of death. This illustrates the tense atmosphere surrounding the temple where the confrontation in verses 22–39 took place. Josephus’ descriptions of the temple court (Jewish Antiquities 15.396–401; The Jewish War 5.184–226) confirm details about Solomon’s Colonnade as a place for gathering and teaching, especially during festival periods when large crowds were in Jerusalem.
