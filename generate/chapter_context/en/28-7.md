# Historical and Cultural Context for Hosea 7

## Places

**Samaria** (שֹׁמְרוֹן, v. 1) was the capital of the Northern Kingdom and is mentioned here as the center of corruption and evil. The city was known for its strategic location on a hill and its wealth, but also for its moral decay under the last kings. **Ephraim** is used consistently throughout the chapter (vv. 1, 8, 11) as a poetic designation for the entire Northern Kingdom, named after the dominant tribe.

**Egypt and Assyria** (vv. 11, 16) represent the two great powers between which the Northern Kingdom wavered in its foreign policy during the 730s BC. Under King Hoshea (732–722 BC), Israel first sought support from Egypt against Assyria, which ultimately led to the fall of Samaria in 722 BC. The reference that their fate would become "a mockery in the land of Egypt" (v. 16) suggests that Egypt's promised help never arrived.

## Events and Customs

**The Regicides** (vv. 3–7) refer to the political instability during the last decade of the Northern Kingdom. Between 752 and 732 BC, four kings were murdered by their successors: Zechariah (by Shallum), Shallum (by Menahem), Pekahiah (by Pekah), and Pekah (by Hoshea). Hosea describes these conspirators as an oven that burns through the night—they plot in secret while "the baker sleeps," only to strike at dawn. "The king's day" (v. 5) may refer to celebrations where princes drank themselves drunk, giving conspirators the opportunity to act.

**Pagan Lamentation Rituals** (v. 14)—the expression "they cut themselves" (יִתְגּוֹרָרוּ) suggests Canaanite rituals in which worshippers slashed themselves to evoke divine attention, a practice forbidden in the Mosaic Law (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1). That they do this "for grain and wine" shows that they turned to Baal for agricultural blessings.

## Key Terms

**"A cake not turned over"** (עֻגָה בְּלִי הֲפוּכָה, v. 8) is a striking image from daily life. Flatbread was baked on hot stones and had to be turned to cook through. A cake that is not turned becomes burned on one side and raw on the other—useless. Such was Ephraim: partially assimilated into pagan culture, partially believing in YHWH, but actually neither one nor the other.

**"A silly dove without sense"** (כְּיוֹנָה פוֹתָה אֵין לֵב, v. 11) describes Israel's naive foreign policy. The dove was known for being easily lured into traps. The expression "without heart" (אֵין לֵב) in Hebrew thought means "without understanding" or "without judgment," since the heart was regarded as the seat of reason, not emotions.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian annals from Tiglath-Pileser III (745–727 BC) confirm the political situation Hosea describes. The annals mention that King Hoshea of Israel first paid tribute to Assyria but later conspired with "So, king of Egypt" (2 Kgs 17:4). This double game—seeking help from both Egypt and Assyria—is exactly what the prophet criticizes as naive and self-destructive policy (v. 11).
