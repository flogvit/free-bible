## People

Nebuchadnezzar II is the main character in this chapter, where he himself serves as the narrator through much of the text (verses 1–15 and 31–34). The chapter presents the king in a unique situation—as one who personally experiences divine judgment and restoration. His proud statement in verse 27 about Babylon as "the great city that I have built" reflects his self-image as a mighty builder.

Daniel (Belteshazzar) appears as the dream interpreter and advisor (verses 5–6, 16–24). The king acknowledges that "a spirit of the holy gods" is in him and that no secret is too difficult for him to reveal.

## Places

Babylon is explicitly mentioned in verses 26–27, where Nebuchadnezzar walks on the roof of his royal palace (היכל מלכותא) and admires the city he has built. This sets the stage for his downfall—judgment comes right in the midst of his pride.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes a condition in which Nebuchadnezzar lives like an animal, eating grass like oxen, and letting his hair and nails grow wildly (verse 30). The period of "seven times" (שבעה עדנין, verses 13, 20, 22, 29) is commonly understood to mean seven years. This is a divine judgment that lasts until the king acknowledges that "the Most High rules over the kingdoms of men" (verses 22, 29).

Daniel urges the king to repent: "break off your sins by doing what is right, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed" (verse 24). The judgment is delayed for twelve months (verse 26) before it comes to pass.

## Key Terms

The expression "a watcher, a holy one" (עִיר וְקַדִּישׁ, verses 10, 20; plural עִירִין in verse 14) is an important concept in this chapter. The Aramaic word "ir" (עיר) literally means "awake" and refers to angelic beings who keep watch. This term does not appear elsewhere in the Old Testament but is found in later Jewish literature such as 1 Enoch.

The expression "the Most High rules over the kingdoms of men" (שַׁלִּיט עִלָּאָה בְּמַלְכוּת אֲנָשָׁא) is repeated several times (verses 14, 22, 29) and forms the theological heart of the chapter. This emphasizes God's sovereignty over all earthly kings. The chapter concludes with Nebuchadnezzar's praise of "the King of heaven" (מֶלֶךְ שְׁמַיָּא, verse 34).
