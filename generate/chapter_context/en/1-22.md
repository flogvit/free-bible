# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 22

## Characters

**Abraham** faces his greatest test of faith in this chapter. His response, "Hinne-ni" (vv. 1, 11), and immediate obedience (v. 3) demonstrate his mature faith after decades of walking with God.

**Isaac** appears strong enough to carry the wood for the sacrifice up the mountain (v. 6). The text does not specify his age, and Jewish tradition has varied in its estimates from a young boy to an adult man. His question about the sacrificial lamb (v. 7) and his apparent willing submission at the binding have been the subject of extensive theological reflection in both Jewish and Christian traditions.

**Rebekah** is introduced in the genealogy (v. 23) as the daughter of Bethuel, Abraham's nephew. This prepares the narrative for chapter 24, where she becomes Isaac's wife. The genealogy links the line of promise back to Abraham's family in Mesopotamia and shows that God had already prepared Isaac's future spouse.

**Nahor and Milcah** represent the branch of Abraham's family that remained in Mesopotamia (cf. 11:27–32). Milcah bore eight sons (v. 23), and his concubine Reumah bore four (v. 24)—twelve in total, a pattern paralleling other genealogies in Genesis, including Ishmael's twelve sons (25:13–16) and Jacob's twelve sons.

## Places

**The land of Moriah** (הַמֹּרִיָּה, *ha-Moriyyah*) is the only place in the Pentateuch where this name appears. The journey took three days from Beersheba (v. 4), corresponding to approximately 75–80 kilometers to the Jerusalem area. Later Jewish tradition (2 Chron. 3:1) identifies Moriah with the mountain in Jerusalem where Solomon built the temple. This connection gave the site enormous theological significance in Jewish tradition, viewing Abraham's sacrifice as a precursor to the temple's sacrificial service. The Samaritan tradition, however, identified the place with Mount Gerizim.

**Beersheba** is mentioned as Abraham's dwelling both before and after the event (v. 19).

## Events and Customs

**Child sacrifice** was a known practice in the ancient Near East, especially among the Canaanites and Phoenicians. The narrative in Genesis 22 marks a radical break with this practice: Israel's God *stops* the sacrifice and provides a substitute, fundamentally distinguishing Yahweh worship from surrounding religions.

**The burnt offering** (עֹלָה, *olah*) was the most complete form of sacrifice, where the entire animal was burned on the altar. The root of the word means "to go up"—the smoke ascended to God. That Abraham prepared this sacrifice shows that he fully understood what God requested.

## Key Terms

**"Hinne-ni"** (הִנֵּנִי, "Here I am") is used three times in the chapter: as an answer to God (v. 1), to Isaac (v. 7), and to the angel (v. 11). The expression signals complete availability and obedience. It implies more than physical presence—it expresses readiness to act.

**"Your only son"** (יְחִידְךָ, *yechidkha*) emphasizes Isaac's irreplaceable position. Although Abraham had Ishmael, Isaac was the son of promise. The word *yachid* denotes something unique and irreplaceable.

**"Akedah"** (עֲקֵדָה) comes from the verb עָקַד (*aqad*, "to bind") in verse 9, and became in Jewish tradition the technical term for the entire narrative—the "Binding of Isaac." This is the only place in the Bible where this verb is used regarding binding a human being.

**"JHVH Jir'eh"** (יְהוָה יִרְאֶה, "The Lord will see/provide") in verse 14 plays on the Hebrew verb *ra'ah*, which means both "to see" and "to provide." The place name connects God's foresight and care with His presence.
