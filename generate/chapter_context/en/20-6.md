## Events and Customs

### Guarantees and Debt Guarantors (verses 1-5)
The chapter opens with a warning against acting as a guarantor (Hebrew: עָרַב, *'arav*) for another person. In ancient Israel, it was common for someone to formally guarantee another's debt through a handshake (תָּקַע כַּף, *taqa' kaf* – "to strike hands"). This was a legally binding act that obligated the guarantor to pay if the debtor defaulted. The practice was widespread throughout the ancient Near East, and economic texts from Mesopotamia show similar arrangements. The warning reflects the serious consequences this could bring—a guarantor could lose all their possessions and in the worst case become a debt slave.

### Adultery and the Legal System (verses 29-35)
The passage on adultery reflects ancient Israelite society's view of marriage as a legal and economic contract. Adultery was not only a moral transgression but also a violation of property rights and family honor. The text's comparison with theft (verses 30-31) is legally interesting: a thief who stole out of necessity could pay restitution (sevenfold according to this text), but an adulterer had no such way out. The offended husband's "jealousy" (קִנְאָה, *qin'ah*) had legal backing—he had the right to take revenge, and no compensation could satisfy him.

## Key Terms

### Belial (בְּלִיַּעַל, verse 12)
The expression אָדָם בְּלִיַּעַל (*'adam beliyya'al*) denotes a "worthless" or "useless" person. The word likely comes from *beli* (without) and *ya'al* (use/value). In this text, it describes a type of person who actively works against social order and uses their body to communicate evil.

### Body Language as Communication (verse 13)
The description of the wicked man's body language—winking with his eyes (קֹרֵץ בְּעֵינָו), scraping with his feet (מֹלֵל בְּרַגְלָו), and pointing with his fingers (מֹרֶה בְּאֶצְבְּעֹתָיו)—refers to secret communication used to plot deceit.

### The Seven Things the Lord Hates (verses 16-19)
This passage contains a numerical proverb structure ("six... yes, seven") listing things that are abhorrent to the Lord: haughty eyes, a lying tongue, hands that shed innocent blood, a heart that devises wicked plans, feet that rush to do evil, a false witness who spreads lies, and one who sows discord among brothers. This structure (x, yes x+1) is typical of Hebrew wisdom literature and emphasizes the completeness of the list.

### The Ant as Teacher (verses 6-8)
The ant (נְמָלָה, *nemalah*) was well known in the Near East for its diligence in gathering grain into stores. The point of the text is that the ant works without external authority—without "a leader, an overseer or a ruler"—but from inner motivation. This contrasts with the lazy person who needs constant supervision.
