## Places

**The Temple Gate and the Temple in Jerusalem** (verses 2, 10–11, 14): Jeremiah was commanded to stand at the entrance of the Lord’s house and proclaim a message. This location was strategically vital because the temple gate served as a central gathering place, especially during major festivals when people from all over Judah flocked there to worship. The temple in Jerusalem, built under Solomon, had become a symbol of God’s unbreakable presence and protection—an understanding Jeremiah directly challenged.

**Shiloh** (verses 12, 14): Jeremiah refers to Shiloh, the ancient religious center where the tabernacle and the ark of the covenant stood during the period of the judges (Josh 18:1; 1 Sam 1–4). Archaeological excavations at Khirbet Seilun have revealed a significant destruction layer dating to around 1050 B.C., likely from the time of the Philistines following the battle at Aphek (1 Sam 4). The site was never rebuilt as a religious center. Jeremiah’s point was shocking: God’s former dwelling place had been destroyed because of the people’s sin—the same could happen to the temple in Jerusalem.

**Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom** (verses 31–32): The Valley of Hinnom (Hebrew: גֵּיא בֶן־הִנֹּם, Ge-Hinnom) lay south and west of Jerusalem. Topheth (הַתֹּפֶת) was a specific cultic site in this valley where children were sacrificed by fire. The name possibly derives from an Aramaic word meaning "hearth" or "burning place." The site has been confirmed through archaeological investigations, and the valley later became so associated with evil that its name (Gehenna) was used to refer to hell in later Jewish and Christian tradition.

## Events and Customs

**Temple Theology and the Slogan "The Temple of the Lord"** (verse 4): The triple repetition "the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord" reflects a liturgical slogan circulating in Jerusalem. This "Zion theology" was based on the belief that God had chosen Jerusalem and the temple as His eternal dwelling (Ps 132:13–14), and therefore the city was invincible. The people had distorted this faith into a magical guarantee of security, regardless of moral behavior.

**Worship of "the Queen of Heaven"** (verse 18): This verse describes a family-involved cultic practice in which the entire household participated: children gathered wood, fathers kindled the fire, and women kneaded dough to make sacrificial cakes (כַּוָּנִים, kawwanim). "The Queen of Heaven" (מְלֶכֶת הַשָּׁמַיִם) is commonly identified with the Mesopotamian goddess Ishtar (Akkadian) or her West Semitic equivalent Astarte/Ashtoreth. Archaeological finds from Judah, including female pillar figurines and inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud mentioning "Yahweh and his Asherah," confirm the widespread nature of goddess worship during this period.

**Child Sacrifice** (verse 31): The practice of "burning sons and daughters in the fire" was linked to the worship of Molech or Baal and was explicitly forbidden in Israelite law (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). Despite this, the practice was introduced under kings Ahaz and Manasseh (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6). Phoenician and Punic parallels, especially from Carthage, confirm that such sacrifices occurred in the region.

## Key Terms

**"Den of Thieves"** (verse 11): The Hebrew expression מְעָרַת פָּרִצִים (me'arat paritsim) describes the temple as a refuge for criminals. Just as robbers seek shelter in a cave after their crimes, the people used the temple as a "sanctuary" that gave them a false sense of security while they continued in wrongdoing. Jesus combined this expression with Isaiah 56:7 during His cleansing of the temple (Matt 21:13; Mark 11:17; Luke 19:46).

**"Cut off your hair"** (verse 29): The expression גָּזִּי נִזְרֵךְ refers to cutting off the locks of a Nazirite or consecrated hair. This was a symbol of mourning and loss of holiness. Jerusalem is personified here as a woman who must perform this humiliating mourning ritual because God has rejected "the generation of His wrath" (דּוֹר עֶבְרָתוֹ).
