# Historical and Cultural Context for Matthew 26

## People

**Caiaphas** (Καϊάφα) was the high priest in Jerusalem from approximately 18–36 AD, appointed by the Roman prefect Valerius Gratus. He was the son-in-law of Annas, who had also served as high priest. Caiaphas's unusually long tenure suggests he was skilled at balancing Jewish interests with Roman authority. In 1990, an ossuary (bone box) inscribed "Joseph, son of Caiaphas" was discovered in Jerusalem, likely the family tomb of the high priest.

**Simon the Leper** in Bethany is otherwise unknown, but his name suggests he had previously suffered from a skin disease (leprosy). The fact that he could host a meal indicates he had been healed or that his condition was no longer contagious. **Judas Iscariot** (Ἰούδας Ἰσκαριώτης) bears a surname likely meaning "man from Kerijot" (איש קריות, ish Qeriyot), possibly a town in Judea (Josh 15:25), which would make him the only non-Galilean disciple.

**The sons of Zebedee** (τοὺς δύο υἱοὺς Ζεβεδαίου), James and John, along with Peter, formed Jesus's innermost circle. These three were also witnesses to the Transfiguration on the mountain (Matt 17:1) and were taken by Jesus during his prayer struggle in Gethsemane.

## Places

**Bethany** was located on the eastern side of the Mount of Olives, about 3 km from Jerusalem. The village served as Jesus's base during his final weeks, safely outside the immediate control of the city's religious authorities. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of a first-century village at this site.

**Gethsemane** (Γεθσημανί) comes from the Aramaic "gat shemanim," meaning "oil press." The place was located at the foot of the Mount of Olives, east of the Kidron Valley. The area was known for olive groves, and an oil press has been found near the traditional location. Today's "Church of All Nations" is built over the ruins of a Byzantine church that marked the site.

**The high priest's courtyard** (αὐλή) was likely located in the Upper City of Jerusalem, the affluent area south of the temple. Archaeological finds from this region reveal luxurious first-century homes with inner courtyards consistent with the description in the text.

## Events and Customs

**The Passover meal** (τὸ πάσχα) was the most important Jewish festival, commemorating deliverance from Egypt. The meal included unleavened bread, bitter herbs, wine, and the Passover lamb. The expression "the first day of the Feast of Unleavened Bread" (πρώτῃ τῶν ἀζύμων) technically refers to Nisan 15, but in common usage was often applied to Nisan 14, the preparation day when the Passover lamb was slaughtered in the temple. The meal was eaten in the evening while reclining (ἀνακειμένου), a Hellenistic custom adopted by Jews for festive occasions.

**Anointing with costly oil** followed a widespread Middle Eastern hospitality custom. The alabaster jar (ἀλάβαστρον) was typical for storing perfume, made from a white, translucent stone. That the woman broke the jar and poured the oil over Jesus's head carried connotations of royal anointing, but Jesus interpreted it as preparation for burial (ἐνταφιάσαι).

**The thirty silver coins** (τριάκοντα ἀργύρια) corresponded to the price of a slave gored by an ox, according to Exodus 21:32, and allude to the prophecy in Zechariah 11:12–13. The sum was likely Tyrian shekels, the only coinage accepted in the temple due to its silver content.

**The Sanhedrin** (τὸ συνέδριον) was the Jewish council of about 71 members, led by the high priest. It had jurisdiction over religious matters but required Roman approval for carrying out a death sentence. Nighttime trials were unusual and potentially in conflict with later known rabbinic procedural rules.

## Key Terms

**"Blood of the covenant"** (τὸ αἷμά μου τῆς διαθήκης) is a direct reference to the covenant-making at Sinai (Exod 24:8), where Moses sprinkled sacrificial blood on the people. The Greek word διαθήκη can mean both "covenant" and "testament," and both meanings are relevant here.

**"Messiah, the Son of God"** (ὁ χριστὸς ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ) in the high priest's question combines two titles. While "Son of God" in Jewish tradition could refer to Israel's king (Ps 2:7), it was Jesus's connection to "the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" (καθήμενον ἐκ δεξιῶν τῆς δυνάμεως) – a combination of Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13 – that was perceived as blasphemous, since it implied divine status.

**Tearing garments** (διέρρηξεν τὰ ἱμάτια) was a traditional Jewish gesture of mourning, but also a prescribed reaction to blasphemy according to rabbinic sources. That the high priest did this during the trial signaled that he considered the case settled.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Excavations in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter have uncovered several first-century priestly homes with ritual baths (mikvaot) and fine stone vessels. One of the most impressive is the so-called "Burnt House," which shows how the upper class lived. These finds illustrate the environment where Jesus's trial took place, with inner courtyards where servants could gather around a fire (verse 69).

Studies of Galilean dialect confirm that northern Jews had a distinctive pronunciation easily recognized in Jerusalem, explaining how the servant girls identified Peter as a Galilean from his speech (λαλιά).
