# Historical and Cultural Context for Ephesians 3

## People

Paul introduces himself in this chapter with two specific designations: "a prisoner for Christ Jesus" (v. 1) and "the very least of all the saints" (v. 8). The term "δέσμιος" (prisoner) points to his actual imprisonment, likely house arrest in Rome. His self-description as "least" (ἐλαχιστοτέρῳ, an unusual comparative form of a superlative) may reflect his past as a persecutor of the church, but also serves as a rhetorical device to highlight the greatness of grace.

## Key Terms

**Μυστήριον (mystērion – "mystery/hiddensecret")**: This term appears three times in the chapter (vv. 3, 4, 9) and is central to Paul’s argument. In Greco-Roman culture, "mysteries" were secret religious rituals (such as the Eleusinian mysteries) known only to initiates. Paul radically redefines the concept: God’s mystery is now revealed and must be proclaimed publicly. Its content is that Gentiles are "fellow heirs, members of the same body, and partakers of the promise" (v. 6).

**Συγκληρονόμα, σύσσωμα, συμμέτοχα** (v. 6): Paul uses three compound words with the prefix "συν-" (together with), which may be his own coinages. These terms emphasize the complete equality between Jewish Christians and Gentile Christians—they are "fellow heirs", belong to the "same body", and are "joint partakers". In a culture marked by sharp ethnic and religious divisions, this was revolutionary.

**Οἰκονομία (oikonomia – "stewardship/administration")**: The term (vv. 2, 9) comes from "oikos" (house) and "nomos" (law/order) and originally referred to the management of a household. Paul uses it to describe God’s plan of salvation and his own role as steward of that plan. In the Roman world, an "oikonomos" was often a trusted slave with great responsibility.

**Πολυποίκιλος σοφία** (v. 10 – "the manifold wisdom of God"): The adjective "polypoikilos" literally means "many-colored" or "highly varied" and was used to describe embroidered fabrics with many colors and patterns. Ephesus was known for its textile production, which may have made this image especially vivid for the readers.

## Events and Customs

Paul’s posture of prayer, "I bow my knees" (v. 14), was unusual in Jewish tradition, where standing prayer with raised hands was the norm. Kneeling expressed special reverence or intensity. Romans and Greeks also rarely knelt in prayer; it was more associated with submission to a superior power. Paul’s word choice thus signals deep awe and earnest prayer.

The term "προσαγωγή" (v. 12, "access") has its background in court ceremony, where a foreigner needed an introducer to gain access to the king. In Persian and Hellenistic courts, this was a formalized procedure. Paul uses this image to show that believers have direct access to God through Christ, without human intermediaries.
