# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 24

## People

**Abraham’s servant** is referred to as "the oldest servant of his household, who had charge over all that he owned" (v. 2). Although he is not named in this chapter, Jewish tradition has identified him with Eliezer of Damascus (Gen 15:2). The title "the oldest of the house" (זְקַן בֵּיתוֹ) indicates a trusted household steward with considerable authority—a role well attested in Mesopotamian and Egyptian sources from the second millennium B.C.

**Rebekah** (רִבְקָה) is introduced as the daughter of Bethuel and sister to Laban. Her lineage is closely tied to Abraham’s brother Nahor through her father Bethuel (vv. 15, 24). Her name may mean "the one who binds" or "the one who captures" (from the root ר-ב-ק used for binding or tethering animals). She is described as "very beautiful in appearance, a virgin whom no man had known" (v. 16)—phrasing that underscores her suitability as a bride.

## Places

**Aram-Naharaim** (אֲרַם נַהֲרַיִם, v. 10), literally "Aram between the two rivers," refers to the region in upper Mesopotamia between the Euphrates and the Khabur River (or Tigris). This corresponds to the Akkadian Naharaim and Egyptian Naharina. "Nahor’s city" (עִיר נָחוֹר) is believed to be the Haran area, where archaeological findings confirm Semitic settlement during the patriarchal period.

**Beer-lahai-roi** (בְּאֵר לַחַי רֹאִי, v. 62), "the well of the Living One who sees me," in the Negev region was the place where Isaac lived. This is the same location where Hagar met the angel of the Lord (Gen 16:14), giving the site theological significance as a place of divine presence.

## Events and Customs

**Oath under the thigh** (vv. 2, 9): The custom of placing the hand under the thigh (תַּחַת יְרֵכִי) when taking an oath was a solemn practice in ancient Israel. The thigh (יָרֵךְ) was closely associated with the reproductive organs and thus with descendants. Some scholars suggest that the oath was thereby taken at the most sacred point—the promise of future generations. Other texts show similar symbolism where "thigh" represents lineage (Gen 46:26).

**Bride-price and gifts** (vv. 22, 53): The servant gave Rebekah a gold nose-ring (נֶזֶם) weighing half a shekel (בֶּקַע) and two gold bracelets weighing ten shekels. Later, he gave silver and gold articles, clothing, and valuables to her family. This aligns with the מֹהַר tradition—the bride-price that legitimized the marriage. Mari texts and Nuzi documents from the second millennium B.C. confirm similar practices where gifts to the bride’s family were legally binding.

**Drawing water at the well** (vv. 11, 13–20): The well was the central social gathering place in ancient villages. The text states that the servant arrived "in the evening, at the time when women usually go out to draw water" (לְעֵת עֶרֶב לְעֵת צֵאת הַשֹּׁאֲבֹת, v. 11). Rebekah’s hospitality in providing water not only for the servant but also for his ten camels—requiring considerable effort (a camel can drink up to 100 liters)—demonstrated her character. This well scene establishes a narrative pattern (type-scene) that is repeated with Jacob and Rachel (Gen 29) and Moses and Zipporah (Exod 2).

**The woman’s consent** (vv. 57–58): It was remarkable that the family asked Rebekah directly, "Will you go with this man?" Although marriage arrangements were primarily a family matter, Nuzi texts indicate that women’s consent was required in certain cases, especially when the bride was to leave her homeland.

## Key Terms

**Chesed ve’emet** (חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת) – "steadfast love and faithfulness" or "grace and loyalty" – is a word pair expressing God’s reliable covenantal faithfulness. The servant uses this expression to describe how God has led him (v. 27 with suffix: חַסְדּוֹ וַאֲמִתּוֹ; v. 49 in base form). This word pair often appears in connection with God’s covenant relationship with His people.

**Beqa** (בֶּקַע, v. 22) was a unit of weight equivalent to half a shekel, approximately 5–6 grams. Archaeological finds of weights marked with this word confirm the use of this weighing system in ancient Israel.

**Tsa’if** (צָעִיף, v. 65) – the veil that Rebekah put on when she saw Isaac – was a garment that could signal a woman’s status as a bride or mark her transition to a new role. Veiling in connection with marriage is known from several ancient cultures in the region.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Mari archives (ca. 1800–1750 B.C.) from upper Mesopotamia contain marriage contracts and correspondence that illuminate several aspects of this chapter: long-distance marriage arrangements, bride-prices, negotiations with family members, and gift exchanges. These texts show that the practices described in Genesis 24 were well established in the region to which Abraham sent his servant.

The Nuzi texts (ca. 1500–1350 B.C.) document a practice where a brother could play a prominent role in negotiating his sister’s marriage—possibly explaining Laban’s prominent role in the negotiations (vv. 29–31, 50, 55), even though her father Bethuel was still alive.
