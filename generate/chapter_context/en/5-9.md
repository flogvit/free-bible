# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 9

## People

**Anakites (בְּנֵי עֲנָקִים)** are mentioned in verse 2 as a feared people of unusually large stature. The name likely comes from the Hebrew word for "neck" or "necklace," possibly referring to their height. The verse suggests that rumors about them were well known: "you have heard it said yourself: Who can stand against the sons of Anak?" The Anakites lived in the mountainous regions around Hebron in southern Canaan. Biblical texts link them to Kiriath-Arba (later Hebron), and they are considered descendants of Arba, a great man among the Anakites.

**Aaron (אַהֲרֹן)** is highlighted in verse 20 as an object of the Lord's intense anger because of his role in the golden calf incident. As high priest and Moses' brother, he had special responsibility, yet failed by leading the people into idolatry. Moses' intercession for Aaron emphasizes that even the highest leaders depended on God's grace and the mediator's role.

## Places

**Horeb (חֹרֵב)** (verse 8) is the same mountain as Sinai, where the covenant was made. The name Horeb is used consistently in Deuteronomy and likely means "desert land" or "dry land." The mountain is traditionally located on the Sinai Peninsula, although its exact location remains debated among scholars.

**Taberah, Massah, and Kibroth-hattaavah** (verse 22) were stopping places during the wilderness journey named after Israel's rebellions. Taberah means "burning" (Numbers 11:1-3), Massah means "testing" (Exodus 17:1-7), and Kibroth-hattaavah means "graves of craving" (Numbers 11:31-34). These place names serve as monuments to the people's repeated disobedience.

**Kadesh-barnea (קָדֵשׁ בַּרְנֵעַ)** (verse 23) was an oasis in the Negev desert where the Israelites stayed for a long time, and from which the spies were sent into the land. It was here that the people refused to enter the promised land after the spies' negative report, leading to thirty-eight additional years of wandering in the wilderness.

## Events and Customs

**The Golden Calf (עֵגֶל מַסֵּכָה)** described in verses 12-21 was a cast idol that represented a breach of the covenant almost immediately after it was made. Calf worship was widespread in Egypt and Canaan, where the ox symbolized fertility and strength. Egyptian gods such as Apis and Hathor were depicted as or with oxen. Moses' destruction of the calf—burning, grinding, and scattering it into the stream flowing down from the mountain (verse 21)—was a ritual annihilation demonstrating the powerlessness of the idol.

**The forty days and nights** without food or water (verses 9, 18, 25) was a period of intense fasting and intercession. The number forty has symbolic meaning in the Bible and marks periods of testing, preparation, or transition. Moses' fast represented total dependence on God and wholehearted dedication to the task.

## Key Terms

**"Stiff-necked people" (עַם־קְשֵׁה־עֹרֶף)** in verses 6 and 13 is an image drawn from agriculture: a stiff-necked ox refuses to be led by the yoke and goes its own way. The expression describes Israel's repeated disobedience and resistance to God's guidance. This was a well-known term for the Israelites as an agricultural society.

**"God's finger" (אֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים)** in verse 10 emphasizes that the stone tablets were written directly by God Himself, not by humans. This expression highlights the divine origin and authority of the law. The same expression is used regarding God's miraculous intervention in Egypt (Exodus 8:19).

**Tsedakah (צְדָקָה)** and **yosher (יֹשֶׁר)** in verses 4-6, translated as "righteousness" and "uprightness of heart," are rejected as the basis for the promise of the land. Moses stresses that the conquest was not earned but resulted from the Canaanites' wickedness and God's promise to the fathers. This served as an important corrective against spiritual self-righteousness.
