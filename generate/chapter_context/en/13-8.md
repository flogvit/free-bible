# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 8

## People

The chapter provides an extended genealogy for the tribe of Benjamin, with special emphasis on the lineage of King Saul (vv. 33–40). This genealogy is repeated almost word-for-word in 1 Chron 9:35–44. The name "Eshbaal" (אֶשְׁבָּעַל, v. 33) is notable—it is the original form of the name that was changed to "Ish-boshet" in the books of Samuel (2 Sam 2:8), where "baal" was replaced with "boshet" (shame) to avoid associations with the Canaanite god. Similarly, "Merib-baal" (מְרִיב בָּעַל, v. 34) is used instead of "Mephibosheth." Here, Chronicles preserves these older, authentic name forms. Ulam's descendants are described as "mighty warriors, archers" (v. 40), reflecting the well-known reputation of the tribe of Benjamin as skilled archers (Judg 20:16; 1 Chron 12:2).

## Places

The chapter mentions several geographical locations. **Geba** (גֶּבַע, v. 6) and **Gibeon** (גִּבְעוֹן, v. 29) were both Benjaminite towns north of Jerusalem. **Manahath** (מָנָחַת, v. 6) was likely a town in the area of Judah (cf. 1 Chron 2:54) to which some Benjaminites migrated or were relocated. **The fields of Moab** (שְׂדֵה מוֹאָב, v. 8) indicate that the Benjaminite Shaharaim had children while staying in Moab—evidence of contact between Israelites and Moabites. **Ono and Lod** (v. 12) were towns in the coastal plain that Benjaminites built or rebuilt. **Aijalon** (v. 13) was located in the Shephelah region, and the text notes that Benjaminites drove out the inhabitants of Gath—a reference to conflict with the Philistines.

## Events and Customs

The mention that Shaharaim "sent away" (מִן־שִׁלְחוֹ אֹתָם) his wives Hushim and Baara (v. 8) suggests divorce, which was permitted under Israelite law (Deut 24:1–4). That he then had children with a new wife named Hodesh (חֹדֶשׁ, "new [moon]") in Moab shows both the practice of polygamy and marriage across ethnic boundaries. The expression "heads of fathers' houses" (רָאשֵׁי אָבוֹת), repeated throughout the chapter (vv. 6, 10, 13, 28), refers to the patriarchal clan system in which the eldest male in each family line held legal and religious authority.

## Key Terms

The expression "mighty warriors" (גִּבֹּרֵי חַיִל, v. 40) denotes not only military skill but also social status as landowning, armed men with full citizenship rights. "Archers" (דֹּרְכֵי קֶשֶׁת, literally "those who tread the bow") describes the technique of using the foot to draw large composite bows—a hallmark of Benjaminite warriors throughout Israel's history.
