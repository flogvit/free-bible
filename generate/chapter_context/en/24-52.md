## People

**Zedekiah** (צִדְקִיָּהוּ) was the last king of Judah. According to verse 1, he was twenty-one years old when he became king; his mother was Hamutal (חֲמוּטַל) from Libnah, and he reigned for eleven years in Jerusalem. He "did what was evil in the Lord’s sight" (v. 2) and rebelled against Babylon.

**Nebuchadnezzar** (נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר), king of Babylon, led the siege of Jerusalem. At Riblah he judged Zedekiah, executed his sons and the leading men of Judah, and blinded the king.

**Nebuzaradan** (נְבוּזַרְאֲדָן) held the title "chief of the guard" (רַב־טַבָּחִים). He oversaw the practical execution of Jerusalem’s destruction, the plundering of the temple, and the deportations.

**Seraiah** (שְׂרָיָה) was the high priest (כֹּהֵן הָרֹאשׁ), and **Zephaniah** (צְפַנְיָה) was the second priest (כֹּהֵן הַמִּשְׁנֶה). Both were taken to Riblah and executed along with other leaders (vv. 24–27).

**Jehoiachin** (יְהוֹיָכִין), the former king who had been deported in 597 BC, is mentioned at the end of the chapter. In the thirty-seventh year of his exile, he was elevated by **Evil-Merodach** (אֱוִיל מְרֹדַךְ), the new king of Babylon (vv. 31–34).

## Places

**Riblah** (רִבְלָתָה) in the land of Hamath was Nebuchadnezzar’s military headquarters. Here Zedekiah was judged, his sons were executed, and he himself was blinded (vv. 9–11). The priests and other leaders were also executed here (vv. 26–27).

**The gate between the two walls** (שַׁעַר בֵּין־הַחֹמֹתַיִם) near the king’s garden (v. 7) was the escape route used by Zedekiah and his men. They fled toward the Arabah plain (הָעֲרָבָה), but were overtaken in the plains of Jericho.

**Babylon** (בָּבֶל) is mentioned as the destination for the deported (v. 15), for the temple articles (v. 17), and as the place where Jehoiachin was held and later elevated (vv. 31–34).

## Events and Customs

The siege of Jerusalem began in Zedekiah’s ninth year, on the tenth day of the tenth month (v. 4), and lasted until the eleventh year, in the fourth month (vv. 5–6). The Babylonians built siege ramps (דָּיֵק) around the city. The famine became so severe that "there was no bread for the people in the land" (v. 6).

The blinding of Zedekiah after he had first been forced to watch his sons being executed (vv. 10–11) was a deliberate strategy: the last thing he saw was the destruction of his dynastic line. He was then put in bronze chains and taken to Babylon.

The temple’s destruction occurred in the fifth month, on the tenth day, in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth year (vv. 12–13). The house of the Lord, the king’s house, and all the great houses were burned, and Jerusalem’s walls were torn down.

## Key Terms

**"Those who see the king’s face"** (רֹאֵי פְנֵי־הַמֶּלֶךְ) in verse 25 was a technical term for high-ranking court officials with direct access to the king.

**"The house of custody"** (בֵּית־הַפְּקֻדֹּת, v. 11)—literally "the house of oversight"—was the place where Zedekiah was held until his death.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The deportation figures** in verses 28–30 are unique to this chapter and provide precise numbers: 3,023 Judeans in the seventh year, 832 from Jerusalem in the eighteenth year, and 745 in the twenty-third year—totaling 4,600 people. These figures are lower than those in 2 Kings, possibly because only adult men were counted.

**Ration texts from Babylon** (circa 592 BC) mention rations for "Yaukin, king of the land of Judah" and his sons. This confirms the chapter’s concluding account of Jehoiachin’s stay in Babylon and his later elevation.

**The temple inventory** is described in detail in verses 17–23, including the two bronze pillars (eighteen cubits high), the bronze sea, and the twelve bronze oxen. King Solomon had made these, and the weight of the bronze was beyond measure.
