# Historical and Cultural Context for Leviticus 18

## Events and Customs

The chapter contains detailed laws concerning sexual relations and stands in sharp contrast to practices in Egypt and Canaan (v. 3). The text reflects a time when Israel had to define its identity as distinct from surrounding cultures. The laws regarding incest (vv. 6–18) were stricter than in many neighboring societies. In Egypt, sibling marriage was known among royal families, and in Mesopotamia certain forms of kinship marriage were accepted. The biblical prohibitions cover both blood relatives and relatives by marriage, demonstrating a comprehensive understanding of family bonds.

The prohibition against "giving one's offspring to Molech" (v. 21) refers to child sacrifice. The expression לְהַעֲבִיר לַמֹּלֶךְ (*leha'avir lamolekh*, "to cause to pass through for Molech") is debated, but it likely refers to sacrifice by fire, a practice documented in Phoenician and Canaanite cultures. This prohibition is notably placed among sexual laws, which may indicate a connection between fertility cults and these practices.

## Key Terms

The expression **"uncover someone's nakedness"** (Hebrew: גַּלּוֹת עֶרְוָה, *galot ervah*) is a euphemism for sexual intercourse and appears repeatedly throughout the chapter. The term עֶרְוָה (*ervah*) literally means "nakedness" or "shame" and is consistently used to describe forbidden sexual relations.

The word **תּוֹעֵבָה** (*to'evah*), often translated as "abomination" or "detestable thing" (vv. 22, 26–30), is a central term describing actions fundamentally incompatible with Israel's covenant relationship with God. The term is used in the Old Testament for both religious and ethical transgressions, particularly those linked to Canaanite religion and cultic practices.

The expression that the land "vomits out" (קָאָה, *qa'ah*) its inhabitants (vv. 25, 28) is a powerful metaphor personifying the land itself as a moral agent. This reflects the idea that the land is holy and cannot tolerate persistent impurity.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Archaeological excavations at the tophet sanctuary in Carthage (a Phoenician colony) have uncovered thousands of urns containing burned remains of children, along with stelae dedicated to Baal Hammon and Tanit. Similar findings have been made at other Phoenician sites. This confirms that child sacrifice was a real practice in cultures related to the Canaanites and provides historical background for the prohibition against Molech offerings in verse 21.

Hittite laws (ca. 1650–1180 BCE) contain parallel lists of forbidden sexual relations, but with significant differences regarding which relationships are prohibited and what penalties are imposed. The comparison shows that such regulations were common in the ancient Near East, yet the biblical laws have their own distinct profile and theological basis tied to Israel's call to holiness.
