# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Samuel 11

## People

**Nahash, the Ammonite king**, was the ruler over the Ammonites, a people who lived east of the Jordan. The name נָחָשׁ (Nahasj) means "snake" in Hebrew. According to biblical tradition, the Ammonites were descendants of Lot’s youngest daughter (Gen 19:38) and were often in conflict with Israel over the territories in Transjordan. Nahash appears in the text as a brutal warrior-king who used mutilation as psychological warfare and humiliation of his enemies.

## Places

**Jabesh Gilead** (יָבֵשׁ גִּלְעָד) was an Israelite city east of the Jordan, in the region belonging to the tribe of Manasseh. The city lay in a fertile area near the valley of the Jabbok River. Jabesh had a special connection with the tribe of Benjamin (cf. Judg 21), which may explain why Saul from Benjamin responded so strongly to their cry for help.

**Bezek** (בָּזֶק) was the muster point for the Israelite army, located in the central highlands west of the Jordan. The site was strategically positioned to gather troops from the Israelite tribes before the campaign against the Ammonites.

**Gilgal** (הַגִּלְגָּל) was an important cultic site near Jericho, associated with Israel’s entry into the land (Josh 4–5). The place served as a religious and political gathering point, and it was here that Saul’s kingship was "renewed" and publicly confirmed.

## Events and Customs

**Gouging out the right eye** (v. 2) was a known practice in ancient warfare. The right eye was especially important for warriors because the shield was carried on the left arm, so the left eye was already partially blocked in battle. Losing the right eye made a man militarily useless. This was therefore both an act of humiliation and a way to neutralize future military resistance.

**Cutting up the oxen** (v. 7) was a powerful symbolic action that functioned as a mobilizing ritual threat. By sending pieces of the oxen to all parts of Israel, Saul signaled that the same fate would befall the livestock of anyone who did not come out to fight. Similar practices are known from other ancient cultures, where animals were butchered as part of covenant rituals and oaths (cf. Gen 15:9–17; Jer 34:18–19). The action also recalls the dreadful event in Judges 19:29, where a Levite sent his dead concubine’s body around Israel.

## Key Terms

The expression **"the Spirit of the Lord came upon Saul"** (וַתִּצְלַח רוּחַ־אֱלֹהִים, v. 6) uses the verb צָלַח, which means to "rush into" or "come powerfully upon". This is the same expression used for the charismatic leaders of the period of the judges (cf. Judg 11:29 regarding Jephthah; Judg 14:6, 19 regarding Samson). It marks divine empowerment for a specific task and legitimizes Saul’s leadership as God-given.

**"Renewal of the kingship"** (נְחַדֵּשׁ הַמְּלוּכָה, v. 14) does not indicate a new anointing, but rather a public confirmation and popular recognition of Saul’s royal status following his successful military leadership. This creates continuity from the private anointing (chap. 10) to broad national acceptance.
