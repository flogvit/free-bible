# Historical and Cultural Context for Genesis 15

## People

**Eliezer of Damascus** is mentioned as Abram’s steward (בֶן־מֶשֶׁק בֵּיתִי, "son of my house" or "he who manages my house") and potential heir (vv. 2–3). The name means "God is help." The practice of appointing a trusted servant as an heir when one was childless is well documented in texts from the ancient Near East. The Nuzi texts from the 15th–14th centuries BC describe how childless couples could adopt a servant or relative as an heir, with the obligation to care for them in old age. This heir would then be replaced if a biological child was later born—exactly as God assures Abram in verse 4.

## Places

**Ur of the Chaldeans** (אוּר כַּשְׂדִּים, v. 7) was a major Sumerian city-state in southern Mesopotamia, near the modern city of Nasiriyah in Iraq. Archaeological excavations have revealed a highly developed civilization with impressive architecture, including a large ziggurat dedicated to the moon god Nanna (Akkadian: Sin). The city was a center for moon worship. By identifying Himself as the one who "brought you out from Ur," God establishes a parallel to the later Exodus formula and emphasizes that Abram’s journey was a divine deliverance from idolatry.

**The Boundaries of the Land** are defined from נְהַר מִצְרַיִם ("the River of Egypt") to נְהַר־פְּרָת ("the Euphrates, the great river") (v. 18), representing the maximum extent of the promised land. "The River of Egypt" likely refers to Wadi el-Arish on the Sinai Peninsula, not the Nile. These boundaries reflect known political and geographical markers from the second millennium BC.

## Events and Customs

**The Covenant-Ratification Ritual** (vv. 9–17) represents a well-known ancient Near Eastern ceremony for establishing a binding agreement. The animals were cut in two, and the parties walked between the pieces. This action was symbolic: "May it happen to me as with these animals if I break the covenant." Parallels are found in Hittite, Assyrian, and Aramaic treaties, as well as in Jeremiah 34:18–19. The striking feature of this chapter is that only God, represented by תַנּוּר עָשָׁן ("a smoking firepot") and לַפִּיד אֵשׁ ("a flaming torch"), passes between the pieces. Abram does not actively participate—this is a unilateral, unconditional covenant in which God alone assumes the obligation.

**The Birds of Prey That Attack** (v. 11)—the Hebrew עַיִט (*ayit*)—may symbolically represent threats to the covenant or hostile nations that would attempt to prevent the fulfillment of the promises. Abram’s driving them away shows his role as protector until God’s promises are fulfilled.

**The Prophecy About the Sojourn in Egypt** (vv. 13–16) predicts that Abram’s descendants will be strangers in a land not their own, become slaves, and be oppressed for 400 years. God promises to judge the oppressors and bring the people out with great wealth. The reference to "the fourth generation" (v. 16) and that "the sin of the Amorites has not yet reached its full measure" provides both a timeframe and a theological rationale for the conquest of Canaan.

## Key Terms

**Machazeh** (מַחֲזֶה, "vision") in verse 1 is a rare term otherwise used primarily in prophetic literature (Num 24:4, 16; Ezek 13:7). This is the first time in the Bible that God reveals Himself through a formal vision, marking the revelation as a formal prophetic experience.

**Tsedaqah** (צְדָקָה, "righteousness") in verse 6 is theologically central. The verb וְהֶאֱמִן ("and he believed") is in the Hiphil form and expresses active, enduring trust. That Abram’s faith "was counted to him as righteousness" does not mean that faith is a meritorious work, but rather that God recognized Abram’s trusting response as the right relationship to the covenant promises. The verb "counted" (חָשַׁב) is otherwise used in accounting and evaluation contexts. This verse is quoted in the New Testament (Rom 4:3; Gal 3:6; Jas 2:23).

**Tardemah** (תַּרְדֵּמָה, "deep sleep") in verse 12 is the same word that describes Adam’s sleep in Genesis 2:21. It denotes a God-given, unnatural state of sleep in which God acts while the human remains passive—a literary device emphasizing that the covenant is solely God’s work.

## Historical Parallels

The Mari texts (c. 1800s BC) and the Alalakh texts (c. 1700–1400 BC) contain several covenant documents using the expression "to cut/make a covenant" (Hebrew: כָּרַת בְּרִית, *karat berit*). This expression, used in verse 18, refers directly to the ritual cutting of the animals. The texts confirm that this was standard terminology throughout the Fertile Crescent for describing the establishment of binding agreements, showing that Genesis 15 reflects authentic practices from the second millennium BC.
