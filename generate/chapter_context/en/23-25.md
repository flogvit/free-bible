# Historical and Cultural Context for Isaiah 25

## Places

**"This mountain"** (בָּהָר הַזֶּה) refers to the Mount of Zion in Jerusalem, mentioned repeatedly throughout the chapter (vv. 6, 7, 10). In Old Testament tradition, Zion was not merely a geographical location but the theological center of God’s presence among His people. The idea that nations would stream to the mountain of Zion to meet the God of Israel was a central element of prophetic eschatology.

**Moab** (v. 10) was a neighboring people east of the Dead Sea, often in conflict with Israel. According to tradition, the Moabites descended from Lot (Gen 19:37). **Madmenah** (מַדְמֵנָה) is mentioned in v. 10 in connection with Moab’s humiliation. The word may be a place name, but it also means "dung heap" or "muck pile"—something that intensifies the degrading image of Moab’s shame.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes a **grand feast** (מִשְׁתֵּה, v. 6) that the Lord will prepare for all nations. In the ancient Near East, such meals featuring "rich foods" and "aged wine" expressed royal hospitality and covenant-making. The meal symbolized peace, fellowship, and abundance. That God Himself hosts such a feast on Zion represents a radical vision of universal salvation.

**Covering with a veil or shroud** (v. 7) carried several meanings in the culture: grief, shame, or blindness. That God removes this covering from all peoples symbolizes the removal of sorrow and ignorance of the true God.

## Key Terms

The expression **"He will swallow up death forever"** (בִּלַּע הַמָּוֶת לָנֶצַח, v. 8) is one of the most radical formulations in the Old Testament. The verb בלע ("swallow") turns the Canaanite mythological motif on its head—where death (personified as the god Mot) swallowed people, here it is God who swallows up death itself. This verse is alluded to in 1 Cor 15:54.

**"Wipe away tears"** (מָחָה דִּמְעָה) is an intimate, almost parental image of God’s care, also found in Rev 21:4.

## Chapter-Specific Parallels

The image of a strong city reduced to ruins (v. 2) and a feast on the holy mountain have parallels in Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (ca. 1400–1200 BCE), which describe divine meals and mythical conflicts between gods. Isaiah likely uses familiar mythological motifs but radically transforms them in light of Israel’s monotheistic faith—it is YHWH alone who conquers death and gathers the peoples.
