# Historical and Cultural Context for Romans 2

## Events and Customs

The chapter addresses circumcision (περιτομή, *peritomē*), which was the central covenant sign of Jewish identity. In the first century, circumcision was a hotly debated topic throughout the Roman Empire. Under Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167 B.C.), circumcision was forbidden under penalty of death, making the ritual a symbol of Jewish resistance and faithfulness. By Paul’s time, some Jews had begun undergoing a painful procedure called *epispasmos* to conceal their circumcision in Greco-Roman bathhouses, while others wore it proudly as a mark of election.

The expression "circumcision of the heart" (v. 29) was not new but drawn from Jewish tradition (Deut 10:16; 30:6; Jer 4:4). Paul applies this prophetic concept to argue that external ritual without inner transformation is insufficient.

## Key Terms

The term *προσωπολημψία* (*prosōpolēmpsia*, v. 11) – "to regard the person" – is a Semitic idiom meaning partiality or favoritism based on outward status. In Jewish thought, God was known as one who shows no partiality (Deut 10:17), in contrast to human judges who could be swayed by wealth or social position. Paul uses this established theological principle to challenge the idea that Jewish ethnicity alone grants special standing before God.

The word *συνείδησις* (*syneidēsis*, v. 15), often translated "conscience", was an important concept in Greco-Roman philosophy, especially in Stoic thought. The Stoics believed all people possessed an inner capacity for moral self-assessment. Paul employs this philosophical term to show that even Gentiles without the Mosaic Law have an inner moral awareness that testifies to the law’s requirements.

## Chapter-Specific Insights

Paul’s accusation of temple robbery (*ἱεροσυλεῖς*, *hierosyleis*, v. 22) can be illuminated by historical sources. Josephus reports that Jewish law forbade mocking other peoples’ gods and taking from pagan temples (Against Apion 2.237). Nevertheless, several ancient sources document that some Jews were accused of precisely this. An inscription from Delphi and writings by Philo confirm that temple robbery was a serious charge in that era, and that Jews were sometimes associated with such practices – an association that provoked offense among Gentiles and damaged the Jewish reputation.
