## Events and Customs

Psalm 115 has clear liturgical features that suggest use in temple worship. The structure with alternating voices—between the congregation, priests, and possibly a cantor—points to antiphonal (call-and-response) practice. The threefold exhortation to "Israel," "the house of Aaron," and "those who fear the Lord" (vv. 9–11) reflects a structural division addressing the whole people, the priesthood, and devout followers of the YHWH cult separately. The blessing formula in verses 14–15 parallels the Aaronic blessing and may have been spoken by priests over the assembly.

## Key Terms

The word **עֲצַבִּים** (*atsabbim*, "idols") in verse 4 literally means "painful things" or "formed work," carrying a negative connotation that emphasizes the emptiness of idols. The polemic against idol images (vv. 4–8) parallels other Old Testament texts such as Isaiah 44:9–20, where gods are criticized through their physical representations—they have sense organs that do not function. The expression **דּוּמָה** (*dumah*, "silence/quietness") in verse 17 refers to the realm of the dead as a place without praise, underscoring the importance of worshipping God while one lives.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The critique of idols in verses 4–8 can be understood as a polemical response to well-known Mesopotamian and Egyptian rituals such as the "mouth-opening ritual" (mīs pî / wpt-r), in which divine statues were ritually "awakened to life." Israelite polemic turns this on its head: regardless of rituals, the images remain dead. Archaeological finds of gold and silver divine statues from throughout the Middle East confirm the psalm's description of "silver and gold, the work of human hands." This type of criticism was especially relevant during the exile and early post-exilic period, when Israelites lived surrounded by foreign cult practices.
