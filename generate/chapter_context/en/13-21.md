# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Chronicles 21

## People

**Satan (שָׂטָן)**: This is one of the few places in the Old Testament where שָׂטָן (satan) appears without the definite article, which may indicate a proper name rather than a functional description ("the adversary"). In the parallel account in 2 Samuel 24:1, the action is attributed to the Lord himself, who in his anger "incited David" to count Israel. The presentation in Chronicles reflects a theological development in the understanding of evil and God's sovereignty following the exile.

**Gad (גָּד)**: David's seer and prophet who served as the mediator of God's word to the king. Seers had an established role at the court in ancient Israel and the Middle East, where they gave counsel based on divine revelation. Gad is also mentioned in 1 Samuel 22:5 and was active throughout much of David's reign.

**Ornan/Araunah (אָרְנָן)**: A Jebusite who owned the threshing floor where the temple would later be built. The fact that he was a Jebusite—one of the original peoples of Canaan—underscores that the site held significance even before Israelite times. His offer included oxen for burnt offerings, the threshing sled for wood, and the wheat for grain offerings (verse 23). David's insistence on paying the full price follows a known pattern from ancient Near Eastern property transfers.

## Places

**Ornan's Threshing Floor**: This site is traditionally identified with Mount Moriah, where Abraham was to sacrifice Isaac (Genesis 22). Threshing floors were typically located on high ground where the wind could separate grain from chaff, and such places were often used as gathering sites and cultic locations. The place later became the temple mount in Jerusalem. Traditionally, this is identified with the area beneath today's Dome of the Rock on the Temple Mount.

**Gibeon (גִבְעוֹן)**: Mentioned in verse 29 as the place where the Lord's tabernacle, which Moses had made in the wilderness, and the altar for burnt offerings were located. Gibeon (modern el-Jib, about 10 km northwest of Jerusalem) was an important cultic site during this period. Archaeological excavations have revealed a large water reservoir and remains from the Bronze Age and Iron Age.

## Events and Customs

**Census as Sin**: In ancient Israel, taking a census was problematic because the people belonged to God, and only he had the right to count them. A census for military purposes could express reliance on human strength rather than God's protection. In surrounding cultures, censuses were also linked to taxation and military service, which made them unpopular.

**Choice Among Three Punishments**: The pattern of three alternative punishments—three years of famine, three months of war, or three days of pestilence—is found in other Old Testament texts and covenant curses. The structure of the triad with decreasing time periods is theologically significant. In Mesopotamian treaties and vassal agreements, such punishments were standard consequences for breaking covenants. David's choice to fall into God's hand rather than man's reflects deep theological insight into God's mercy.

**Purchase of the Threshing Floor**: David's insistence on paying the full price (600 gold shekels according to Chronicles) follows the principle that offerings must cost something. The negotiation pattern recalls Abraham's purchase of the cave of Machpelah (Genesis 23) and reflects ancient Near Eastern property transfer practices involving witnesses and a formal price.

## Key Terms

**Fire from Heaven (בָאֵשׁ מִן־הַשָּׁמַיִם)**: God's response with fire from heaven to David's burnt offering and peace offerings (verse 26) confirmed that the place was divinely chosen. This motif is repeated at the temple dedication under Solomon (2 Chronicles 7:1) and in Elijah's confrontation on Mount Carmel. Fire from heaven was a sign of divine acceptance and presence in several ancient Near Eastern cultures.

**Shekels of Gold (שִׁקְלֵי זָהָב)**: The price of 600 gold shekels in Chronicles differs from the 50 silver shekels in 2 Samuel 24:24. The higher amount in Chronicles may include the entire area for the future temple complex, while Samuel may mention only the price for the threshing floor alone. A gold shekel weighed about 11–12 grams.

**David's Fear of the Angel's Sword**: The chapter concludes with David being unable to go to Gibeon to seek God because he was "terrified by the sword of the Lord's angel" (verse 30). This explains why David established worship at Ornan's threshing floor instead of at the tabernacle in Gibeon, and points forward to the building of the temple on this site.
