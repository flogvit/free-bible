# Historical and Cultural Context for 1 Kings 14

## People

**Abijah, Jeroboam’s son** is the sick boy who sets in motion the events of the first part of the chapter (verses 1–18). His name means "The Lord is my father," which is ironic given Jeroboam’s idolatry. The prophet Ahijah declares that something good was found in Abijah toward the Lord (verse 13), and that he alone among Jeroboam’s household would receive a dignified burial.

**Jeroboam’s wife** remains unnamed but plays a central role in the chapter’s narrative. It is she, at Jeroboam’s command, who disguises herself and travels to Shiloh to ask the prophet Ahijah about her son’s fate. Her obedience to her husband’s instructions, and her encounter with the blind prophet who nevertheless recognizes her, drive the story forward.

**Ahijah the prophet** from Shiloh was the same prophet who earlier had foretold that Jeroboam would become king over the ten tribes (1 Kings 11:29–39). His blindness due to old age (verse 4) underscores that his prophetic ability came from God, not natural sight. The fact that Jeroboam sent his wife specifically to this prophet shows that he still acknowledged Ahijah’s authority as the Lord’s spokesperson, despite his own idolatry.

**Nadab, Jeroboam’s son** is mentioned briefly in verse 20 as the one who became king over Israel after Jeroboam’s death. Jeroboam reigned for twenty-two years, and Nadab succeeded him on the throne.

**Maacah, the Ammonitess** is mentioned twice as Rehoboam’s mother (verses 21, 31). She was one of Solomon’s many foreign wives, and her Ammonite background may have contributed to the religious syncretism that characterized Rehoboam’s reign. The Ammonites worshipped the god Milcom, and Maacah’s influence may have opened the door for foreign cultic practices in Jerusalem.

**Abijam, Rehoboam’s son** is mentioned in verse 31 as the one who became king over Judah after Rehoboam’s death.

**Shishak (Shoshenq I)** was the founder of Egypt’s Twenty-second Dynasty and reigned approximately 943–922 B.C.E. His campaign against Judah is confirmed by his own inscription on a temple wall at Karnak, Egypt, where he lists more than 150 cities he conquered in both Israel and Judah. This is one of the most important extrabiblical confirmations of biblical history from the monarchy period.

## Places

**Shiloh** had been Israel’s religious center during the time of the judges, where the tabernacle stood. The fact that Ahijah still lived there may carry symbolic significance—he represented the old, legitimate Yahwistic worship that Jeroboam had abandoned. Shiloh was located in the territory of the tribe of Ephraim, within the northern kingdom’s borders.

**Tirzah** is mentioned as Jeroboam’s residence city (verse 17). The town became the first capital of the northern kingdom before Omri later moved the capital to Samaria. Archaeological excavations at Tell el-Far’ah (North) have uncovered remains of a significant city from this period, including administrative buildings consistent with a royal residence.

## Events and Customs

**The disguise and gifts** that Jeroboam’s wife took along—ten loaves of bread, cakes, and a jar of honey (verse 3)—were typical offerings brought to a prophet or seer in ancient Israel (cf. 1 Samuel 9:7–8). The fact that she was instructed to disguise herself reveals Jeroboam’s fear of being recognized, possibly because he felt ashamed seeking help from a prophet he had betrayed, or because he feared receiving a negative prophecy.

**"Sacred men" (קָדֵשׁ, qadesh)** in verse 24 refer to cultic prostitution associated with Canaanite fertility rites. Both male and female temple prostitutes were common in surrounding cultures as part of rituals intended to ensure fertility for the land and its people. The fact that such practices now occurred in Judah shows how deeply religious decay had penetrated.

**High places, sacred pillars, and Asherah poles** (verse 23) constituted the three main components of Canaanite high-place worship: offering mounds, stone pillars (representing male deities), and wooden poles (representing the goddess Asherah). That these were erected "on every high hill and under every green tree" follows a consistent pattern in Deuteronomistic literature for describing illegitimate worship.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

**The Shishak inscription at Karnak** is the most important archaeological parallel to this chapter. The relief depicts the Egyptian god Amun leading bound captives from conquered cities. Among the place names are cities in both Judah and Israel, showing that the campaign was more extensive than the biblical account alone suggests. A fragment of a stele bearing Shishak’s name has also been found at Megiddo.

**Chronological synchronism**: Shishak’s campaign in Rehoboam’s fifth year (verse 25) can be dated to approximately 925 B.C.E. based on Egyptian chronology. This provides an important anchor point for dating the Israelite kings and confirms that the division of the kingdom occurred around 930 B.C.E.
