# Historical and Cultural Context for Ezekiel 43

## Places

The chapter focuses on the east gate of the visionary temple and the temple building itself. In verses 1–5, God's glory enters through the east gate and fills the temple, the same direction from which it had departed in Ezekiel 10:19 and 11:23. This eastern direction held special significance in Israelite thought—the sun rises in the east, and God's glory is often described using light metaphors. Ezekiel also refers to the Kebar River (נְהַר־כְּבָר, verse 3), where he received his first visions, and to Jerusalem ("the city that should be destroyed," לְשַׁחֵ֣ת אֶת־הָעִ֔יר), linking this vision to his earlier revelations in chapters 1 and 10.

## Events and Customs

The chapter describes the consecration ritual for the altar (verses 18–27), showing striking parallels to the consecration of the tabernacle in Exodus 29 and Leviticus 8. The seven-day purification process (שִׁבְעַ֣ת יָמִ֗ים יְכַפְּרוּ֙ אֶת־הַמִּזְבֵּ֔חַ, verse 26) with sin offerings, burnt offerings, and the use of salt follows established patterns from Israelite sacrificial practice. The use of salt on the sacrifices (verse 24) reflects ancient covenant symbolism—salt was associated with permanence and enduring agreements (cf. Numbers 18:19, "covenant of salt"). The consecration ritual is literally called to "fill their hands" (וּמִלְא֖וּ יָדָֽיו, verse 26), an expression stemming from the custom of placing sacrificial portions in the priest's hands during ordination.

## Key Terms

**כְּבוֹד יְהוָה** (kevod YHWH, "the glory of the Lord") – This term denotes God's visible, manifested presence. In this chapter, the glory is described with a sound like "the roar of mighty waters" (verse 2, קוֹל֗וֹ כְּקוֹל֙ מַ֣יִם רַבִּ֔ים) and light that causes the earth to shine (וְהָאָ֖רֶץ הֵאִ֥ירָה מִכְּבֹדֽוֹ). It was this glory that filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34–35) and Solomon's temple (1 Kings 8:10–11), and which Ezekiel saw depart from the temple before its destruction.

**הַֽהַרְאֵל/אֲרִיאֵל** (har'el/ari'el, verses 15–16) – This word for the upper part of the altar is debated. It may mean "God's mountain" or "God's lion/fireplace." Isaiah 29:1 uses the same term to refer to Jerusalem. The term suggests the altar's cosmic significance as a meeting point between heaven and earth.

**פִגְרֵי מַלְכֵיהֶם** (verses 7, 9) – The expression "the corpses of their kings" likely refers to tomb monuments or burial cults for deceased kings near the temple. Archaeological findings show that royal tombs were often located near sanctuaries in the ancient Near East, creating ritual impurity.

## People

**The descendants of Zadok** (זֶּ֨רַע צָד֜וֹק, verse 19) are highlighted as the only legitimate priests at the future altar. Zadok was high priest under David and Solomon and remained loyal during Absalom's rebellion (2 Samuel 15:24–29). This restriction implicitly excludes other Levitical lines that had participated in idolatry, a theme Ezekiel develops further in chapter 44.
