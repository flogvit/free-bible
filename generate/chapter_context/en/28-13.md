# Historical and Cultural Context for Hosea 13

## Places

**Ephraim** dominates this chapter as a designation for the northern kingdom of Israel. The name comes from the powerful tribe of Ephraim, which had its heartland in the central highlands. In verse 1, there is reference to a time when "Ephraim spoke" and there was "trembling" (רְתֵת) – people feared and respected him. This describes the tribe's former political and religious authority before Baal worship took over. Ephraim was home to important cultic centers such as Bethel and Shiloh, which made the tribe's apostasy especially serious.

**The wilderness** (v. 5) and "the dry land" point back to Israel's wilderness journey after leaving Egypt. This was the period when God "knew" Israel in their vulnerability and dependence, before they entered fertile Canaan and forgot him (v. 6).

## Events and Customs

The expression "those who sacrifice humans kiss calves" (v. 2) is debated, but it likely refers to the syncretistic cult at the centers of Bethel and Dan, where the golden calves stood. Kissing cult images was a common gesture of worship in the ancient Near East, known from both Canaanite and Mesopotamian religion. Some scholars interpret "sacrifice humans" as a reference to child sacrifice, a practice that occurred in the region (cf. 2 Kings 17:17), while others see it as describing people who offer sacrifices – that is, the act of offering itself.

Verses 10-11 allude to Israel's demand for a king "like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:5, 20). This was fulfilled, but the kings Hosea describes – who were "given in anger" and "taken away in wrath" – reflect the chaotic period of regicide and coups during the last decade of the northern kingdom (ca. 752-722 B.C.), when several kings were killed by their successors.

## Key Terms

**Sheol** (שְׁאוֹל) in verse 14 refers to the realm of the dead in Old Testament thought – not a place of punishment, but the dwelling place of the dead beneath the earth. God's power over Sheol and Death (מָוֶת, "mavet") underscores his sovereignty even over the forces of death. The expressions "your plagues, O Death" (דְּבָרֶיךָ from דֶּבֶר, 'pestilence') and "your destruction, O Sheol" (קָטָבְךָ) personify death as an enemy whom God can defeat.

**The east wind/Kadim** (קָדִים) in verse 15 is the east wind or desert wind that blows from the Arabian Desert. This dry, scorching wind was feared for its destructive power over crops and water sources. Here it is used as a symbol of the Assyrian invasion that would come from the east and dry up Israel's resources.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

Assyrian annals from Tiglath-Pileser III (reigned 745-727 B.C.) and Shalmaneser V (reigned 727-722 B.C.) document the systematic plundering of conquered territories, as described in verse 15: "He shall plunder the treasury, all that is precious." Assyrian kings boasted of carrying away "countless treasures" from defeated peoples. The reliefs from Nimrud show Assyrian soldiers carrying off valuables from captured cities – a visual parallel to Hosea's prophecy concerning Samaria's coming fall.
