# Historical and Cultural Context for Deuteronomy 2

## People

**Sihon, king of Heshbon**, was an Amorite ruler who controlled the region between the Arnon and Jabbok rivers in Transjordan. Heshbon (modern Tell Hesban) was his capital. The Amorites were a Semitic people who had established several kingdoms in the area. According to Numbers 21:26, Sihon himself had conquered this territory from the Moabites, which explains why God allowed Israel to take this land—it no longer belonged to Moab.

**Esau's descendants (the Edomites)** are described as Israel's "brothers" (vv. 4, 8), a designation reflecting the kinship between Jacob and Esau. They had settled in the Seir mountains and driven out the original Horite inhabitants (v. 12). This terminology of "brotherhood" carried diplomatic implications and obligated Israel to seek peaceful passage.

**Lot's descendants**—the Moabites and Ammonites—are mentioned as recipients of God's land promises parallel to Esau's descendants (vv. 9, 19). God had given Ar to the Moabites and the land of Ammon to the Ammonites as an inheritance, and Israel was forbidden from attacking these territories.

## Places

**The Seir mountains** formed the core territory of the Edomites, located in the mountainous region southeast of the Dead Sea, in present-day southern Jordan. Archaeological investigations show that the area had permanent settlement from the late Bronze Age, with fortified villages along major trade routes.

**Elat and Ezion-Geber** (v. 8) were port cities on the Gulf of Aqaba, important for trade with South Arabia and East Africa. Ezion-Geber is identified with Tell el-Kheleifeh, where archaeologists have found remains of copper working and storage buildings from the Iron Age.

**The Zered Brook** (vv. 13–14) marked a significant turning point in Israel's journey. Crossing this brook ended the 38 years of wandering from Kadesh-barnea and symbolized the conclusion of judgment upon the generation of rebels.

**Ar** (vv. 9, 18, 29) was the main territory of the Moabites, given by God to Lot's descendants as an inheritance. Israel passed through this area while traveling north toward Amorite territory.

**The Arnon River** (the Wadi Mujib today) formed the boundary between Moab and Amorite territory (v. 24). The river flows through a dramatic gorge, over 500 meters deep in some places, down to the Dead Sea. Crossing it marked entry into Sihon's kingdom.

**The wilderness of Kedemoth** (v. 26) was the place from which Moses sent peaceful messengers to King Sihon with an offer of safe passage.

**Jahaz** (v. 32) was the site of the decisive battle against Sihon. Its location is disputed but is thought to be on the high plateau east of the Dead Sea, possibly at Khirbet el-Medeiyineh.

## Events and Customs

**The 38 years of wandering** (vv. 14–16) are explicitly described as the period from Kadesh-barnea until crossing the Zered Brook. During this time, the entire war-generating generation—the "warriors"—whom God had sworn would not see the promised land died out. The text emphasizes that God's hand was against them to destroy them from the camp.

**Diplomatic passage protocol** is described in verses 26–29, where Moses sent peaceful messengers offering to buy food and water. This followed established diplomatic norms in the ancient Near East. Similar negotiations for passage are documented in letters from Mari and the Amarna archives, where payment for supplies was standard practice.

**Herem warfare** (v. 34)—"we devoted every city to destruction"—refers to ritual destruction in which war spoils were dedicated to God. This concept was not unique to Israel; the Mesha Stele from Moab (ca. 840 BC) uses similar terminology regarding Moabite warfare. The practice signified that victory belonged to the deity, not the army.

**God hardening Sihon's heart** (v. 30) is described as God's means of delivering Sihon into Israel's hands. This motif parallels the account of Pharaoh in Exodus 2.

## Key Terms

**Rephaim, Emim, and Zamzummites** (vv. 10–11, 20–21) were names for prehistoric peoples regarded as giants, "as tall as the Anakites." The Hebrew *refa'im* can mean both "the weak/dead" and be used of mythical giants. The Moabites called them *emim* ("the fearful ones"), while the Ammonites used *zamzummim* (possibly onomatopoeic for mumbling or buzzing). These traditions reflect memory of earlier populations in the region.

**The Horites** (vv. 12, 22) were the original inhabitants of Seir before the Edomites. They were once identified with the Hurrians from northern Mesopotamia, but most scholars now believe they were a distinct local people.

**The Avvites** (v. 23) lived in villages (*chatserim*) as far as Gaza until they were driven out by the Caphtorites. They represent part of the chapter's broader overview of population movements in the region.

**The Caphtorites** (v. 23) came from Caphtor, identified with Crete or the wider Aegean area. This verse links the origin of the Philistines to Crete, which aligns with archaeological finds of Aegean pottery in early Philistine cities.

## Chapter-Specific Findings

The Mesha Stele (the Moabite Stone), discovered in 1868 at Dibon in Jordan, provides a parallel to the ethnographic and geographic information in this chapter. King Mesha of Moab describes his war against Israel and mentions several of the same places (Aroer, the Arnon region). The stone also confirms the use of *herem* terminology in the region and illustrates the complex territorial conflicts between Israel, Moab, and the Amorites described in Deuteronomy 2.
